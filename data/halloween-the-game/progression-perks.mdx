<h2 id="progression-overview">
  Halloween: The Game Progression at a Glance
</h2>

There are several progression systems moving beside each other, and the
confusing part is that a higher number in one does not replace progress in
another.

This is the way I separate them:

- **Profile Level** is the broader account progression track.
- **Killer Level** advances through Michael play.
- **Civilian Level** belongs to the specific Civilian being played.
- **Weapon Level** belongs to the equipable Michael weapon being used.
- **Perk Points** are a separate currency for Civilian perk cards rather than
  another form of XP.
- **Prestige** is the next progression layer after a track reaches its cap.

The practical question is therefore not simply, “How do I level faster?”

It is:

**Which level, currency, or challenge actually controls the thing I am trying
to unlock?**

That distinction becomes especially important once Prestige and the perk
economy start competing for the same Perk Point balance.

<h2 id="prestige">
  What to Do When a Progression Track Reaches Its Cap
</h2>

Once the track I care about reaches its cap, I stop treating the next step as
ordinary leveling.

Prestige becomes a separate decision with its own requirements and challenge
rules. I keep that process outside this progression page because the useful
questions change from **how to gain XP or spend Perk Points** to **when to
start Prestige, what objective is active, and whether the challenge is
tracking correctly**.

For that point onward, use the
<Link href="/halloween-the-game/prestige-guide">
  {" "}Prestige guide
</Link>
for the current level requirements, confirmed objectives and rewards, Perk
Point costs, Private Match farming, and the problems that can prevent an
attempt from registering.

Until I actually reach that point, I keep my attention on the progression
track that controls the next unlock I want.

<h2 id="progression-tracks">
  Profile, Killer, Civilian, and Weapon Progression
</h2>

The progression menu separates XP into four tracks:

- **Profile Level**
- **Killer Level**
- **Civilian Level**
- **Weapon Level**

They can move during the same match, but they do not represent the same thing.

**Profile Level** is the account-wide track. XP earned through Story Mode,
Civilian matches, or Michael play contributes to it.

**Killer Level** comes from playing Michael.

**Civilian Level** belongs to the individual Civilian you select. Progress on
one character does not automatically level the rest of the roster.

**Weapon Level** belongs to the specific equipable Michael weapon being used
against victims.

That means playing Michael can move Profile progress, Killer progress, and the
currently equipped weapon at the same time.

Playing a Civilian can advance Profile progress while also building that
character's own Civilian level.

The mistake is assuming those gains are interchangeable.

If the unlock is attached to a Civilian, more Profile XP does not replace the
Civilian-specific progress.

If the goal is tied to one Michael weapon, swapping to another weapon may keep
broader Profile and Killer progression moving while leaving the original weapon
track behind.

### What progression can unlock

Levels and challenge milestones feed several kinds of rewards, including:

- additional playable Civilians;
- Michael abilities;
- Civilian starting items;
- executions;
- higher challenge tiers;
- cosmetics and customization rewards.

Some only change appearance.

Others change what is available before or during a match.

That difference matters most on a younger account. A useful starting item or
Michael ability can change the next round immediately, while a cosmetic does
not change the options available once the match begins.

Michael and Civilians also build differently.

Michael starts with **Killer Sense** and **Shape Jump** as core traits and has
three selectable ability slots. Ability options shown include **Reality Tear**,
**Blackout**, and **Detection Pulse**.

Weapon choice adds another progression layer because individual equipable
weapons level separately.

Civilians do not use that same ability structure. Their setup combines
character attributes and traits with starting items and perk cards.

<h2 id="perk-points">
  Perk Points, Card Rolls, Upgrades, and Scrapping
</h2>

XP raises progression levels.

**Perk Points are a separate Civilian currency.**

Completing matches supplies Perk Points, and the perk menu lets you spend them
to roll random cards.

The roll screen shown here starts at **100 Perk Points**.

Adding more points increases the chance of receiving a higher-rarity result.

That creates an early-account tradeoff.

Paying more into each roll improves the rarity odds, but it also means making
fewer total rolls with the same balance.

When my collection is still thin, I value **usable options** more than chasing
one expensive high-rarity result.

Once a Civilian already has a dependable loadout, spending more aggressively
for stronger rarity odds becomes easier to justify.

### Judge perk cards by the job they change

Rarity tells you where a card sits inside the perk system.

Its effect tells you whether it actually belongs in the loadout.

For example, **Delicate** appears as an **Uncommon** card on the current roll
screen. At the displayed level, it reduces the transparency of the noise pings
you generate by **30%**.

That is a real stealth benefit.

It still does not make Delicate automatically better for every Civilian.

A character being used for repairs, resident interaction, movement, or
combat support can value completely different effects.

The useful comparison is therefore not just rarity versus rarity.

It is:

**Does this card improve the problem this Civilian is repeatedly being asked to
solve?**

### Equip, upgrade, or scrap owned cards

The perk inventory is not only a collection screen.

Owned cards can be **equipped**, **temporarily upgraded**, or **scrapped for
Perk Points**.

One visible example, **Barricade**, makes locked doors **35% tougher** at the
displayed level.

On that same screen, Barricade shows an upgrade cost of **2,839 Perk Points**
and a scrap value of **904 Perk Points**.

That makes a weak or duplicate roll less final than it first appears.

A card that no longer belongs in any loadout can return some currency to the
account.

I would still avoid scrapping everything below the current favorite rarity.

A young collection needs variety. Two cards with different jobs can be more
useful than several versions of the same idea, even when one has a lower
rarity.

Keep cards while they solve distinct problems.

Recycle them once another option has clearly replaced what they do.

### Perk upgrades are temporary

Upgrading an owned perk does not permanently raise it forever.

The improvement lasts for a set number of matches.

That changes when the upgrade is worth buying.

A temporary upgrade is strongest when several upcoming rounds are already
planned around the same Civilian and the same card.

Spending heavily just before switching characters wastes much of the value.

Perk Points also compete with card rolls, better rarity odds, future upgrades,
and Prestige attempts.

I therefore buy temporary upgrades when I already know the next matches will
use that card, rather than simply because the current balance can afford the
button.

<h2 id="challenge-types">
  Passive, Progressive, and Story Challenges
</h2>

Challenge progress is split into different lanes rather than one master list.

That makes the best approach depend on what kind of challenge is being tracked.

**Passive challenges** accumulate through normal play. Categories shown include
areas such as Killer basics, stalking, executions, violence, and Shape mastery.

These are the challenges I am most willing to leave running in the background.

If normal matches already perform the required actions, distorting every round
to force one percentage bar upward usually is not worth it.

**Progressive challenges** are more specific. They can follow a particular
Civilian, Michael appearance, or weapon through a sequence of objectives.

Those are more reasonable to target deliberately because changing character,
appearance, or weapon can stop advancing the chain you actually care about.

Story Mode has its own optional challenge sets across the Prologue and five
chapters.

<h2 id="civilian-stats">
  Civilian Stats and Starting-Item Unlocks
</h2>

Every Civilian has four attributes:

- **Athleticism**
- **Personality**
- **Resourcefulness**
- **Capability**

The numbers can differ sharply between characters, which means perk cards are
being added on top of an existing strength profile rather than creating one
from nothing.

Rachel Calahan, for example, is shown with:

- **Capability 7**
- **Athleticism 5**
- **Personality 2**
- **Resourcefulness 8**

### Athleticism

Athleticism affects movement **on foot and by bicycle**.

It matters when a role depends on covering distance, moving between houses,
delivering an escape item, or creating space from Michael.

### Personality

Personality affects social and fear-related interactions, including convincing
residents, alerting authorities, commanding additional followers, fear
resistance, and stalk resistance.

A Civilian dealing heavily with residents or police therefore cares about more
than raw movement speed.

### Resourcefulness

Resourcefulness relates to repair expertise, environmental awareness, vehicles,
and firearms competence.

That lines up naturally with jobs built around making an escape route function:
repairing, using vehicles, understanding the environment, and handling
firearms.

### Capability

Capability governs effectiveness with melee weapons and throwable household
items.

Its value is often measured in time rather than kills.

Interrupting Michael can protect a repair, a phone call, an escort, or the last
few seconds of an exit interaction.

The four attributes are useful before any perk card is equipped because they
show where the character already leans.

Perks and starting items then modify that foundation rather than replacing it.

### Starting items remove one unknown from the opening search

Civilian progression can unlock additional starting-item choices.

That matters because the opening of a multiplayer match is usually an
information problem.

You are searching for weapons, healing, phones, escape tools, and the pieces
needed for whichever exit becomes viable.

Starting with something useful removes one of those unknowns before the first
house is searched.

For the broader match flow, the
<Link href="/halloween-the-game/multiplayer-guide">
  {" "}multiplayer guide
</Link>
shows how quickly finding a Repair Kit, Sedan Key, phone, or another escape
tool can change the route of a match.

A starting item does not guarantee that route.

It simply means one piece of the opening inventory problem is already solved.

<h2 id="spending-plan">
  What to Spend and Level First
</h2>

The same Perk Point balance has a different job depending on how developed the
account is.

**Early progression:** build enough cards to create real loadout choices.

At this stage, I prefer multiple usable rolls over pouring too much currency
into rarity while the collection still has obvious holes.

**Established Civilian:** improve the cards that repeatedly help that
character.

Once a Civilian already has a clear role and a dependable set of cards,
targeted rolls and temporary upgrades make more sense.

**Near a progression cap:** stop treating every spare Perk Point as money that
has to be spent immediately.

If Prestige is the next goal, I keep a reserve instead of draining the balance
on another roll or temporary upgrade. The
<Link href="/halloween-the-game/prestige-guide">
  {" "}Prestige guide
</Link>
covers the current Prestige costs and when I would actually start an attempt.

The same principle applies to XP.

Profile progression happens across much of the game, so I do not spend much
time deliberately trying to force that track in isolation.

The more meaningful decision is where to concentrate the progression that is
specific to a character or weapon.

If Civilian multiplayer is the priority, staying with one character for
several matches builds that Civilian's progression while also teaching how
their stats, traits, starting items, and perk cards work together.

Constantly swapping Civilians spreads that character-specific progression
across the roster.

If Michael is the priority, Killer progression and the weapon being used most
often are the tracks I pay attention to.

Changing weapons can still move the broader Profile and Killer systems, but it
does not build the same weapon-specific track.

So I would not automatically level whichever number currently looks lowest.

I would put the next block of play into the progression lane attached to the
**Civilian, Michael loadout, weapon, or Prestige goal I actually expect to use
next**.
