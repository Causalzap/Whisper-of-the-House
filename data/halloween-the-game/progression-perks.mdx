<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
**Separate XP from Perk Points before deciding what to grind or spend.**

Profile, Killer, Civilian, and Weapon progression all track different parts of
play. Perk Points are a separate Civilian currency used for card rolls,
temporary upgrades, and recycling unwanted cards.

Early on, use Perk Points to create a useful collection rather than forcing
high-rarity rolls. Once a Civilian has a dependable loadout, spend more
selectively. When Prestige is close, keep enough points available for another
attempt if the timed challenge fails.

<h2 id="progression-tracks">
  Four XP tracks can be moving at the same time
</h2>

The progression menu separates experience into four categories:

- **Profile Level**
- **Killer Level**
- **Civilian Level**
- **Weapon Level**

**Profile Level** is the account-wide track. XP earned through story mode,
Civilian matches, or Michael play contributes to it.

**Killer Level** comes from playing Michael.

**Civilian Level** belongs to the individual Civilian you select. Progress on
one character does not automatically level the rest of the roster.

**Weapon Level** belongs to the specific equipable Michael weapon being used
against victims.

That means a single match can matter in more than one place.

Playing Michael can advance Profile progress, Killer progress, and the weapon
being used.

Playing a Civilian can advance Profile progress while also building that
character's own Civilian level.

The important part is knowing which number controls the unlock you actually
want. A rising Profile Level does not substitute for Civilian-specific or
weapon-specific progression.

<h2 id="unlocks">
  Progression unlocks more than cosmetics
</h2>

Levels and challenge milestones feed several kinds of rewards:

- additional playable Civilians;
- Michael abilities;
- Civilian starting items;
- executions;
- higher challenge tiers;
- cosmetics and customization rewards.

Some rewards only change appearance.

Others change what is available before or during a match.

That difference matters most while the account is still young. A useful starting
item or Michael ability can alter the next match immediately, while a cosmetic
does not change the decisions available once the round begins.

Michael and Civilians also use different build systems.

Michael starts with **Killer Sense** and **Shape Jump** as core traits, then has
three selectable ability slots. Ability options shown include **Reality Tear**,
**Blackout**, and **Detection Pulse**.

Weapon choice adds another progression layer because individual equipable
weapons level separately.

Civilians do not use that same ability structure. Their setup combines character
stats and traits with starting items and perk cards.

<h2 id="challenge-types">
  Challenges reward three different kinds of play
</h2>

Challenge progress is divided into different lanes rather than one master list.

**Passive challenges** accumulate through normal play. Categories shown include
areas such as Killer basics, stalking, executions, violence, and Shape mastery.

These are easiest to leave running in the background. If normal matches already
perform the required actions, there is little reason to distort every round just
to accelerate one percentage bar.

**Progressive challenges** are more specific. They can follow a particular
Civilian, Michael appearance, or weapon through a sequence of objectives.

Those make more sense to target deliberately because changing character,
appearance, or weapon can stop advancing the chain you currently care about.

Story mode has its own optional challenge sets across the Prologue and five
chapters.

The practical difference is commitment.

Passive progress can follow whatever you already enjoy playing.

A progressive weapon chain rewards staying on that weapon.

A story challenge rewards replaying the chapter whose condition you actually
missed.

<h2 id="perk-points">
  Perk Points run a separate economy from XP
</h2>

XP raises progression levels.

**Perk Points are spent inside the Civilian perk system.**

Completing matches supplies Perk Points, and the perk menu lets you spend them
to roll a random card.

The roll screen shown here starts at **100 Perk Points**.

Adding more points increases the chance of receiving a higher-rarity result.

That creates an early-account choice.

Putting more currency into every roll gives you better rarity odds, but it also
means fewer total attempts.

When the collection is still thin, more usable options often solve more problems
than one expensive card surrounded by empty choices.

Once the collection already supports the Civilian you play, paying for better
rarity odds becomes easier to justify.

<h2 id="perk-cards">
  Judge a perk by the job it changes
</h2>

Rarity tells you how a card sits inside the perk system.

Its effect tells you whether it belongs in the loadout you are building.

For example, **Delicate** appears as an **Uncommon** card on the current roll
screen. At the displayed level, it reduces the transparency of the noise pings
you generate by **30%**.

That is a concrete stealth benefit.

It still does not mean every Civilian needs Delicate more than every other card.

A repair-focused character, resident-focused character, mobile courier, and
combat-support Civilian can all value different effects.

The useful comparison is between the perk and the problem that character handles
most often.

<h2 id="perk-management">
  Equip, upgrade, or scrap cards from the same collection
</h2>

The perk inventory is not only a storage screen.

Owned cards can be:

**equipped**, **temporarily upgraded**, or **scrapped for Perk Points**.

One visible example, **Barricade**, makes locked doors **35% tougher** at the
displayed level.

On that same screen, the card shows an upgrade cost of **2,839 Perk Points** and
a scrap value of **904 Perk Points**.

This makes weak or duplicate rolls less final than they first appear.

A card that no longer has a place in any loadout can return some currency to the
account.

But scrapping everything below the current favorite rarity can leave a new
collection without enough variety to adapt.

Keep cards that solve distinct problems.

Recycle cards once another option has clearly replaced what they do.

<h2 id="temporary-upgrades">
  Card upgrades are temporary
</h2>

Upgrading an owned perk does not permanently raise it forever.

The improvement lasts for a set number of matches.

That changes the value of the button.

A temporary upgrade is strongest when several upcoming rounds will use the same
Civilian and the same card.

Spending heavily just before switching characters wastes much of the reason to
buy the upgrade.

Perk Points also have other uses, so every upgrade competes with:

card rolls,

better rarity odds,

future card upgrades,

and later Prestige attempts.

Use temporary upgrades when the next matches are already planned around the
card, not simply because the currency balance can afford them.

<h2 id="civilian-stats">
  Civilian attributes describe the work a character starts out good at
</h2>

Every Civilian has four attributes:

**Athleticism**

**Personality**

**Resourcefulness**

**Capability**

The numbers can differ sharply between characters.

Rachel Calahan, for example, is shown with:

- **Capability 7**
- **Athleticism 5**
- **Personality 2**
- **Resourcefulness 8**

### Athleticism

Athleticism affects movement **on foot and by bicycle**.

It matters when a role depends on covering distance, moving between houses,
delivering an escape item, or creating space from Michael.

### Personality

Personality affects social and fear-related interactions, including:

convincing residents,

alerting authorities,

commanding additional followers,

fear resistance,

and stalk resistance.

A Civilian handling residents and police pressure therefore cares about more
than raw movement speed.

### Resourcefulness

Resourcefulness relates to:

repair expertise,

environmental awareness,

vehicles,

and firearms competence.

This lines up naturally with jobs built around making an escape route function:
repairing, using vehicles, understanding the environment, and handling firearms.

### Capability

Capability governs effectiveness with melee weapons and throwable household
items.

Its value is usually time.

Interrupting Michael can protect a repair, a phone call, an escort, or the last
few seconds of an exit interaction.

The four attributes are useful before any perk card is equipped because they
show where the character already leans.

Perks and starting items then modify that foundation rather than replacing it.

<h2 id="starting-items">
  Starting items remove one unknown from the opening search
</h2>

Civilian progression can unlock additional starting-item choices.

That matters because the beginning of a multiplayer round is usually an
information problem.

You are searching for weapons, healing, phones, escape tools, and the pieces
needed for whichever route becomes viable.

Starting with something useful removes one of those unknowns before the first
house is searched.

It simply means one part of the opening inventory problem is already solved.

<h2 id="prestige">
  Prestige adds a timed challenge after the level requirement
</h2>

Prestige is not an automatic reset the moment a level gets high enough.

The **Profile Prestige** screen shown here requires **Level 100** before
Prestige 1 becomes available.

After that requirement is met, Prestige asks for a timed challenge.

Only **one Prestige Challenge can be attempted at a time**.

If the challenge fails, another attempt requires more Perk Points.

That is the point where the Perk Point economy changes again.

Far from Prestige, spending the balance on card rolls and useful upgrades can
produce immediate value.

Once the Prestige screen is available, draining the remaining currency on one
speculative roll can leave the account unable to retry the challenge without
earning more points first.

Prestige therefore creates a reason to hold a reserve that barely matters at
lower levels.

<h2 id="spending-plan">
  Let the account stage decide what Perk Points are for
</h2>

The same currency has different value depending on how developed the account is.

**Early progression:** build enough cards to create real loadout choices.

**Established Civilian:** improve the cards that repeatedly help that character.

**Near Prestige:** keep enough currency available for the timed attempt and a
possible retry.

That progression avoids two common wastes.

The first is spending heavily on rarity before there is enough of a collection
to build around.

The second is carrying a huge Prestige reserve dozens of levels before Prestige
can matter.

Currency is most useful when it solves the next system you are actually about
to interact with.

<h2 id="what-to-level-first">
  Put character-specific XP where you intend to keep playing
</h2>

Profile progression happens across the game, so it does not need much special
planning.

The more meaningful choice is where to concentrate the tracks that are
specific.

If Civilian multiplayer is the priority, staying with one character for several
matches builds that Civilian's progression while also teaching how their stats,
traits, starting items, and perks work together.

Constantly swapping characters spreads Civilian progression across the roster.

If Michael is the priority, Killer progression and the weapon being used most
often are the tracks that deserve attention.

Changing weapons still moves the broader Profile and Killer systems, but it
does not build the same weapon-specific track.

The best first target is therefore not whichever number looks lowest.

It is the progression lane attached to the character, weapon, or loadout you
expect to use again next match.
