<h2 id="opening-route">
  Leave the first house with information, not just more loot
</h2>

My first multiplayer match began with just under **20 minutes** showing on the
clock.

The HUD was already asking for several things:

- help Haddonfield residents escape;
- save special targets;
- search for items and weapons;
- use a phone to call the police;
- find a way to escape.

That looks like five jobs.

In practice, the first house only needs to answer one question:

**What can I build from here?**

I enter the closest accessible building and check it for a phone, a resident,
and useful items. If I find an escape-related item, I remember it. If I find a
phone, I decide whether the room is safe enough for a call. If the building has
nothing that gives the match direction, I leave.

The bad opening is not “I found too little.”

It is spending several minutes searching house after house while still carrying
random items with no destination.

A Repair Kit means more once a car has been found.

Bolt Cutters mean more once a chained exit is known.

Sedan Keys become urgent only when the car route is actually close enough to
finish.

Until one of those connections exists, I am collecting possibilities rather
than making progress.

  A quiet phone room is worth using early

A usable phone gives you a clear **Call Police** interaction.

The interaction is not instant.

There is dialogue and an input sequence while your Civilian remains vulnerable,
so I check the room before starting:

Can I leave through another door?

Is Michael already close enough that I would have to abandon the call?

Has another player just pulled him toward this building?

If the answer looks bad, I move first and call later.

If the house is quiet, I prefer getting the call started early because the value
continues after I leave the phone behind.

A failed attempt also does not necessarily kill the plan.

One of my calls ended with **Unconvincing Call**. The phone was still available,
so I tried again rather than writing off the entire police route. The retry
succeeded.

The real cost of a retry is exposure.

If Michael has reached the house between attempts, insisting on finishing the
second call can turn a recoverable failure into a death.

<h2 id="inventory">
  Once an escape is identified, inventory stops being a collection
</h2>

The early item pool creates a very believable trap: almost everything looks
worth carrying.

Healing Spray can save a run.

A weapon can interrupt Michael.

A Repair Kit can move the car.

Sedan Keys can finish the car.

That does not mean they are equally valuable in the same moment.

The inventory I tested supports **one large weapon plus three smaller items**.
That gives you flexibility, but not enough space to carry an answer for every
future route.

In one early match I had Healing Spray, Sedan Keys, and a Repair Kit competing
for inventory space while the car route was developing.

That is when I stop asking “Which item is generally better?”

I read the route.

If the car engine is unfinished, the Repair Kit has an immediate destination.

If the engine is done but the tank is empty, gas moves ahead of another repair
item.

If repair and gas are already complete, carrying the Sedan Keys toward the car
matters more than continuing to search houses for theoretically useful gear.

Before an escape is discovered, healing and a defensive option are easier to
justify because you still do not know what the match will demand.

After an escape is discovered, carrying an item with no connection to that
route becomes harder to defend.

<h2 id="residents">
  Give residents jobs that match what the team already knows
</h2>

A successful resident interaction can lead to three commands:

**Follow Me**

**Hide**

**Call For Help**

    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    A convinced resident is not automatically an escort objective. The command
    should match what you already know about the match.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The screenshot above caught a useful state: the resident interaction succeeded,
but the HUD still showed **No Open Escapes Discovered**.

That changes the choice.

Leading the resident across town without a destination only creates another
moving responsibility.

At that stage I prefer **Call For Help** when I want more authority pressure, or
**Hide** when Michael is close and I cannot safely move the resident.

**Follow Me** becomes much stronger after an exit has been found.

Now there is somewhere to go.

This is one of the places where multiplayer rewards doing things in the right
order rather than simply doing more things.

A resident plus no destination is another problem.

A resident plus an open exit is a rescue route.

<h2 id="car-escape">
  The car is three jobs, not one discovery
</h2>

Finding the car feels like a breakthrough.

The HUD quickly shows why it is only the beginning.

The route requires:

1. **Repair the Car's Engine**
2. **Fill the Car's Gas Tank**
3. **Use the Sedan Keys to Start the Car**

Those jobs remain separate.

A Repair Kit solves the engine.

It does not fill the tank.

Sedan Keys do not compensate for missing gas.

And finding another repair item after the engine is already complete does not
move this particular car any closer to leaving.

The fastest way to waste a car route is to keep searching for “car stuff”
without checking which line is unfinished.

I start treating the vehicle as my main route once there is more than a marker
on the map.

A located car plus one completed requirement is meaningful progress.

A located car with no repair, no gas, and no known key is still one possibility
among several.

That difference matters when another teammate discovers an exit on the other
side of the map.

<h2 id="storm-cellar">
  The Storm Cellar can change its barrier, so read the objective in front of you
</h2>

The Storm Cellar is not finished when you find the door.

One version I encountered required:

**Use Bolt Cutters to Cut the Chains**

followed by:

**Open the Storm Cellar**

Another setup showed **Key or Bolt Cutters** at the padlock and also tracked
boards that needed to be destroyed.

Because of that variation, I would not enter every match carrying one memorized
Storm Cellar shopping list.

Use the current HUD.

If it asks for chains, find what clears the chains.

If the padlock accepts another solution, follow that version instead.

Reaching the entrance with the right tool also does not mean the escape is
already safe.

Opening the route can take time and involve skill checks. Michael arriving
during that interaction can erase the advantage of finding the correct item in
the first place.

When a teammate is already opening the cellar, that is a good moment for someone
else to stop looting and protect the interaction.

<h2 id="switch-routes">
  Compare what is still missing before you defend sunk progress
</h2>

A route can be half complete and still become the worse option.

Suppose the car engine is repaired.

Gas is still missing.

Nobody has seen the Sedan Keys.

Michael has returned to the car more than once.

Then another player discovers a Storm Cellar and already has the tool needed for
its current barrier.

The repaired engine feels valuable because the team has already invested time
there.

That time is gone either way.

The useful comparison is what each route costs **from this moment forward**.

A route missing one known piece is usually attractive.

A route missing two unknown pieces while Michael controls the area becomes much
harder to justify.

An alternate exit with its missing tool already in someone's inventory can move
from backup plan to best plan immediately.

I recheck the route whenever one of three things happens:

- a new exit is discovered;
- a required item appears;
- Michael starts repeatedly controlling the objective area.

That keeps an early decision from becoming a match-long obligation.

<h2 id="police-window">
  Use police pressure and weapons to buy interaction time
</h2>

Police create trouble for Michael.

A weapon can create a brief interruption.

Neither one needs to become the main event.

When Michael is occupied, I look for the longest useful action the team has been
unable to finish:

- repair the engine;
- add gas;
- open the Storm Cellar;
- complete a phone call;
- move a resident through exposed ground.

That is the payoff.

Standing beside the police just to watch them pressure Michael spends the same
window without finishing anything.

Weapons work the same way.

The useful question after a stun is not how much damage I dealt.

It is whether those seconds allowed somebody to finish an objective.

A short interruption can be enough to complete the last part of an escape.

Chasing Michael down the street afterward usually gives those seconds back.

<h2 id="open-exit">
  An open exit turns staying into the risky choice
</h2>

One of my clearest multiplayer decisions happened after the Storm Cellar was
already open.

The escape marker was only **12 meters** away.

Other players were still alive.

    width={1600}

    className="rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    At 12 meters from an open exit, escaping is already solved. Staying behind
    needs a specific rescue plan.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

I went back for another player.

The match immediately became another chase.

That is not proof that helping teammates is a bad play. It showed me where the
decision changes.

Before the exit opens, taking risks is part of solving the match.

After the exit opens, there needs to be a reason to add risk back in.

I am comfortable turning around when I know:

- where the other player is;
- how I can reach them;
- where Michael is occupied;
- how both of us can return to the open escape.

If all I know is “someone is still alive somewhere,” I leave.

An open route that I understand is real value.

An unknown rescue on the opposite side of Haddonfield is a new objective with
no guarantee that I can solve it.

<h2 id="next-match">
  Let the first useful discovery give you a job
</h2>

The opening feels less chaotic after a few matches because roles begin forming
without anyone formally assigning them.

Find a safe phone first?

Handle the call.

Pick up Sedan Keys and learn where the car is?

That connection gives you a route to follow.

Find the tool for a marked Storm Cellar?

Deliver it instead of continuing to search unrelated houses.

Convince a resident after somebody has already opened an exit?

Now escorting them has a destination.

The useful role can change halfway through the match. Dropping that role when
new information makes another route cheaper is part of playing well, not a
failure to finish what you started.

That is the multiplayer loop I use:

**discover something useful → connect it to a real objective → finish that job
or switch when a cheaper route appears.**
