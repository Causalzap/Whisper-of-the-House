<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
With only **three inventory slots**, keep one item that can save you from
Michael, one item that directly advances the escape you are actually working
on, and leave the third slot flexible.

If you find an **Ancient Amulet** or one of the grab-escape blades, it is
usually worth protecting that defensive slot. Do not carry three different
escape parts across the map unless they are all for a route your team is
already finishing.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="three-slots">
  What Should You Keep With Only Three Inventory Slots?
</h2>

Three slots are enough until the match gives me four useful things at once.

That is where most bad inventory decisions start. I find a rare pickup, then a
car part, then Bolt Cutters, then a weapon, and suddenly I am trying to carry
every possible plan instead of finishing one. The better question is not
whether an item is useful. It is whether that item has a **job in the next few
minutes**.

I normally reserve one slot for survival. A Pocket Knife, Butterfly Knife, Ice
Pick, or Ancient Amulet lets me commit to slower interactions without feeling
that a single grab ends the run.

The second slot goes to the route that already has progress. If the Sedan has
been found and only needs one missing piece, I carry that piece. If the Storm
Cellar is sitting behind a padlock, Bolt Cutters suddenly matter more than Gas
for a car nobody has seen.

The third slot is where I stay flexible. A weapon, Flare Gun, healing item,
throwable, light source, or temporary objective item can move through that
slot as the map changes.

<h2 id="rare-items">
  Ancient Amulet, Brass Lantern, and Fragile Hourglass Do Three Very Different Jobs
</h2>

Rare does not automatically mean "carry this until the match ends." The three
special pickups ask for completely different decisions.

### Ancient Amulet: the rare pickup I almost never walk past

The **Ancient Amulet** protects the carrier from one damaging event before it
breaks. That protection is valuable against a normal attack and especially
important when Michael is trying to convert a close approach into a grab.

The drawback is just as important: the amulet is not saving itself for the
perfect moment. Damage from another source can consume it, including friendly
damage. I treat it as one protected mistake rather than permanent safety.

The real value appears when I am about to stand still. Opening an escape,
repairing something, or working around a phone gives Michael time to close the
gap. An Amulet means that first successful connection does not immediately
turn into the result he wanted.

### Brass Lantern: protection before Michael reaches me

The **Brass Lantern** approaches the same danger from the other direction.
Once active, it creates a large light source around the carrier. Michael cannot
use his hidden movement to reappear as close as he normally would inside that
lit area, so he has to complete more of the approach in a way I can react to.

The light also improves awareness. Michael can be exposed while moving inside
the illuminated area, which matters most when I am tied to one location instead
of freely sprinting away.

That makes the Lantern particularly attractive beside a Cellar, Gate, phone,
or another objective where leaving halfway through wastes progress.

### Fragile Hourglass: wait for a controlled throw

The **Fragile Hourglass** is the one rare item I refuse to panic-throw. It can
deal severe damage, but the same effect is dangerous to nearby teammates.

I want Michael separated from the group, ideally already down or close enough
to a down that the throw is easy to control. Throwing it into a pile of
Civilians turns a valuable pickup into a way to destroy my own team's position.

<h2 id="grab-escape-items">
  Pocket Knife, Butterfly Knife, and Ice Pick Are for the Grab, Not for Showing Off
</h2>

The **Pocket Knife, Butterfly Knife, and Ice Pick** all solve the state I care
about most: Michael already has me.

When that happens, the blade is consumed to break the grab. I therefore value
one of these more highly than another ordinary melee weapon, especially before
an interaction where I cannot instantly run.

point another Pocket Knife, Butterfly Knife, or Ice Pick is once again a

<h2 id="flare-gun">
  Flare Gun: Shoot Michael or Fire It Into the Sky?
</h2>

The Flare Gun has two completely different uses, and using the wrong one at the
wrong time wastes more than ammunition.

A direct shot gives me a long-range way to hurt Michael and can create a blind
or stun window. That is valuable when somebody is trapped, Michael is standing
between the team and an objective, or one clean interruption lets everyone
reset.

The other use can be more important early:

**Fire the Flare Gun into the sky and it immediately adds one Police Badge.**

check the police progress. When another badge meaningfully moves that route

<h2 id="light-items">
  Flashlight, Firecrackers, and Road Flare Buy Time in Different Ways
</h2>

All three can interfere with Michael, but they do not solve the same situation.

A **Flashlight** needs time on target. I have to keep the beam on Michael long
enough to build the blind, and the remaining battery determines whether that
plan is realistic. A nearly empty flashlight can look like security in the
inventory while failing at the moment I actually need it.

That is why a **Flashlight Battery** is a strange pickup. It has value only
because another item needs it, and together they can consume two of my three
slots. Unless the flashlight is central to what I am doing, I would rather find
another usable light source than carry a battery across the map.

**Firecrackers** act immediately. A good throw can blind Michael, potentially
stun him at close range, and create enough noise to draw his attention toward
the wrong location. When a teammate is opening an exit somewhere else, that
noise can be more useful than the damage.

A **Road Flare** is better for controlling a space. I activate it first, then
throw it where Michael has to look or pass through the light. Throwing it
without activation removes the reason I carried it.

<h2 id="repair-kit">
  A Repair Kit Is Not Just a Sedan Item
</h2>

The Sedan is the obvious reason to grab a **Repair Kit**, but it is not the
only one.

Michael can damage vehicles, phone lines, and power equipment. A Repair Kit can
restore those systems. That matters when a useful building has lost power or
when a phone or radio is sitting behind infrastructure Michael already broke.

collapses. A used-up route does not deserve permanent backpack space.

<h2 id="sedan">
  Sedan Key and Gas Become Valuable After the Car Is Actually a Real Escape
</h2>

The car objective is unusually clear about its requirements:

1. **Use the Sedan Key to start the car.**
2. **Repair the car's engine.**
3. **Fill the gas tank.**

Finding one of those parts does not mean the Sedan is immediately the best
route.

When I know where the Sedan is, staging parts near the vehicle solves a lot of
inventory pressure. A key sitting beside the correct car is still useful to the
team; a key riding in my backpack while I search the opposite side of
Haddonfield is using a slot without helping anyone.

The same logic applies to Gas. Once the car is found and its other requirements
are progressing, the Gas Can becomes high priority. Before that point it is a
bet on a route that may lose to an almost-complete Cellar or Gate.

When several escapes are already visible and I need to decide which one is
closest to completion, I use the

</Link>
instead of carrying parts for all of them.

<h2 id="bolt-cutters">
  Bolt Cutters Are Excellent Until the Locks You Need Are Already Open
</h2>

The Storm Cellar objective makes the choice explicit:

**Use a Key or Bolt Cutters on the Padlock.**

That turns Bolt Cutters into one of the strongest early utility pickups because
I do not need the exact matching key when the cutters can remove the lock
directly.

The important moment comes afterward.

In one run, I used the cutters on a nearby Cellar, moved to another Cellar,
then left the cutters around that escape area before going back for a weapon.
That is exactly how I want to treat a utility tool: carry it while its job
exists, stage it where teammates may still need it, then recover the slot.

Walking around for another ten minutes with Bolt Cutters after every useful
lock is already open means I am giving up a weapon, healing item, or grab
escape for a job that is finished.

<h2 id="exit-gate">
  The Exit Gate Needs More Than the Electric Fuse
</h2>

The Gate can create another inventory trap because finding the rare-looking
**Electric Fuse** feels like progress before the other requirements are ready.

The objective can require:

- clearing the overgrown plants,
- installing the Fuse,
- opening the padlock with a Key or Bolt Cutters,
- and finally opening the Gate.

That means the Fuse is critical **after the Gate becomes the route**, not
automatically the moment I find it.

A long Fuse or Gate interaction is also exactly where I want a defensive blade
in the remaining inventory. Carrying the Fuse, Bolt Cutters, and a random third
objective item with no protection leaves Michael a very simple answer: wait for
the interaction, close the distance, and grab.

The Fuse appears to be relatively uncommon in ordinary containers. When I find
one and the Gate is already viable, I stop wandering and move it toward the
objective instead of gambling that I will find another later.

<h2 id="keys-and-codes">
  Escape Keys, Locker Codes, and Police Car Keys Need a Destination
</h2>

A key becomes much easier to value once I know the lock it belongs to.

**Escape Keys** can replace Bolt Cutters on compatible escape padlocks. That
saves the tool slot, but a generic key is still only useful while a matching
locked route is relevant.

**Locker Codes** open locked stashes and weapon lockers. I use one quickly
when I already know where the container is. Carrying a code across the map in
the hope that I eventually find a worthwhile stash is much harder to defend
with only three slots.

**Police Car Keys** have an extra failure state: the nearby police car may not
be the matching one. Finding the keys does not mean I should immediately throw
away a working escape plan. I first confirm that the vehicle they belong to is
actually available.

<h2 id="consumables">
  Beer, Joint, and Pop Are Often Better Used Than Stored
</h2>

Consumables solve their own inventory problem once I stop saving them for a
perfect moment that never comes.

A **Beer Can** applies the drunk status. Movement becomes awkward and the screen
distorts, but fear builds more slowly. That can matter in a fight, particularly
for a character or perk setup that benefits from being drunk.

A **Beer Bottle** has the same status value plus one additional option: throw
it. The bottle can hit Michael from range, then leave a broken bottle behind
as a small melee weapon. I care more about the throwable use than protecting
the broken weapon afterward.

A **Joint** is different. The high status improves distant noise awareness,
making faraway activity easier to notice. That can help me locate Residents
and notice Michael moving nearby. Since the effect is the point, I normally
use the Joint rather than carry it unopened.

**Pop** is one of the easiest consumables to spend immediately. Reduced stamina
consumption helps on a long cross-map rotation, during a chase, or in a fight
where repeated movement matters. Drinking it before that section of the match
turns an occupied slot into an active buff.

<h2 id="healing">
  Healing Spray Is Faster; Medkit Is Bigger
</h2>

The two healing items solve different damage states.

**Healing Spray** restores part of the health bar and can also be used on a
teammate. The speed is why I like it. If Michael has left me injured and my
movement is suffering, partial health now is often worth more than a larger
heal I cannot safely finish.

A **Medkit** restores much more health but locks me into a longer animation.
I want cover, distance, or a teammate creating pressure before I commit to it.

Healing is also competing with grab protection. Full health does not save me
from a successful grab the way a Pocket Knife, Ice Pick, Butterfly Knife, or
Ancient Amulet can. When Michael is playing for grabs rather than normal
damage, I give that defensive slot priority.

<h2 id="throwables">
  Lawn Darts Are Better Than Their Size Suggests
</h2>

Baseballs, Rocks, Bricks, and Mugs are simple ranged answers. They hurt or
stagger Michael without forcing me into melee range, which is often enough to
free a teammate or create an escape window.

In my runs, the **Brick** is the basic throwable I trust most for raw impact.
The **Mug** is disposable because it breaks after the throw.

The **Lawn Dart** is much more interesting because the inventory system treats
it efficiently:

**up to three Lawn Darts can stack in one slot.**

They also stick into Michael and can later fall back onto the ground, letting
me recover them. That makes a three-dart stack unusually good value when I
want repeated ranged interruptions.

Missing still has a cost. Michael can pick the dart up and throw it back, so
one bad attempt can hand the weapon to the other side.

<h2 id="firearms">
  Magnum, Shotgun, and Rifle Ask for Different Distances
</h2>

There are three firearm types I treat as the main ranged weapons.

The **Magnum Revolver** is the easiest one for me to use consistently. It holds
**six rounds**, appears commonly on police, and Loomis can also carry one.
Headshots give it meaningful damage without demanding the extreme spacing of
the Rifle.

The **Shotgun** carries **four rounds** and has been the easiest firearm for me
to replace. It is still better than being unarmed, but four mediocre shots are
a poor use of one-third of the backpack when a strong objective item appears.

The **Rifle** has the highest ceiling. At range, a clean headshot can put
Michael down outright, and the same reach makes it useful for interrupting him
when he grabs another Civilian.

The weakness appears when Michael is already close. A weapon built around
distance becomes much less comfortable once he has closed it.

<h2 id="melee-weapons">
  The Best Melee Weapons Give Me Something Besides a Basic Swing
</h2>

A short melee weapon is better than empty hands, but I replace one quickly when
a weapon offers another function.

**Kitchen Knife, Fire Poker, Police Baton, Pipe Wrench, and Broken Bottle**
mostly live in that emergency category. A Fire Poker is particularly easy to
find around fireplaces inside homes, so it is a reasonable early pickup without
being something I build the whole match around.

The **Fire Axe** earns more respect because it is both a weapon and a tool. It
can hit Michael hard, break wooden panels on escape routes, and break doors
that are blocking access to Residents. Fire Axes also appear commonly outside
around trees.

After enough use, the Axe can break into a **Hatchet**. That changes the role
from heavy utility weapon to throwable. A good Hatchet hit can do major damage
and interrupt Michael from range, but a miss can leave the weapon available
for him to pick up.

The **Sledgehammer** is the close-range option I use when I deliberately want
to fight. Heavy hits build strong stun pressure, and once Michael is stunned,
the team gets a short period where he cannot move or attack. When the
Sledgehammer finally breaks, it leaves a Brick behind for one more ranged
attempt.

The **Rake** behaves almost like a spear. I can poke at close range or throw it
from farther away, which makes it much more flexible than the smallest melee
weapons. The tradeoff is the same one I accept with a Hatchet: Michael can
recover it and throw it back.

<h2 id="drop-first">
  What Should You Drop First When the Backpack Is Full?
</h2>

I start by asking which item has already lost its condition.

A Sedan Key does not need to travel with me after I have staged it beside the
Sedan. Bolt Cutters can stay near an opened Cellar. A Flashlight Battery is not
worth protecting when I no longer have a good Flashlight. A Locker Code with
no known locked container is a promise, not progress. A nearly empty firearm
may be less useful than a simple throwable that still gives me one reliable
interruption.

Consumables often solve the problem without wasting anything. Drink the Pop,
smoke the Joint, use the healing item when the health loss justifies it, then
take the new pickup into the freed slot.

What I avoid dropping is the item that ends the work directly in front of me.
When the Gate is waiting on its Fuse, that Fuse matters more than another
interesting weapon. When the Sedan only needs its Key, the Key matters more
than winning a fight the team could simply avoid.

By the middle of a good match, my backpack has usually changed several times.
The three items I found first are almost never the three items I want at the
exit. The useful inventory is the one that keeps me alive through the next
mistake, advances the route that already has progress, and still leaves enough
room to react when Michael changes where the pressure is coming from.
