<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
**Ines Cortez is the woman behind La Llorona.**

The part that initially confused me is that Last Rites introduces **La
Llorona** as the target, then starts talking about **Ines Cortez** as if I am
tracking somebody new.

I am not.

Ines was one of Emily Price's former intelligence assets. Price believed Ines
had been killed after her cover was exposed, but she never personally verified
that death. Ines survived, while her children were killed, and she later became
the figure the Penitentes worship as La Llorona.

That is also why the final order changes.

Killing Ines would turn La Llorona into a martyr, so Price changes the mission
from **terminate** to **exfiltrate**.

The ending line that **“La Llorona is dead”** does not mean Ines was killed.

cult leader and threat.

<h2 id="who-is-la-llorona">
  Who La Llorona really is in Last Rites
</h2>

For the first half of Last Rites, I treated **La Llorona** like an unknown
person I had not identified yet.

That is exactly how the operation begins.

Emily Price gives me the name La Llorona and an order to find her, while the
investigation gradually replaces that codename with another name:

**Ines Cortez.**

The point where it finally clicked for me was the conversation with Price after
the Vargas operation.

Nomad stops accepting vague answers and asks Price directly what she knows
about Ines.

Price admits:

**Ines was one of her assets.**

**Ines Cortez = the person**

**La Llorona = the identity and cult figure built around her.**

That distinction matters again at the very end.

<h2 id="what-happened-to-ines">
  What happened to Ines before Last Rites
</h2>

Price's history with Ines explains why the final mission stops being a simple
assassination.

Ines had been married to **Marco Cortez** and was passing intelligence while
working as Price's asset.

Then her cover was blown.

Price had been told that Ines had been killed, along with her twin daughters.

But when Nomad asks whether Price actually verified what happened, the answer
is effectively no.

Price relied on what she had been told.

She admits she did not know Ines had survived.

That detail changed how I understood Price for the rest of the operation.

She is not discovering an unrelated cult leader.

She is discovering that an asset she believed dead survived the consequences of
an operation she helped run.

And Ines did not come out of it intact.

Her children were killed.

The final confrontation makes that personal cost impossible to ignore.

<h2 id="why-price-is-personally-involved">
  Why Emily Price stops feeling like a neutral mission handler
</h2>

Before this reveal, I could still treat Price as somebody feeding me
intelligence from outside the operation.

After it, I cannot.

There are now two missions happening at the same time.

Nomad still needs to stop the Penitentes threat.

Price is also facing the consequences of what happened to one of her own
assets.

That explains why Nomad becomes increasingly suspicious of her judgment near
the end.

It also explains why I do not read Price's later order change as a random
last-minute rewrite.

She has a personal connection to Ines.

But there is also a tactical reason for changing the mission.

Both things can be true at the same time.

<h2 id="why-terminate-changes-to-exfiltrate">
  Why the order changes from terminate to exfiltrate
</h2>

The final stronghold intel eventually puts Ines at an old gold mine.

At first, Nomad is still operating under the original logic:

**find the target and terminate her.**

Price changes that before the final confrontation.

Her reasoning is the key to the ending:

**if I kill Ines, the Penitentes can turn La Llorona into a martyr.**

That would give the cult exactly the kind of death it could build a mythology

Price even suspects Ines may understand that herself.

So the mission changes from removing the person to preventing the cult from
getting the ending it wants.

That is why:

**terminate**

becomes:

**exfiltrate.**

For me, this is the most important gameplay/story crossover in the ending.

I do not keep following the first order just because it was the original
briefing.

Once the objective changes, killing Ines is no longer the solution.

Getting her out alive is.

Ines confronts Emily directly about what happened when her intelligence work

That is why the confrontation works better for me once I already understand

Ines is not simply angry because Price has come to arrest her.

She sees Price as part of the chain of events that destroyed her old life.

<h2 id="why-price-refuses-to-kill-ines">
  Why Price still refuses to kill Ines at the mine
</h2>

Even after Ines turns on them, Price does not return to the original kill
order.

Nomad points out the obvious problem:

the mission was supposed to terminate her.

Price answers by asserting command and keeping the extraction order in place.

That decision matters because it shows the martyr explanation was not just
something she said before entering the mine.

She follows through when killing Ines would be much easier.

From the player's side, my judgment is simple:

**once Price says exfiltrate, I stop treating Ines as a boss I need to finish
off.**

I get her up and get her out.

Whether Price is making that decision entirely because it is strategically
correct or partly because she feels responsible for Ines is deliberately harder
to separate.

Nomad clearly questions her objectivity.

The game gives me good reason to do the same.

But the tactical argument still holds:

dead La Llorona can become a symbol.

Living Ines can be removed from the cult.

<h2 id="what-la-llorona-is-dead-means">
  What “La Llorona is dead” means in the final debrief
</h2>

This is the line that made me stop and check what the ending was actually
saying.

After I extract Ines alive, Price later tells Nomad that the mission was
technically successful and that:

**La Llorona is dead.**

Nomad immediately asks about:

**Ines Cortez.**

Price answers that Ines is **not a threat anymore**.

That wording only makes sense if I stop treating the names as two physical
people.

The operation has killed **La Llorona as a functioning cult identity and
leader**.

It has not killed Ines Cortez.

The Penitentes lose the woman around whom their movement was organized, without
receiving the clean martyrdom ending that killing her would have created.

That is why the objective switch matters so much.

If Nomad had simply followed the original terminate order, Ines would be dead
but La Llorona could have become even more powerful as a symbol.

Instead:

**Ines survives**

while:

**La Llorona stops functioning as the active threat.**

That is the ending I think Price is describing.

<h2 id="what-the-ending-means">
  What I take away from the Last Rites ending
</h2>

The ending is not really about whether Nomad can reach Ines.

By the final mission, I already know where she is.

The harder question is what actually ends the threat.

The operation begins with a simple instruction:

**La Llorona needs to die.**

By the end, that turns out to be the wrong solution if I interpret it literally.

Killing Ines gives the Penitentes a martyr.

Extracting her separates the real woman from the identity the cult built around
her.

Price also has to confront the fact that the target did not appear from
nowhere.

Ines' history is connected directly to an intelligence operation Price was
part of, to an unverified death, and to the loss of Ines' children.

So I do not read the ending as:

**Price suddenly feels sorry for the villain and cancels the mission.**

I read it as two realizations arriving at the same time:

**killing Ines is strategically wrong**

and:

**Price is personally tied to the reason Ines became La Llorona in the first
place.**

That is why the final debrief uses two different names.

The woman survives.

The identity I was originally sent to eliminate does not.
