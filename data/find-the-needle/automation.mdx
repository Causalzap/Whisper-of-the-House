<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Unlock **Conveyor Plans**, run a short belt to the selling stand, and feed a **Hay Generator** so a powered **Piston Rake** or **Robot Arm** can collect hay for you. Check the belt arrows and Power Pole coverage before switching the line on.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

A conveyor can sell hay while you are still digging it by hand. The shift to automatic collection happens when a powered Piston Rake or Robot Arm starts dropping hay onto that belt. From there, three failures matter most: a missed drop, a belt running away from the selling stand, or a collector that has stopped receiving power.

<section id="first-automatic-line" className="scroll-mt-24">

## What Do You Need for Your First Automatic Hay Line?

The opening steps give you enough cash and building controls to start with one simple route. After the early hand-tool tasks, unlock **Conveyor Plans** in the Tech Tree for **$40**. Belt construction then costs approximately **$8 per meter**, so avoid spending your remaining money on a long run around the entire shed.

Build toward the selling stand first. Put hay on the moving belt and confirm that it reaches the stand before buying more equipment. At this point, the belt is useful even while you are still digging and loading it by hand.

The next purchase is **Electricity**, which unlocks the Hay Generator and Power Poles. The unlock costs **$90**, and a Hay Generator costs **$150** to build. The tutorial provides your first Power Pole for free; additional poles normally cost **$45**. The generator burns hay to produce electricity, so make sure it has a steady supply of fuel.

Once the generator and pole are in place, position a hay-collecting machine where it can reach the pile and unload onto the belt. The first Piston Rake provides a way to get this started. A Robot Arm is a useful follow-up when the rake's unloading behavior begins to create clutter.

Keep the initial route short enough that you can see both the collector's drop point and the selling stand. A missed load is easier to catch before you extend the belt around the yard.

## How Do You Place and Connect Conveyor Belts?

Open the build menu and select the conveyor. Lay a short section from the collection area toward the stand, watching the cost increase as the route grows. You do not have to reach the hay pile with the belt itself; the collecting machine needs to be able to drop its output onto it.

**Check the moving arrows after placement.** The arrows show which direction the belt will carry material. If they point away from the stand, reverse the belt before allowing a full load onto it. An otherwise correctly powered factory can appear to be doing nothing useful when the items are simply traveling in the wrong direction.

These controls help when a belt or machine needs repositioning:

- **G** toggles grid snapping while placing a building. Use it when you need belts and machines to line up cleanly.
- **R** rotates a held building. Check its input and output sides again after rotating.
- **Mouse wheel** changes the placement distance while building.
- **Hold X** while aiming at a placed structure to dismantle it. The opening tutorial demonstrates getting the building cost back, making it practical to correct a badly routed first belt.

For the first production line, keep the route direct. A short conveyor is easier to inspect, costs less to build, and leaves space to add a processing machine later. When you change the route, turn the affected line off first, make the connection, and only then resume movement.

## How Do You Power Machines With a Hay Generator?

The starter Hay Generator can provide up to **6 kW**, and the starting Power Pole supplies machines within **6 meters**. Placing a generator somewhere in the shed is not enough: your rake or arm must be inside the reach of the connected power-pole network.

Set up the power side of the factory in this order:

1. Build the Hay Generator close enough to receive hay from your line or from a separate feed.
2. Place a Power Pole near the machines you want to run.
3. Check that the pole network reaches the generator and that the powered machines fall inside a pole's coverage area.
4. Start the line and confirm that hay is reaching the generator and that the collecting machine begins working.

A pole's visible coverage circle matters more than how close a machine looks from where you are standing. If a machine sits just outside that circle, add or reposition a pole instead of immediately buying another generator.

The generator also needs hay. If the power line worked earlier and then stopped, inspect the generator's incoming belt before spending money on upgrades. A conveyor change elsewhere in the yard can leave the generator without fuel even though all the poles are still connected.

### Why Is a Powered Machine Running Slowly?

**Coverage and capacity are different problems.** A machine can be connected to the grid and still run below full speed when the line's demand exceeds available generation.

When electricity demand exceeds the available supply, machines on that line run below full speed. A Smelter, for example, can show a warning that Line 1 is operating at only **63%**. If the machine is already connected to a Power Pole, the problem is insufficient generating capacity rather than coverage. Add another Hay Generator or unlock **Stronger Generator** in the Tech Tree to increase the available power.

## Should You Use a Piston Rake or a Robot Arm?

A **Piston Rake** is a reasonable first collector because it gets hay moving while you are still saving for more equipment. Its range and unloading position are worth checking before you commit to a long conveyor layout.

A **Robot Arm** is useful when your conveyor is already carrying a steady flow of hay. A Piston Rake can drop material onto the floor when its landing area is occupied, while a Robot Arm can wait for an opening before unloading. This makes the Robot Arm easier to manage on a crowded belt, especially when several collectors share the same route.

To make an arm useful rather than merely expensive, check three things after placing it:

- **Reach:** Its collection area must overlap the hay you want it to pick up.
- **Drop point:** Its output needs to land on the intended belt, not beside it or on a different production route.
- **Power:** The arm must have a working connection to the pole network with enough capacity to operate.

Watch the first few loads. If hay piles up on the floor, correct the unloading position or the receiving belt before adding another arm. If the arm is idle while the belt moves, check its reach, power, and whether the belt has room to accept an item.

Robot Arms are not a reason to dismantle every rake immediately. Keep a working rake while saving for the next machine, then replace or move it when the new setup can handle the same job without interrupting sales.

## How Do You Fix a Machine Jammed by Scrap Metal?

Scrap can travel out of the hay pile alongside ordinary loads. If it enters a machine that cannot process it, the machine can jam even while the surrounding belt and electrical line are working normally.

When the game reports that **metal got into a machine and it stopped**, walk up to the affected machine and **press E to clear the jam**. Then watch the next few loads to see whether the machine resumes processing or jams again.

Clearing the warning restores the interrupted machine, but it does not change the material arriving on the belt. If the same machine repeatedly jams, inspect what the collecting equipment is placing on its input route. Keep obvious scrap away from hay-only processing equipment until you have a proper way to separate it.

Changing a Robot Arm's pickup settings may reduce unwanted material, but it will not tell you what has already entered the processing line. If jams return, follow the incoming belt and look for the scrap that is reaching the machine.

**Do not confuse a jam with a power shortage.** The jam has a machine-specific warning and an interaction to clear it. A power shortage instead requires checking generator fuel, pole coverage, or available electrical capacity.

## How Should You Expand the Factory Without Breaking the First Line?

Once a single collector is reliably selling hay, add a second route only for a clear purpose. For example, you may want to send some hay toward a processing machine while leaving another path available for direct sales or generator fuel.

An orderly expansion is easier than rebuilding everything while it is moving:

1. Let the current belt clear as much of its load as practical, then switch off the line you intend to change.
2. Identify where the new machine will collect from and where its output will go.
3. Place the machine and belt connection, rotating parts as needed so their directions agree.
4. Check Power Pole reach and the added electricity demand.
5. Switch the line back on and follow a few loads all the way to their destination.

As the factory grows, keep Robot Arms from reaching across unrelated conveyor routes. A larger setup with six Robot Arms can work, but their collection ranges and unloading points need to be checked individually to prevent material from entering the wrong belt. Before switching on an expanded line, confirm that every collector has enough power, can reach the intended hay, and unloads onto the correct conveyor. Adding more arms will not improve production if the receiving belts cannot keep up.

## How Do You Know the Factory Is Running Correctly?

Before leaving the equipment to work, follow one complete load through the system rather than judging the factory by how many machines are moving.

1. Wait for the Rake or Arm to collect hay from the pile.
2. Watch where it drops the load. It should reach the belt rather than the floor.
3. Follow that load along the belt to the selling stand or the intended machine.
4. Check that sales increase or the receiving machine accepts and processes the load.
5. Let several more loads pass. The line should keep running without an electrical warning, repeated jam, or a growing pile of missed items.

If the test fails, correct the first step that did not happen. There is no advantage in adding another machine when the current one cannot deliver a full load to its destination.
