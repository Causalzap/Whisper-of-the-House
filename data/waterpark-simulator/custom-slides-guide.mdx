Custom Slides were one of those unlocks I wanted to use immediately and then
deliberately ignored for several park days.

That turned out to be the right choice.

When I unlocked them, the game explained that Custom Slides are built by mixing
and matching individual segments in the Build Menu, and that **every piece costs
money**. My park still had more urgent Research and progression bottlenecks, so
I did not start drawing a giant ride just because the editor had become
available.

When I finally tested it, I learned the more important lesson: the editor will
let me be much more ambitious than I should be on the first attempt.

One route test reached about **34.91 metres**. Later I experimented with a much
larger cross-park idea, discovered that the route could gain height, and managed
to launch myself far enough to end up **across the street**.

That was enough to change my build rule:

**prove the start, height and landing with a small route first; only then spend
money on a park-wide Custom Slide.**

<h2 id="when-to-build-custom-slides">
  I Do Not Build a Custom Slide as Soon as It Unlocks
</h2>

This was one of my clearest buy-or-wait decisions in the late game.

At one point I had **17 Research Points** available and the slide branch was
opening toward Custom Slides. I still chose a Hiring Level improvement instead
because the park needed more operating capacity.

Later, after Custom Slides were actually unlocked, my immediate progression
target was still Prestige Slides.

So I waited again.

That is the difference between an unlock being interesting and an unlock being
the current bottleneck.

I start spending on a Custom Slide when:

- the existing park survives an ordinary day without me constantly rescuing it;
- staff salaries and restocking are already covered;
- the current star objective is not blocked by another specific Research unlock;
- I have a pool or landing area I actually want to build toward;
- I can afford to rebuild part of the route if the first test is bad.

I wait when:

- I am still saving Research for a required Prestige attraction;
- ticketing or staffing is the real bottleneck;
- I would have to use the operating reserve just to experiment;
- I have no clear landing target;
- the surrounding themed area is still being rebuilt.

Every extra piece costs money, so experimentation becomes expensive much faster
than placing one normal prefab slide.

<h2 id="start-with-a-short-route">
  How I Build My First Custom Slide
</h2>

My first goal is not to make the longest slide possible.

It is to prove that the editor, starting position and landing plan work together.

I choose the **destination first**.

For a permanent park ride, I prefer a pool with enough empty space around its
edge that I can change the final approach without immediately colliding with
another slide, path or decoration.

Then I work backward:

1. choose the pool;
2. identify the side I want riders to approach from;
3. choose a starting area with enough room for the first segments;
4. create a short path;
5. check height and direction;
6. test it;
7. only then extend it.

That is much safer than starting high above the middle of the park and assuming
I will somehow find a good landing point 20 segments later.

One editor test displayed approximately **34.91 metres**.

<h2 id="can-custom-slides-go-up">
  Can Custom Slides Go Up?
</h2>

This was one of the first things I wanted to know before attempting a
park-spanning design.

I initially assumed I might need to think about Custom Slides like a realistic
gravity ride: start high, keep losing elevation and never ask the route to climb
again.

The editor test changed that assumption.

While experimenting with the route, I was able to make the path gain height.

That means I do not plan these rides only as one continuous downhill slope.

I still test the result rather than assuming every aggressive shape will behave
well, but the editor gave me much more vertical freedom than I expected.

The important distinction is:

**editor geometry being possible does not mean my final landing is good.**

I separate those tests.

First I check whether the shape can be built.

Then I check where the rider actually ends up.

<h2 id="long-custom-slide">
  Can I Build a Custom Slide Around the Whole Park?
</h2>

That was the design I eventually wanted.

My idea was to start near the central part of the park, run the slide through
multiple themed sections and eventually return toward the Wellness Pool.

The problem was that I did not yet know how far the system would let me push the
route or how the final ride would behave.

So I did not commit the full project immediately.

This is where a lot of players can waste money.

The editor encourages me to think:

> I have all this space. Why not use all of it?

But every additional segment creates another thing I may need to undo if:

- the final direction is wrong;
- the height becomes awkward;
- the ride overshoots the intended landing;
- a later attraction needs the same land;
- I decide the route is interfering with a themed area.

I therefore build the long version in stages.

My checkpoints are:

**start → first major turn → first themed-area crossing → final approach →
landing.**

If one stage behaves badly, I fix that stage before extending the next one.

<h2 id="across-the-street">
  My First Big Test Sent Me Across the Street
</h2>

This was the moment that convinced me not to blindly trust a dramatic route.

I experimented with more height and a longer path, tested it, and ended up
**across the street** from the park.

The good news was that the concept worked.

The bad news was that "it works" and "this is a good permanent slide" are very
different standards.

After another test, I was convinced that a fast route around a large part of the
park was possible enough to keep developing.

I was **not** convinced that the first version deserved to stay.

That is the judgment I use whenever a Custom Slide produces a funny result:

- if the route itself fails, shorten it;
- if the route works but the landing is bad, change the final approach;
- if the landing works but the ride is too expensive, remove unnecessary
  segments;
- if everything works, then extend it.

<h2 id="fix-overshoot">
  What I Change When the Custom Slide Overshoots
</h2>

I do not rebuild from the starting point first.

The landing end is where I troubleshoot.

If a test carries me far beyond the intended area, I work backward from the
final section.

I check:

1. whether I gave the ride too much height before the finish;
2. whether the final direction is still pointing toward the pool;
3. whether I can shorten the last section;
4. whether the landing target itself is too small;
5. whether a slightly different pool edge gives me more room.

I only go back to the first half of the ride if the route has a bigger design
problem.

This saves a lot of work.

A 30-plus-metre route does not need to be deleted because the last few metres
are bad.

<h2 id="choose-the-landing-first">
  I Choose the Landing Before the Starting Tower
</h2>

This is the change I would make on a fresh park.

The exciting decision is where the ride starts because that determines the
view.

The expensive decision is where it ends.

A bad start can often be moved.

A bad destination can force me to redesign the entire route.

So for a permanent build I start at the pool:

**Where do I want guests to finish?**

Then I ask:

- which edge has the most open space;
- which direction gives me the easiest final approach;
- whether another slide already occupies the same landing zone;
- whether I expect to rebuild this themed area later;
- whether the pool is large enough for the kind of route I am testing.

Only after that do I pick the dramatic elevated starting point.

<h2 id="custom-slide-cost">
  Why I Do Not Spam Segments
</h2>

The unlock itself gives the most important economic warning:

**every piece costs money.**

That makes Custom Slides different from experimenting with free terrain or
decoration placement.

A route that bends unnecessarily across the whole park is not only harder to
debug. It also keeps adding construction cost.

I therefore ask what each major section is doing.

A segment should usually provide at least one of these:

- necessary height;
- necessary direction change;
- distance to the destination;
- clearance around an existing building;
- a deliberate visual feature.

If I cannot explain why a long detour is there, I remove it before committing
to a larger build.

This matters even more on Hard Mode.

I do not use Custom Slides as an excuse to spend the wage and restock reserve.

If the normal park is still losing money without construction, I fix that

<h2 id="advanced-custom-slides">
  When I Wait for Advanced Custom Slides
</h2>

Advanced Custom Slides appear farther along the same Research direction.

I do not treat that as a reason to refuse to touch the base editor.

The basic Custom Slides unlock already gave me enough to learn:

- how the editor behaves;
- how distance builds up;
- how much vertical freedom I have;
- how easy it is to overshoot;
- how much park space a long route can consume.

That information is useful before spending more Research.

My rule is:

**learn the base editor first; buy Advanced Custom Slides when I know what
limitation I am actually trying to remove.**

If I cannot yet build a clean short route with the normal system, another
Research unlock is not the real solution.

<h2 id="custom-slide-not-working">
  My Custom Slide Troubleshooting Order
</h2>

When a Custom Slide is not doing what I expected, I do not immediately delete
the whole project.

I troubleshoot in this order.

### The route is much more expensive than expected

I stop extending it.

Every piece costs money, so I remove unnecessary distance and prove the short
version first.

### I cannot get the route where I want it

I stop thinking only in two dimensions.

My test could gain height, so I check whether changing elevation solves the
obstacle before moving the entire start.

### The ride sends me too far

I return to the final approach.

The across-the-street test proved that a route can be technically successful
while producing a terrible destination.

### The destination pool is awkward

I try another edge of the same pool before rebuilding the whole ride.

If the pool itself is too constrained, I choose a larger landing area.

### I want to build around the whole park

I split the idea into checkpoints instead of drawing the complete route at once.

I prove each major section, then extend.

### I just unlocked Custom Slides but the park is struggling

I leave the editor.

An experimental slide does not fix broken ticketing, staff shortages, dirty
pools or an unfinished Prestige objective.

I come back when the existing park can afford the experiment.

<h2 id="final-takeaway">My Custom Slide Rule</h2>

Custom Slides became much easier once I stopped trying to design the final ride
on the first attempt.

I unlock the system, choose a landing, build a short route, test its height and
direction, and only then add distance.

A **34.91-metre** editor test was already enough to teach me a lot about the
system.

A later experiment that sent me across the street taught me even more.

The editor gives me enough freedom to create a huge park-spanning ride, but that
freedom is exactly why I do not start with the huge version.

My order is:

**landing first → short route → test → fix final approach → extend → test
again.**

That costs less, makes failures easier to diagnose and lets me abandon a bad
idea before it becomes a giant piece of the park.
