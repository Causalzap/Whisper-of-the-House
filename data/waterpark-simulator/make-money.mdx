My biggest money mistake was reading every negative Daily Profit number as a
failed day.

On the first day of my Hard Mode park, **14 guests** produced **$91 from
tickets** and **$374 from attractions**, but the report still showed
**-$405 profit** because I had spent **$766 on buildings**. Satisfaction was
100%, the park was operating, and the opening construction simply cost more
than one small day could repay.

Much later, a day with **103 guests** generated **$3,206 from tickets**,
**$6,260 from attractions** and **$2,819 in tips**. Its Daily Profit was still
**-$463** after **$5,431 in new construction**, **$2,015 in staff salaries**,
maintenance, fines and refunds.

I therefore separate temporary building expenses from recurring operating
costs. I make money by stabilising one working section, improving attractions
guests already use and expanding only when the current service route can afford
the next area.

> Tested in Waterpark Simulator 1.0 on a new Hard Mode Beach Map save through
> four stars. Building prices, wages and operating pressure may differ on Normal
> difficulty or after later balance changes.

<h2 id="read-daily-report">Why Daily Profit Is Negative Even When the Park Earns Money</h2>

I begin with the income and expense columns, not the final Profit number.

The income side tells me whether the park is functioning:

- **Tickets** show whether guests are entering and being processed;
- **Attractions** show whether pools, slides and other paid facilities are being
  used;
- **Tips** show how much additional value the park is receiving from
  Satisfaction, reviews and decoration.

The expense side tells me where the money went:

- **Maintenance** repeats while the facility remains active;
- **Staff Salary** repeats while the worker remains employed;
- **Staff Overflow tax** repeats while I stay above the normal limit;
- **Ticket Refunds** and fines usually point to service or safety failures;
- **Buildings** are purchases I chose to make that day.

For practical decisions, I read the report as two separate results:

**Operating result:** income minus wages, maintenance, tax, fines and refunds.

**Expansion result:** the operating result after subtracting that day's
building purchases.

This distinction prevents me from deleting a profitable attraction simply
because I bought several new buildings on the same day.

Before calling a day unprofitable, I ask:

1. Would it have been positive without new construction?
2. Are salaries, maintenance, tax, fines and refunds consuming the income?
3. Is there enough cash to pay workers and restock before reopening?

A one-day construction loss is not automatically dangerous. A repeated
no-build loss is.

If the park loses money on a day where I build nothing, I stop expanding and
check:

- attractions that are empty, broken or closed;
- guests waiting outside the entrance;
- food and vending machines without stock;
- employees without enough useful work;
- recurring Staff Overflow tax;
- repeated refunds and fines.

<h2 id="early-game-money">My Early-Game Money Route</h2>

The opening park does not need a full staff team or a large themed section.

It needs one compact route I can operate without crossing the map for every
ticket, hot dog, puddle or repair.

My first reliable route keeps these close together:

1. ticket entrance;
2. food and drink;
3. toilets and showers;
4. the first pools and slides;
5. trash bins and loungers;
6. the shop and Research Table route.

I handle tickets, food, cleaning and repairs myself while the park is small.
This keeps salaries out of the first reports and reveals which job becomes the
first real bottleneck.

### I do not hire only because a role is unlocked

One early Ticket Worker candidate cost about **$270 per day**. That was too
expensive for a park that sometimes ended with only a few hundred dollars.

I waited because I could still clear the entrance queue myself.

I hire the first worker when:

- the same job interrupts income several times per day;
- a normal operating day can cover the salary;
- the worker frees me to protect or create more income elsewhere.

A Ticket Worker is worth paying when guests remain outside while I am serving
food or repairing attractions. It is not worth paying when I can still clear
the queue before demand builds.

### I keep an operating reserve

I do not spend the last dollars on decoration, another pool or a themed
facility.

Before opening, I keep enough money for:

- current staff salaries;
- hot dogs and lemonade supplies;
- snacks, drinks and inflatables;
- Chlorine when dirty water is already common;
- one emergency restock trip.

A new attraction cannot repay its cost if I have no money left to fill its pool
or support the services around it.

<h2 id="upgrade-existing-attractions">What I Buy First: Upgrade an Attraction That Already Earns</h2>

The first useful pool upgrade doubled the guest price from **$2 to $4** and
added **three visitors** to maximum capacity.

It also improved hygiene, durability, safety and Fun, although daily
maintenance increased.

This is the investment I prefer early:

- the guest path already reaches it;
- showers, toilets and bins already exist nearby;
- I know guests use the attraction;
- staff do not need to travel into another section;
- the higher price can begin earning immediately.

I delay a remote pool when it also requires:

- a new path;
- another shower and toilet area;
- trash coverage;
- food or vending support;
- a longer cleaning and repair route;
- another employee;
- enough decoration to make the section usable.

I considered placing an Ocean pool and slide at the far edge of an expansion,
then turned back because the active park could not support the distance yet.

An attraction being unlocked does not mean the park is financially ready to
operate it.

### I replace one section at a time

Destroying an older facility returns some money, but I did not receive the full
original purchase price.

I replace facilities in this order:

1. upgrade one active pool, slide or service;
2. run one ordinary day;
3. check attraction income and maintenance;
4. replace the next item only after the cash balance recovers.

The same rule applies to comfort upgrades. Resort Sunbeds reached **$825 each**
in this Hard Mode save. Replacing twenty loungers together would create a large
construction loss without proving that the upgrade solved the current
bottleneck.

<h2 id="tickets-tips-and-marketing">How I Increase Tickets, Attractions and Tips</h2>

Ticket-price Research is useful because it increases the value of guests who
successfully enter.

It does not help while those guests are still waiting outside.

Before spending Research Points on ticket income or traffic, I check:

- the entrance can clear the opening queue;
- the Ticket Worker remains available through the busy period;
- pools are filled and repaired when guests enter;
- food and vending machines have stock;
- Satisfaction is stable enough to protect tips.

Attraction income needs the same operating discipline.

A paid pool or slide earns only while it is:

- accessible;
- filled with water;
- connected correctly;
- repaired;
- clean enough for guests to keep using it.

### Tips are extra income, not the first repair

The opening park earned **$0 in tips** before Tipping was unlocked.

The mature report later showed **$2,819 in tips**, but that growth happened
alongside better services, more attractions, decoration and higher
Satisfaction.

I do not read that result as “buy decoration first.”

I stabilise tickets, pools, food and cleanliness first, then decorate the areas
where guests already spend time.

### When I use Poster Marketing

Poster Marketing increases guest traffic.

I place posters only after the current park can process more visitors.

<h2 id="vending-machines">When a Vending Machine Is Better Than Another Employee</h2>

Vending machines sell without requiring me or a Vendor to remain at a counter.

They are not passive forever.

A machine still requires:

- its Research unlock;
- the purchase price;
- initial stock;
- repeated restocking;
- nearby trash coverage;
- enough demand to justify the investment.

I buy one when I repeatedly leave a higher-value job to serve a small queue.

Examples include:

- leaving tickets to hand out snacks;
- abandoning pool repairs to serve lemonade;
- interrupting a Museum objective for one food stand;
- letting guests wait because two manual counters are active together.

### I include the first stock in the purchase price

My first Inflatable Vending Machine was a useful warning.

I placed the machine, then discovered I did not have enough cash to stock it
immediately.

A machine without stock is only a construction expense.

I now buy in this order:

1. save the machine price;
2. keep additional cash for its first stock;
3. place it on an existing high-traffic route;
4. add a nearby trash bin;
5. watch one full operating day;
6. add capacity only if the first machine repeatedly sells out.

Later, Inflatable demand became high enough that the machine was difficult to
keep stocked.

### When I consider a Restocker

I do not hire a Restocker for one lightly used machine.

I consider one when:

- several vending machines are active;
- stock runs out during most operating days;
- restock trips interrupt tickets, safety or paid attractions;
- the salary and any extra tax are lower than the income protected.

Until then, I restock before opening and schedule one shop run during a quieter
period.

<h2 id="staff-costs">How Much Staff My Park Can Actually Afford</h2>

Staff are worth paying when they protect more income than they consume.

I do not ask only whether a worker performs a task. I ask whether that task was
already manageable without them.

### I calculate the complete daily cost

The cost of another employee can include:

- salary;
- Staff Overflow tax;
- breaks that still leave a station uncovered;
- extra demand created by the expansion they support.

Staff Overflow let this park hire above the normal limit, up to 200% of that
limit. Going above the cap added an end-of-day tax.

In one early setup with two Janitors, the expected tax was about **$215**.

I accept the extra cost when the worker prevents something more expensive:

- trash and puddles reducing Satisfaction;
- broken attractions losing operating time;
- queues causing refunds or departures;
- the player being trapped in a low-value task;
- a profitable remote section remaining unsupported.

I avoid it when:

- the worker has no consistent assignment;
- I am hiring for a future expansion that is not open;
- the park cannot yet cover normal salaries;
- restocking or a better layout would solve the problem.

A developed six-worker roster used:

- two Janitors;
- two Vendors;
- one Ticket Worker;
- one Maintenance Worker.

The larger park could support that roster because ticket, attraction, vending
and tip income had scaled with it.

<h2 id="mafia-loan">Should I Take the Mafia Loan?</h2>

A Mafioso offered this park **$750** when cash was tight. I could accept or
reject the offer.

I treat it as emergency cash, not normal income.

I consider accepting only when:

- the existing park already earns money on ordinary days;
- the shortage is temporary;
- the cash will reopen a working pool, restock an active seller or prevent a
  service shutdown;
- I am not using it to support permanent salary costs.

I reject or postpone it when:

- the park loses money even on no-build days;
- I want the cash for decoration or a remote themed section;
- the money would only delay an unavoidable staff cut;
- I have not verified the repayment or task consequences in the current save.

I have not confirmed a universal interest rate or exact repayment formula, so I
do not build the money route around assumptions about the loan terms.

The safe test is the same as any other cash injection: use it only when a
working park can repay the decision through normal operation.

<h2 id="construction-loss-vs-operating-loss">How I Separate a Construction Loss from a Bad Business Day</h2>

The mature Daily Report made the distinction clear.

The park recorded:

- **103 guests**;
- **94% Satisfaction**;
- **$3,206 from tickets**;
- **$6,260 from attractions**;
- **$2,819 in tips**.

The same report included:

- **$5,431 in building expenses**;
- **$2,015 in staff salaries**;
- **$1,160 in maintenance**;
- **$520 in fines**;
- **$103 in ticket refunds**;
- a displayed result of **-$463**.

I treat this as an expansion day, not proof that the park cannot earn.

My next step is a no-build test:

1. buy no new facilities;
2. keep traffic close to the previous day;
3. restock normally;
4. keep the same staff roster;
5. compare the next report.

If the next report improves when Buildings falls to zero, the expansion is
probably sustainable.

If it remains negative, I investigate recurring costs:

- too many employees;
- repeated Staff Overflow tax;
- high-maintenance facilities that are rarely used;
- fines and injuries;
- ticket refunds;
- poor Satisfaction reducing tips;
- empty or broken attractions;
- stock and service failures.

This one test day is more useful than immediately destroying the facilities I
just purchased.

<h2 id="when-to-expand">My Buy, Wait or Turn Back Rules</h2>

I expand only after the active park passes an operating test.

### I buy Park Size or another pool when

- cash remains after salaries and restocking;
- the entrance clears the normal queue;
- food and Inflatable stock survives the day;
- active pools remain filled and clean;
- staff can reach the current outer edge;
- the current capacity is genuinely being used;
- the new area has an immediate purpose.

In this Hard Mode save, **Park Size Level 3 cost $2,000**.

That was only the land-related purchase. A working section still needed a pool,
slide, showers, bins, paths, loungers and staff coverage.

### I wait when

- a current attraction is empty, dirty or broken;
- the park loses money on no-build days;
- the purchase would remove the restock reserve;
- Staff Overflow is already difficult to afford;
- the new section would sit empty;
- the current star objective requires something else.

### I turn back when the complete section is unaffordable

I abandon the build for now when:

- the attraction is too far from the staff route;
- I cannot afford its supporting services;
- the area needs another employee immediately;
- current pools still have unused capacity;
- I am buying only because the theme looks better.

Turning back is not wasted progress. It prevents one unlocked attraction from
creating several recurring costs before it can produce reliable revenue.
