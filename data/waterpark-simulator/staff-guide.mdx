I do not use one fixed hiring order in Waterpark Simulator.

My first employee depends on the job that is already pulling me away from the
rest of the park.

A Ticket Worker makes sense when I keep abandoning food, cleaning and repairs
to clear the entrance.

A Janitor is better when trash, puddles and dirty facilities are spreading
faster than I can reach them.

Maintenance becomes valuable when several paid attractions are breaking during
the same operating day.

Later, Vendors, Restockers, Security and an Entertainer become useful for very
different reasons.

The most important staffing rule I learned is that **unlocking a staff role and
increasing the number of employees I can hire are separate problems**.

I can unlock Security, a Vendor or another specialist and still have no free
employee slot.

In the middle of my Hard Mode Beach run, one practical six-worker core was:

- two Janitors;
- two Vendors;
- one Ticket Worker;
- one Maintenance Worker.

That was not my final roster and it is not a universal best build.

Later I also tested an **Entertainer/Mascot** and **Security** as the park became
much larger.

The right roster changed with the park.

<h2 id="who-to-hire-first">Who Should I Hire First?</h2>

<AdUnit placement="article_after_first_h2" />

I wait for the park to expose a real bottleneck.

Hiring too early is dangerous on Hard Mode.

When Cashier Staff first became available, one Ticket Worker candidate cost
about **$270 per day**, while my cash balance had fallen to only **$63**.

The role itself was useful.

The problem was that paying the worker would have left almost nothing for stock
or the next operating day.

I came back later with roughly **$300**, hired the worker and started the next
day with only **$9**.

That hire worked because the entrance had already become a repeated
interruption.

I still would not treat $300 as a comfortable reserve for a $270-per-day worker.

### I hire a Ticket Worker first when

- I leave food, cleaning or repairs several times per day to sell tickets;
- the queue remains after the opening rush;
- guests wait outside while I am on the far side of the park;
- the park can still afford tomorrow's operating costs after paying the wage;
- freeing the entrance lets me protect more valuable work elsewhere.

### I hire a Janitor first when

- trash is visible across several guest routes;
- full bins and puddles appear faster than I can clear them;
- dirty-facility complaints are repeating;
- cleaning is failing while I can still repair the attractions myself;
- new sections have created a route too long for one person to patrol.

### I hire Maintenance first when

- several attractions become damaged during the same day;
- breakdowns are reducing paid-attraction uptime;
- I repeatedly leave tickets or food just to use the hammer;
- a distant section sits outside my reliable repair route;
- one major failure can remove a large part of current park capacity.

The first hire is therefore not:

> Which worker is best?

It is:

> Which job am I already failing to complete?

<h2 id="how-staff-stats-work">
  How I Compare Staff Stats, Recovery Time and Pay
</h2>

The Hiring List gives me more information than the wage.

I compare:

- **Efficiency**;
- **Precision**;
- **Movement Speed**;
- **Endurance**;
- **Recovery Time**;
- **Pay**.

I do not automatically choose the largest Efficiency number.

The best stat depends on what that employee actually does all day.

### Efficiency

Efficiency matters most when two candidates perform the same repetitive job.

My first Ticket Worker had close to **200% Efficiency**.

That was enough to make the entrance move quickly while the worker was active.

The bigger problem was not processing speed.

It was what happened when the employee became tired and stopped working.

### Movement Speed

Movement Speed matters much more for employees who travel:

- Janitors;
- Maintenance Workers;
- Restockers;
- workers crossing several distant park sections.

A Ticket Worker spends most of the day around the entrance.

A Vendor can spend most of the day at one assigned stall.

For those roles, I care less about travelling speed than I do for a Janitor
running from the Beach entrance to a remote themed area.

### Endurance and Recovery Time

I pay close attention to this pair.

Endurance affects how long the worker can stay active.

Recovery Time determines how long that job disappears when they rest.

For one later Janitor choice, I compared:

- **154% Efficiency**, Movement Speed 4, Endurance 150 and a **69-second
  Recovery Time**;
- **134% Efficiency**, Movement Speed 3, Endurance 198 and a **39-second
  Recovery Time**.

The second candidate looked worse if I compared Efficiency alone.

But they had much stronger Endurance and almost half the Recovery Time.

For a long operating period, that can be more useful than winning one headline
stat.

### Precision

I use Precision as a secondary comparison rather than the first deciding
number.

I have not isolated a reliable universal formula showing exactly how much one
extra point of Precision changes every staff role.

I therefore prioritise the effects I can clearly see:

- work speed;
- walking time;
- stamina;
- downtime;
- salary.

### Pay

I compare the wage to the bottleneck the worker removes.

A cheap employee is poor value when they spend most of the day:

- walking;
- resting;
- standing without an assignment;
- waiting for a job that barely exists.

A more expensive worker can still be worth it when they keep guests entering
or prevent several paid attractions from sitting broken.

<h2 id="ticket-worker">When a Ticket Worker Is Worth the Salary</h2>

My first Ticket Worker removed one of the most repetitive opening jobs.

After hiring, my normal route became:

1. check the ticket booth;
2. confirm the employee has arrived;
3. open the park;
4. leave the entrance;
5. restock food;
6. inspect pools and slides;
7. return only if the worker rests or the queue becomes abnormal.

That does **not** make ticketing completely automatic.

Employees take breaks.

When the Ticket Worker rests, ticket sales can stop long enough for another
queue to form.

### When I cover the booth myself

I return when:

- the Ticket Worker is resting;
- a visible queue has already formed;
- an objective requires me to sell tickets personally;
- one short rush is close to causing departures;
- a newly hired worker has not started yet.

I do not hire a second Ticket Worker because one employee took one normal break.

I first see whether I can cover the short gap myself.

### A new hire may not appear immediately

I saw this with a Maintenance hire.

The worker did not appear immediately after I completed the hire late in the
day.

My check was:

1. open Manage Staff;
2. confirm the person is still listed as hired;
3. move to the next day;
4. check the normal work area again.

The employee appeared after the day changed.

So if a worker does not immediately spawn, I do not fire and rehire them before
checking the next operating day.

<h2 id="janitor-or-maintenance">
  Janitor or Maintenance: Which One Do I Need?
</h2>

This was the hardest early staffing tradeoff because both jobs solved real
problems.

I chose a Janitor when cleaning had become the work I could no longer finish
while repairs were still manageable with my hammer.

That decision changed as the park expanded.

When I later removed a Maintenance Worker to save the wage, damaged
attractions started appearing alongside dirty pools, tickets, food and trash.

The salary disappeared.

So did the repair coverage.

### I choose a Janitor when the failure is spread everywhere

Trash and puddles can appear around:

- the entrance;
- food and vending;
- toilets and showers;
- pool edges;
- loungers;
- paths;
- remote themed sections.

A Janitor removes the constant need to stop for every small cleaning job while I
am travelling between larger problems.

My first Janitor candidate had roughly **159% Efficiency** and was also one of
the cheaper available choices.

### I choose Maintenance when downtime is more expensive than dirt

Maintenance becomes the stronger hire when:

- several slides and pools wear down together;
- the park contains distant attractions;
- repairs repeatedly interrupt ticketing or food;
- broken facilities are causing complaints or lost capacity;
- one high-use attraction staying offline matters more than a few pieces of
  trash.

In one candidate comparison, the faster Maintenance worker had:

- 92% Efficiency;
- Movement Speed 7;
- Endurance 157;
- 60-second Recovery Time.

The other had:

- 172% Efficiency;
- Movement Speed 3;
- Endurance 198;
- 111-second Recovery Time.

On a wide Beach Map, Movement Speed and the shorter Recovery Time can make the
first candidate more attractive than the Efficiency number suggests.

Inside a compact park, I may prefer the worker who performs the actual repair
faster.

### When I add the second Janitor

I add another cleaner when:

- the first Janitor is resting while trash is still spreading;
- the park has split into several active areas;
- weather creates puddles over a large route;
- bins remain full through much of the day;
- cleaning complaints continue despite the first worker;
- I am still spending a large part of my own operating day cleaning.

There is one downside.

Good Janitors can also remove the puddles and cleaning actions I still need for
personal Research or quest counters.

<h2 id="vendors-and-restocker">
  When Vendors and a Restocker Become Worth It
</h2>

A Vendor does not automatically operate every food location.

Each worker needs a real job.

I delayed my first Vendor while:

- ticketing was a larger bottleneck;
- I could still serve food during quieter periods;
- employee capacity was tight;
- the slot had more value as a cleaner or Ticket Worker;
- the park could not comfortably support another wage.

I added Vendors once multiple food stalls had proven their demand.

The split I used at one stage was:

- Vendor 1 on Lemonade;
- Vendor 2 on Popcorn;
- I handled Hot Dogs and emergencies.

### How I assign a Vendor

When a Vendor appears `Not Assigned`, I check:

1. the employee is still hired;
2. they are not resting;
3. the target stall has an assignment control;
4. the correct Vendor is selected;
5. the stall actually has stock.

### When I hire a Restocker

One vending machine does not justify another daily wage.

I hire a Restocker when:

- several vending machines are active;
- food, drink or Inflatable stock empties regularly;
- supply runs interrupt ticketing, safety or paid attractions;
- the larger park makes each restocking route too long;
- the role will protect enough sales to justify its recurring cost.

In my larger park, Inflatable demand became difficult to keep stocked while
other employees were also resting and guests were waiting at food stalls.

<h2 id="security-and-mascot">
  When I Hire Security, an Entertainer or a Mascot
</h2>

These are later staffing decisions in my park.

I do not replace essential ticketing, cleaning, repair or food coverage with
Security or an Entertainer just because the Research node has finally appeared.

### When Security became worth considering

By the late game, disruptive guests and vandalism had become another layer of
work.

Graffiti matters because somebody still has to clean it.

If my Janitors are spending time on vandalism while bins, puddles and normal
cleaning are also piling up, the real cost of a disruptive guest is not just one
annoying interaction.

It can consume the cleaner's time too.

That is when Security begins competing with another Janitor for the next staff
slot.

I do **not** assume that hiring one guard completely eliminates every case of
graffiti.

I judge it from the actual workload.

I add Security when:

- disruptive guests are appearing repeatedly;
- vandalism creates additional cleanup;
- I am personally chasing troublemakers instead of managing the park;
- Janitors already have enough normal cleaning work;
- the roster has room for a specialist role.

### Security required 5 Research Points in my late save

One useful late-game check surprised me.

I was already deep into the developed Beach park and assumed I had Security
available.

I checked the Research Tree and found that I still needed **5 Research Points**
for the Security unlock.

That is another example of why I do not plan the roster from job names alone.

I need:

1. the role unlocked;
2. a free employee slot;
3. a candidate for that role;
4. enough cash for the recurring wage.

If any one of those is missing, “I need Security” does not immediately produce a
Security Guard.

### Entertainer and Mascot are effectively the same late-game staffing search

This terminology can be confusing.

In my Hiring List, the role I was looking for was **Entertainer**.

After I refreshed the candidates, I hired **Libby** as the Entertainer.

When Libby actually appeared in the park, the employee was dressed as a
**duck mascot**.

So when I am looking for this system, I treat:

- Entertainer;
- Mascot;
- Mascot Training;

as connected parts of the same late-game staff branch rather than assuming I
need to find a separate employee called “Mascot” in the Hiring List.

### When the Entertainer is worth a slot

I do not hire an Entertainer before:

- the entrance is covered;
- cleaning is stable;
- major attractions have repair coverage;
- proven food queues have Vendors;
- vending demand has a workable restock route;
- the park has an available slot;
- another salary is no longer dangerous.

In the late park where I hired Libby, the staff situation was already completely
different from the opening game.

I was adding specialist employees rather than trying to keep one tiny pool and
ticket booth alive.

That is when an Entertainer makes sense to test.

I have **not** isolated a clean universal number showing exactly how much one
Entertainer increases Satisfaction, income or Prestige.

So I do not write a rule such as:

> Hire a Mascot at four stars because it adds X% Satisfaction.

I have not verified that.

My rule is simply:

**core operations first, specialist entertainment later.**

### If the Entertainer does not appear in the Hiring List

I do not assume the role is broken.

In my late save, I wanted an Entertainer but the current candidates did not
include one.

I waited until I could refresh the Hiring List.

After refreshing, Libby appeared and I hired her.

That means the immediate checklist is:

1. confirm the relevant staff system is unlocked;
2. confirm I have a free slot;
3. check the current candidate list;
4. refresh when available;
5. look again before assuming the role cannot be hired.

<h2 id="staff-limit">Why I Cannot Hire More Employees</h2>

Unlocking another role does **not** increase the number of employees I can have.

This is one of the easiest staffing traps to hit.

The Research Tree separates:

- staff-role unlocks;
- **Hiring Level Up**;
- **Staff Overflow**.

Those solve different problems.

### Hiring Level Up

Hiring Level Up increases the normal staff cap.

In my larger park, one upgrade cost **6 Research Points** and opened room for
**two additional employees**.

That was valuable because I already knew exactly what I wanted to do with the
new slots.

I prioritise another Hiring Level when:

- the current roster is full;
- two real jobs are already uncovered;
- I am repeatedly paying Staff Overflow tax;
- the mature park can support the additional wages.

I do not spend 6 Research Points on more capacity simply because the node exists.

Empty staff capacity produces nothing by itself.

### Staff Overflow

Staff Overflow lets me exceed the normal employee limit up to **200% of the
current maximum**, but the extra headcount comes with an end-of-day tax.

One earlier over-limit setup showed an expected tax of roughly **$215**.

<h2 id="employees-not-working">
  Why an Employee Is Not Working
</h2>

When a worker looks idle, I do not immediately replace them.

I check the cause in a fixed order.

### The employee is resting

Staff stop doing their normal job while on break.

When that happens, I temporarily cover whichever failure matters most.

My order is:

1. drowning or injured guest;
2. blocked entrance;
3. serious food queue;
4. broken high-use attraction;
5. trash and puddles;
6. low-priority restocking.

I do not permanently hire another employee to solve one normal break unless the
same downtime keeps causing failures every day.

### The employee has no assignment

This is especially important for Vendors.

If a Vendor is hired but not assigned to the intended stand, they cannot solve
that food queue.

I check the station itself and assign the worker.

### The employee starts next day

A late hire may not immediately appear.

I confirm the hire in Manage Staff, move to the next day and check again.

### The staff cap is full

This is different from the role being locked.

If the staff role is unlocked but I have no slot, I need:

- Hiring Level Up;
- or Staff Overflow.

Unlocking another employee type will not fix the capacity problem.

### The employee I want is missing from the candidate list

This happened with my Entertainer.

The current candidates did not include that role.

I refreshed the Hiring List when I had the ability to do so and then found the
Entertainer I wanted.

I do not confuse:

> no candidate right now

with:

> the staff role does not exist.

### The worker spends too long travelling

A Janitor, Maintenance Worker or Restocker can technically be working while
still accomplishing very little.

The problem may be the layout.

I respond by:

- preferring better Movement Speed for wide routes;
- delaying another remote expansion;
- splitting a large park between two workers;
- grouping related facilities;
- building complete service areas rather than isolated attractions.

If one Restocker has to cross the entire Beach Map for every vending machine,
hiring another worker may help.

Moving the machines onto a better route may help more.

### The station has no stock

A Vendor cannot sell an item the stand does not have.

Before I blame the employee, I check the inventory.

This is especially important during Night Shift or other long operating periods,
where a station that worked earlier in the day may simply have run empty.

<h2 id="staff-checklist">My Checklist Before Hiring or Firing</h2>

Before hiring anyone, I ask:

- Which repeated job is currently failing?
- Is the failure caused by workload, distance, rest or a missing assignment?
- Can the park afford the wage and still open normally tomorrow?
- Is there a free normal employee slot?
- Would Staff Overflow add another recurring tax?
- Does this role have a real station or route today?
- Is the candidate's Movement Speed relevant to the size of the park?
- Is a long Recovery Time going to leave an important job uncovered?
- Will this employee remove manual actions I still need for a quest or Research?
- Would a better layout solve the same problem without another salary?

Before firing someone, I run an ordinary operating day first.

Removing Maintenance once saved the wage but immediately returned every repair
to me during an already busy day.

That changed how I think about “idle” employees.

I no longer ask whether a worker looked busy during one quiet minute.

I ask whether their job repeatedly disappears **without creating a new problem
for me**.
