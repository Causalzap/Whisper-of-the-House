<h2 id="no-s-tier">
  Why are there no S-tier Tetramon cards?
</h2>

The current distribution is unusual for a tier list:

**0 S-tier, 14 A-tier, 99 B-tier, and 8 C-tier cards.**

I would rather leave S empty than lower the bar simply because a tier list is
expected to have something at the top. S begins at **82 points**, while the
current leader stops at 77.

That gap is useful information. None of the 121 cards combines Play Effect,
reliability, flexibility, synergy, evolution accessibility, and printed stats
strongly enough to clear every part of the test at once. The top cards still
have tradeoffs.

Toximuck is a good example again. The effect is exceptional, but its overall
score does not follow the Effect Value all the way into the 90s because the rest
of the card still matters. Crablox reaches third place by a very different
route: its **94 Reliability**, **75 Synergy**, and **88 Evolution
Accessibility** compensate for an Effect Value of only **59.16**.

So I use A tier as the current high-end shortlist rather than treating an empty
S tier as something that needs fixing.

<h2 id="how-rankings-work">
  What actually raises or lowers a card's rank?
</h2>

Six parts of the card feed into the overall score.

**Play Effect carries the most weight at 28%.** The actual magnitude matters, so
Draw 4 is not treated the same as Draw 1, and forcing four discards is not
treated the same as forcing one. Damage bonuses, shields, drain, field
destruction, evolution locks, damage negation, and similar effects are measured
the same way when their card text provides a number.

**Reliability is 18%.** I want to know how often the card can realistically
contribute rather than how impressive it looks after the perfect setup.
Evolution requirements, draw, filtering, and conditional effects can all move
this number.

**Flexibility, Synergy, and Evolution Setup vs Payoff are 14% each.** These
three are why cards with similar raw effects can land several points apart. A
card that works in several plans has an advantage over one that needs a very
specific board. An evolution card can still score highly when the reward is
large enough to justify the extra pieces needed to reach it.

The remaining **12% comes from printed stats**. I compare both absolute stats
and performance relative to cards in the same class, so a strong Basic card can
receive credit without making every Legendary card automatically better just
because its numbers start higher.

That mix is also why I would not choose a deck by sorting the list from highest
to lowest and adding the first 50 names.

<h2 id="rarity-vs-tier">
  Does Legendary or Epic automatically mean the card is better?
</h2>

No. **Basic, Rare, Epic, and Legendary are card classes, not a ready-made power
ranking.**

The 121-card pool currently contains **27 Basic, 34 Rare, 34 Epic, and 26
Legendary cards**. Some higher-class cards need an earlier evolution, some cards
can begin an evolution route themselves, and others are usable without a
previous form.

That distinction matters more to me than the label at the bottom of the card.
A powerful Epic that stays in my hand waiting for the correct board can be less
useful in that match than a modest card I can deploy immediately.

The reverse is also true. I do not punish an evolution card merely because it
takes setup. When the eventual effect and stats compensate for that cost, the
card can still climb into A tier.

For the actual question of whether a particular evolution line is supported
well enough inside 50 cards, I move over to the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/deck-builder">Tetramon deck builder</Link>.
That is where missing and thin earlier stages become much easier to spot.

<h2 id="c-tier-cards">
  Should I remove every C-tier card from my deck?
</h2>

C tier is not a ban list.

The lowest current score is **Bonfiox at 55**, followed by several cards at 56
or 57. Those scores tell me that the individual card gives up more in areas such
as effect strength, flexibility, setup, or stats than the cards near the top of
the ranking.

A deck can still have a reason to use one.

Evolution is the most obvious case. A card can be weaker when judged alone but
still be part of the route that gets a stronger later form onto the table.
Removing every low-scoring intermediate piece because its individual score is
lower can leave the cards I actually wanted to play stranded in my hand.

That is the distinction I keep in mind while using the filters:

**Tier answers which individual cards look strongest on their own profile.  
The deck decides whether that card has a job.**

<h2 id="choose-by-effect">
  How do I choose between cards in the same tier?
</h2>

Once several cards land in the same letter tier, I stop using the letter as the
decision.

A damage-focused deck sends me toward damage boosts, double damage, shield
pressure, and unblockable TP effects. A control plan gives much more value to
opponent discard, destruction, evolution locks, and attack disruption. A deck
that keeps missing its pieces makes Draw and filtering more attractive even
when another card has a slightly higher overall score.

The filters above are built around that decision. I can narrow the 121 cards by
**Fire, Water, Earth, or Wind**, then by class and Play Effect instead of
scrolling through the whole binder every time.

For close calls, I expand **Why This Score?**. A 72-point card with very high
stats and modest flexibility is solving a different problem from another
72-point card whose score comes from a much stronger effect. The overall number
gets me to the shortlist; the breakdown tells me which one fits the hole in my
deck.

<h2 id="build-with-card">
  What do I do after choosing a high-tier card?
</h2>

I take one card as the core, then build outward rather than adding every other
A-tier card beside it.

First I check whether it needs an earlier evolution. Then I add enough access to
that route, choose effects that support the same plan, and only after that do I
finish the remaining slots. The final list still has to contain exactly
**50 cards** with no more than **4 copies of the same card name**.

That last step is where an attractive binder ranking either turns into a usable
deck or falls apart. Use **Build a Deck With This Card** in the ranking above,
then finish the list in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/deck-builder">Tetramon deck builder</Link> while watching the evolution and consistency checks update.
