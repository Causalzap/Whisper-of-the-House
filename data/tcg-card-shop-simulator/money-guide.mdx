<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Keep proven sellers stocked, price ordinary merchandise modestly above market, and treat replacement stock as the first claim on every sale. Open packs only after the shelf has enough inventory for customers, and use expensive single-card sales as extra capital rather than money the shop depends on receiving that day.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="early-money-loop">
  How do I make money without running the shop out of stock?
</h2>

The useful number in the opening shop is not simply the cash currently showing on screen. I want to know how much of that balance is still mine to spend **after replacing what customers just bought**.

That leads to a simple operating rhythm:

**sell → replace proven stock → keep a small reserve → spend the surplus.**

Following that order keeps successful sales from creating their own problem. If packs are moving quickly, the money from those packs first keeps the same shelf alive. A new license, expansion, or piece of equipment comes from what remains.

The alternative looks fine for a few minutes: buy the newest unlock, watch the balance fall, then discover that the product earning most of the money is nearly gone. The shop is larger, but the next customer has less to buy.

<h2 id="pricing">
  How far above market price should I sell products?
</h2>

I have seen two workable early approaches. One shop started around **10% above market price** with the final number rounded for easier checkout. Another fresh 1.0 run pushed ordinary merchandise to roughly **market price +20%**, also rounded, and customers were still buying.

That gives me a range to test rather than a permanent markup.

Fast-moving stock gives me room to test a little higher. Repeated complaints or a product that sits untouched while nearby shelves empty are signs that the extra margin may be costing too much turnover.

Rounding helps during manual checkout as well. Clean totals mean less time dealing with awkward coins while customers are queueing, which matters more in a small shop where I am still running the register myself.

The goal is not the largest possible markup. It is the price where **margin remains worthwhile without making a proven seller noticeably harder to move**.

<h2 id="stock-turnover">
  A single customer can empty far more stock than expected
</h2>

One customer bought **33 card packs in a single transaction**. At **$6 each**, the checkout came to **$198**; the customer paid $200 and received $2 back.

That was a great sale, but it also exposed how fragile a shallow shelf can be.

Once a product proves that customers buy it consistently, I keep more than one normal transaction's worth available. Otherwise one unusually large basket forces an emergency stock order while the next customers are already walking through the door.

This matters most for merchandise that sells in multiples. Deep stock on a reliable product buys me time to handle the register, check new unlocks, open cards, or reorganize the store without constantly abandoning the task because one shelf has gone bare.

<h2 id="licenses">
  A new license needs money for stock after the unlock
</h2>

The amount printed on a license is not the full cost of adding that product to the business.

When the Epic Pack license became available in one run, I had roughly **$500 of excess cash**. The unlock was within reach, but Epic packs still had to be purchased afterward, and the existing shelves still needed their normal stock the next morning.

That makes the practical cost:

**license + first shipment + enough stock for everything already earning money.**

If those three pieces cannot fit into the balance together, another trading day usually leaves the store in a stronger position than forcing the license immediately.

Which unlock deserves priority is a progression question, so I use the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">progression guide</Link> for that decision. From the money side, the license is ready when buying it does not leave the new product—or the old shop—without inventory.

<h2 id="single-cards">
  Expensive single cards are powerful but unpredictable income
</h2>

Individual cards can produce a cash jump that ordinary merchandise rarely matches.

One card around the **$600 range** sat on display while several customers passed it by. Eventually somebody bought it, producing a **$682 sale** and **+10 XP**.

That is why I treat expensive singles as acceleration rather than baseline income. Once the sale happens, the money can fund a license, stock order, or equipment much faster than several small transactions. Until it happens, that value is still sitting on the table.

A store whose next restock depends on one particular card selling before close is relying on the wrong income stream.

The calculation also changes after the level 7 Workbench becomes available. A duplicate Tetramon card may be useful in a playable deck, so I check its deck value before automatically putting it up for sale. The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tetramon-duel-master">Tetramon Duel Master guide</Link> covers the actual deck rules; economically, the important point is simply that duplicates can now have another use besides cash.

<h2 id="opening-packs">
  Sell the packs the shelf needs; open the surplus
</h2>

Opening packs is attractive because one action can give collection progress, shop XP, useful Tetramon cards, and a chance at a valuable single. The tradeoff is that the sealed pack had a predictable retail value before I opened it.

I use available shelf stock as the dividing line.

When the next customer wave is already covered, extra packs are much easier to open. If the display itself is running thin, tearing through the remaining boxes converts dependable merchandise into uncertain card value at exactly the wrong time.

That distinction becomes less strict later. Large collection targets, pack-opening achievements, missing evolution pieces, and rare cards all create reasons to open more product. During the opening economy, though, I want the retail side funded before gambling on what is inside the wrapper.

This is also why I would not rank packs purely from a handful of valuable pulls. A pack can be good for collection progress, deck building, rare-card hunting, or straightforward retail sales, and those are not the same economic question.

<h2 id="product-mix">
  Add another product line when you can keep it on the shelf
</h2>

Income became noticeably steadier once the store was no longer depending on only one or two categories.

Deck boxes were a useful example. After they reached the shelf, customers bought them strongly enough that they became another meaningful revenue source instead of decorative inventory.

That is the kind of diversification I want. Packs, deck boxes, sleeves, dice, decks, and other merchandise spread demand across the store, so one slow category does not determine the entire day.

The useful limit is stock depth. Adding several licenses at once while each category stays half empty does not create a healthier shop. I would rather support a smaller range consistently, then add the next product once its first shipment and restock no longer compete with the shelves already making money.

<h2 id="play-table-income">
  Let play tables supplement a healthy retail floor
</h2>

Customers playing games generate money while I am working elsewhere, which makes play space more attractive as the store grows.

Early on, merchandise is still easier to control. I set the price, see exactly what sold, and replace the stock when demand is obvious. Tables also consume floor space that may still be needed for profitable displays.

Once the store has more room, that trade becomes much easier to justify. Table income can run in parallel while I handle merchandise, cards, or other systems, instead of being asked to rescue an understocked retail operation.

<h2 id="money-stuck">
  If customers keep buying but the balance stays low, find where the cash is disappearing
</h2>

A busy shop can still be financially stuck. The symptoms usually point to the problem.

**Shelves repeatedly empty:** the store needs a deeper inventory reserve before another major purchase.

**A license is unlocked but its product never reaches the shelf:** the budget covered the unlock and not the first shipment.

**Most expected income is sitting on expensive single cards:** too much depends on an unpredictable buyer.

**Retail packs are running out while I keep opening boxes myself:** known selling value is being turned into random card value too early.

**There are many product categories but only a few stay stocked:** expansion of the catalog has outrun the money available to support it.

**Customers complain about prices and the shelf barely moves:** the markup is probably costing more turnover than it adds in margin.

**Every new purchase leaves the next morning weak:** I am treating the displayed balance as spendable cash instead of accounting for replacement stock first.

I fix the leak that is already visible before adding another revenue system. Once the existing shelves can keep selling without constant rescue, new licenses, larger rooms, play tables, and high-value cards start compounding the economy instead of competing for the same last dollars.
