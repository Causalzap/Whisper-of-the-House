<h2 id="progression-order">
  What should I unlock next in TCG Card Shop Simulator?
</h2>

TCG Card Shop Simulator throws new licenses, furniture, rooms, and card systems at me faster than I can make good use of all of them. The important distinction is that **an item becoming available does not mean the shop is ready for it**.

In a fresh 1.0 run, shop level 7 arrived by day 3 and level 13 by day 7. That gives a useful sense of how quickly the opening unlocks can stack up, but it does not make day count a reliable build order. Two level-13 shops can have completely different floor space, staff, stock depth, cash, and card collections.

I use four questions whenever the next unlock appears:

1. Is an active task still blocking progression?
2. Is the current shop failing to keep products available or customers moving?
3. Does this unlock solve that problem now?
4. Can I use it immediately after buying it?

That last question cuts out a surprising number of premature purchases. A license with no money left for its first shipment, an empty room with nothing useful to place in it, or equipment for a card problem I do not have yet all increase the size of the shop without moving the save forward.

<h2 id="first-days">
  Get the opening shop stable before chasing side systems
</h2>

The first few days move quickly because even basic packs sell and the early tasks keep introducing another mechanic. I let those tasks set the opening route instead of jumping ahead to every item that appears in the menus.

The working sequence is straightforward:

**stock the starting products → complete the current task → serve customers → use the next level-up to solve the next visible problem.**

Around shop level 4, the unlock feed is already showing additional licenses and furniture rather than only the starter setup.

The fastest way to make this stage feel harder than it is comes from buying horizontally across every new category. One useful display, one required table, or one stock license usually changes the shop more than three unrelated purchases that each create another unfinished system.

<h2 id="card-table">
  If the Card Table task will not clear, check the exact furniture name
</h2>

One of the first genuine progression stalls can happen while the game is teaching card play.

I had already placed a table that looked relevant, yet the task still asked me to **buy a Card Table and put it in the shop**. The issue was not placement or a broken objective; the furniture I had bought was not the specific item the task wanted.

The fix is to return to the furniture list and buy the item named **Card Table**.

After that task clears, the next part of the chain asks for **10 customers to play a card game in the shop**. At this point I leave enough play space for customers to use the table and let the counter advance while I continue normal retail work.

This is an important early pattern: if a tutorial objective is still visible, I solve the wording on that objective before assuming another license, room, or research-style purchase will bypass it.

<h2 id="shop-level-unlocks">
  Shop level tells you what is available, not what is urgent
</h2>

More of the game opens through shop level, so XP progression matters throughout the save. What changes from level to level is not just the quantity of unlocked items, but the kind of problems I can start solving.

A shelf that empties constantly points toward stock capacity or restocking.

A checkout line that keeps growing points toward labor.

A collection filling with duplicates creates a reason to look at card-processing systems.

A stable shop with enough room does not need an expansion simply because another lot has become purchasable.

By the middle of the opening phase, specialized equipment begins appearing alongside ordinary retail furniture. That is where I stop reading the unlock screen as a checklist and start reading it as a menu of possible answers.

<h2 id="workbench-level-7">
  Shop level 7 opens the Workbench and the playable Tetramon route
</h2>

Level 7 is more meaningful in 1.0 because the **Workbench** is not only another piece of store equipment. It is where the playable Tetramon side of the game starts becoming relevant.

Once I can use it, cards no longer exist only as inventory, collection progress, or expensive singles. Duplicates may have value inside a 50-card deck, evolution lines begin to matter, and customers waiting to play can become opponents rather than background activity.

I still do not let that pull the whole shop off course.

If retail shelves are collapsing or the current task is unfinished, fixing the store comes first. Once the business is stable enough to spend time on the card game, the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tetramon-duel-master">Tetramon Duel Master guide</Link> covers deck building, Elemental Areas, evolution, Guardians, customer duels, and tournaments.

That handoff is much cleaner than treating Tetramon as another generic furniture unlock. Level 7 is the point where a new progression branch becomes available; whether I follow it immediately depends on how healthy the shop already is.

<h2 id="expansion-timing">
  Expand when floor space is actually blocking the next step
</h2>

At one point the next available shop expansion cost **$400**.

The price itself was not the deciding factor. The current floor still worked, while stock and ordinary operating needs were more pressing, so taking another room would have created space without solving the problem in front of me.

Expansion starts making sense when the existing layout is forcing compromises: merchandise has nowhere useful to go, play tables are competing with shelves, customer movement is becoming awkward, or a new system needs space that the current room cannot provide.

That gives expansion a concrete job from the moment I buy it.

If the shop is simply short on money, another room does not solve that. Pricing, product mix, restocking, and working cash belong to the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money guide</Link>; here the relevant progression question is whether space itself is the blocker.

<h2 id="when-to-hire">
  Hire when manual work starts preventing higher-value work
</h2>

The first employee looks like an obvious quality-of-life upgrade, but the benefit depends heavily on the job I give them.

In one early store, putting the worker on checkout did not remove the pressure I expected. The register still felt slow enough that I kept paying attention to it. Restocking was more useful because repeated shelf trips were consuming the time I wanted for ordering, opening cards, reorganizing merchandise, and checking new unlocks.

That changed the way I judge the first hire.

I look for the task that repeatedly pulls me away from everything else. When that task becomes predictable, an employee can take it over and free me to work on progression that cannot be delegated as easily.

The answer also changes as the store grows. A small shop may be easy to run alone; a larger one with more shelves, customers, singles, play tables, and card systems creates enough simultaneous work that labor becomes a genuine progression upgrade rather than just convenience.

<h2 id="mid-game">
  Mid-game begins when several useful systems are competing for attention
</h2>

The transition into mid-game is less about a single level and more about the shape of the shop.

Early on, most decisions answer an immediate need: more stock, the required furniture, another shelf. Later, several worthwhile systems can all be available at once:

- broader merchandise,
- more floor space,
- additional staff,
- grading,
- duplicate processing,
- pack-opening equipment,
- storage,
- improved play space,
- Tetramon deck building and customer duels.

At that point, progression stops being a straight sequence of purchases.

I decide by looking at what work is accumulating.

If merchandise disappears faster than it can be replaced, inventory flow comes first.

If customers are waiting because I am tied up elsewhere, staffing becomes more valuable.

If the collection is becoming harder to manage than the retail floor, card-processing systems start earning their place.

If the store itself is running smoothly, that is when I am much more comfortable spending time on Tetramon, grading, collection goals, or other systems that do not immediately improve basic retail throughput.

<h2 id="grading-workbench-automation">
  Grading and automation matter when they remove work you already have
</h2>

Grading is available well before it needs to dominate the save.

Submitting cards sends them away for days and costs money up front. That can be perfectly reasonable once the card collection is large enough and the shop can carry the cost, but it does not fix an early store whose basic retail loop is still unstable.

Automation follows the same practical rule from a different direction.

Pack-opening machines, storage, duplicate tools, and related equipment are strongest when manual card work has become repetitive enough to compete with running the business. Before that point, they are capacity waiting for a workload.

The Workbench is the notable exception because 1.0 gives it a new gameplay branch through Tetramon. I may use it earlier for deck building even when I have no need for broader card-processing automation.

That distinction matters: **Workbench access can unlock something new; automation usually improves something I am already doing repeatedly.**

<h2 id="progression-stuck">
  When progress stops, identify the gate before spending again
</h2>

Different blockers can feel identical because they all end with the same result: nothing new seems to happen.

I separate them by what the game is currently waiting for.

**The task text is still active:** complete that exact action. The Card Table objective is the classic early example.

**A license is visible but locked by shop level:** keep the store operating and gaining XP. Furniture cannot bypass the level gate.

**The Workbench is available but Tetramon still feels disconnected from the shop:** build the deck there and move into customer duels rather than waiting for the ordinary play-table system to explain the whole 1.0 card game.

**Shelves are empty or customers are backing up:** the next useful progression purchase is probably tied to stock flow or labor.

**The current room is genuinely crowded:** expansion now has a purpose.

**Grading, packs, and duplicates are consuming too much manual time:** specialized card systems have moved from optional to useful.

**The next thing is unlocked but the shop cannot comfortably fund it:** that is an economy problem, not an unlock-order problem.

TCG Card Shop Simulator rarely needs a rigid “Day 5 buy this, Day 10 buy that” route. Shop level sets the gates, but the store itself tells me which of those gates is worth walking through next.
