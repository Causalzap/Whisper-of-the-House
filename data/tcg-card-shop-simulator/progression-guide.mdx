<h2 id="progression-order">
  What should I unlock next in TCG Card Shop Simulator?
</h2>

TCG Card Shop Simulator unlocks licenses, furniture, employees, card systems, and extra space quickly enough that buying everything as soon as it appears is one of the easiest ways to leave the shop short on cash.

The useful question is not simply what the newest unlock is. I look at what is currently preventing the shop from moving forward.

A fresh 1.0 run reached **shop level 7 by day 3** and **level 13 by day 7**, so several systems can appear while the store is still relatively small. Day count is therefore a poor purchase schedule. Two shops at the same level can have completely different cash reserves, floor layouts, product demand, staffing pressure, and card collections.

Before spending on a new unlock, I check four things:

1. Is an active task still waiting for one specific action?
2. Are customers being slowed down by empty shelves, checkout, or floor space?
3. Does the new purchase solve that problem immediately?
4. Will enough cash remain to actually use the thing I am buying?

The fourth check prevents a lot of bad purchases. A product license without money for the first shipment does nothing. An extra room with no furniture or inventory to fill it does nothing. Expensive card equipment also has little value when the shop is still struggling to keep basic products available.

The best progression purchases change what I can do immediately without breaking the part of the store that already works.

<h2 id="first-days">
  Get the opening shop stable before buying across every category
</h2>

The first few days move quickly because the starting products sell, tutorial objectives introduce new mechanics, and shop levels arrive close together. I let those objectives establish the opening rhythm before spreading money across unrelated licenses and furniture.

A reliable opening sequence is:

**stock the products customers already buy → clear the current task → serve enough customers to restore cash → spend on the next useful unlock.**

By around shop level 4, the unlock feed is already offering several directions rather than a single mandatory purchase.

Buying one useful display and keeping it stocked usually advances the shop more than opening three new product categories that all need their own licenses, shelves, and inventory.

The same applies to side systems. A new mechanic can be interesting without being the best place to spend the next few hundred dollars.

<h2 id="card-table">
  If the Card Table objective will not clear, buy the exact furniture it asks for
</h2>

An early progression stall can happen during the card-play objectives.

I had a table in the store that looked relevant, but the task still asked me to **buy a Card Table and put it in the shop**. Moving the existing furniture did not help because it was the wrong item.

The fix was to return to the furniture list and purchase the object specifically named **Card Table**.

Once that requirement clears, the following objective asks for **10 customers to play a card game in the shop**. From there I leave enough space around the table for customers to use it and continue operating the store while the count rises.

This is a useful pattern for later progression as well. When a task remains active, read its wording literally before spending money somewhere else. A level-up, expansion, or unrelated license does not bypass a requirement that is waiting for one named object or action.

<h2 id="shop-level-unlocks">
  Shop level unlocks options; the shop decides which one is useful now
</h2>

Shop level remains important throughout the save because many licenses and systems are level-gated. What changes is the type of decision attached to those unlocks.

Constantly empty shelves point toward stock capacity or faster restocking.

A register that cannot keep up with customers points toward labor.

A crowded floor creates a reason to buy more space.

A growing card collection creates reasons to use the Workbench, grading, pack-opening equipment, and other card systems.

Those are different problems, even if several solutions become available on the same level-up screen.

A few milestones change the available activities more noticeably than an ordinary product license:

- **Shop Level 7:** the Workbench opens the playable Tetramon branch.
- **Shop Level 18:** the Ascension 32-count license becomes available.
- **Shop Level 25:** tournament hosting becomes available.
- **Shop Level 32:** the larger Ascension 64-count license becomes available.

I treat these as points where the range of choices expands, not dates when every related purchase suddenly becomes mandatory.

<h2 id="workbench-level-7">
  Shop level 7 is the first major Tetramon breakpoint
</h2>

The **Workbench** at shop level 7 changes the role of the cards in the collection. Duplicates are no longer only collection progress or singles waiting to be sold; they can now support a playable **50-card Tetramon deck**.

I still check the retail floor before spending a long stretch building decks. Empty shelves and a growing checkout line continue costing sales while I am in the card menus, so Tetramon is much easier to explore after the shop can operate for a while without constant intervention.

Once the retail side is stable, the next step depends on what is missing.

When individual card strength is unclear, the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tier-list">Tetramon card tier list</Link> helps compare the cards already in the collection.

When I know which cards I want but the 50-card list produces poor opening hands, missing evolution stages, or too many unusable cards, I move to the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/deck-builder">Tetramon deck builder</Link>.

Players who already have enough cards and want a tested game plan can use the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/best-decks">best Tetramon decks</Link> to build around Wolan burst, discard effects, or Shield and Fire.

Once the deck itself is ready, opening hands, Elemental Areas, evolution timing, Guardians, customer challenges, and tournaments are covered in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tetramon-duel-master">Tetramon Duel Master guide</Link>.

Level 7 therefore opens a large new use for the collection, but it does not erase ordinary shop priorities. I spend heavily on Tetramon once the store has enough stock and labor to leave me time to play it.

<h2 id="expansion-timing">
  Buy more space after the current floor starts forcing bad compromises
</h2>

One early shop expansion cost **$400**.

That price was affordable before the expansion was actually necessary.

I wait until the existing floor begins creating visible compromises. Shelves may be competing with play tables, customers may be squeezing through an awkward layout, or a newly useful system may need space that no longer exists.

At that point the expansion has a job on the day it is purchased.

Buying space while the shop is merely short on cash solves the wrong problem. A larger empty room still needs furniture and inventory, which can make the cash shortage worse.

When the actual problem is that sales are not leaving enough money for stock and upgrades, I fix the operating economy through the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">pricing and profit decisions</Link> before adding another room.

<h2 id="when-to-hire">
  Hire after one repeated job starts stealing too much of the day
</h2>

Employees become much more valuable once the shop creates two tasks that need attention at the same time.

In a smaller store I can usually ring up customers, refill shelves, place orders, and reorganize products myself. Paying a worker before those tasks overlap may save a little movement, but it does not necessarily change how quickly the shop progresses.

The calculation changes when one repetitive job keeps interrupting everything else.

In one early setup, assigning the first worker to checkout did not remove as much pressure as expected because the register still felt slow enough to watch. Restocking was more useful. Every shelf trip handed to the employee gave me more uninterrupted time for purchasing stock, organizing the floor, opening cards, and dealing with new systems.

That is the trigger I look for: **which repeated action is preventing me from doing work that cannot be delegated?**

As the store grows, the answer can change. More products create more restocking. More customers create more checkout pressure. Singles, play tables, grading, pack opening, and Tetramon add activities that compete for the player's attention even though they do not keep the basic store running by themselves.

A hire is strongest when the assigned job is already happening often enough to justify the wage.

<h2 id="mid-game">
  Mid-game is where useful purchases start competing with each other
</h2>

The opening shop usually presents one obvious problem at a time. Later, several good purchases can be available simultaneously.

The store may want more merchandise, another employee, extra floor space, grading submissions, card equipment, storage, better play-table capacity, or more time spent on Tetramon.

This is where buying by unlock order starts breaking down.

A shop that sells products faster than they can be replaced gets more value from inventory flow than from a new side system. A shop with stocked shelves but long customer waits gets more from staffing. A collection overflowing with cards creates reasons to spend on tools that would have been wasteful ten levels earlier.

When the retail loop is already stable, progression becomes broader. I can spend money and time on cards, collection goals, customer duels, or automation without returning from the menu to find half the sales floor empty.

The transition is therefore visible in how the shop behaves, not just in the number beside Shop Level.

<h2 id="grading-workbench-automation">
  Grading and automation should start after they solve a real workload
</h2>

Grading can appear while the store still has much more immediate uses for its money. Submitting a card removes it for a period of time and puts cash into a result that will not help today's shelves, so I avoid treating every expensive-looking pull as an automatic submission.

Once restocking, bills, and the next necessary purchase are comfortably funded, grading can run in parallel with the store instead of competing with it.

The exact card decision depends on raw value, possible graded value, turnaround time, submission cost, and whether I need the sale immediately. Those choices are covered in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/card-grading">card grading section</Link> rather than repeating the same calculations here.

Automation has a different trigger. Pack-opening machines, storage equipment, duplicate-management tools, and similar purchases become useful after the manual version of that work consumes enough of the day to interfere with the rest of the business.

The Workbench is unusual because it can justify itself earlier by opening Tetramon deck building. Most other automation is strongest after I already have a repetitive workload for it to remove.

<h2 id="late-game">
  Ascension becomes a real spending decision at shop levels 18 and 32
</h2>

Ascension arrives much earlier than a level 70–85 endgame grind.

The first major stock breakpoint is **shop level 18**, where the **Ascension Card Pack (32) License** becomes available for **$10,000**.

The larger **Ascension Card Pack (64) License** follows at **shop level 32** and costs **$15,000**.

Those prices make the unlock level only half of the decision.

Reaching level 18 with $10,500 in the register does not mean I want to spend almost all of it on the 32-count license. The license still needs stock behind it, and the ordinary products that already earn money still need to be reordered.

By level 32, larger Ascension orders become easier to support, but the same rule applies: I want the shop able to absorb both the license purchase and the inventory that comes after it.

For the Ascension cards themselves — how to stock the packs, when to open them, how many playable copies to keep, and when a valuable pull should be sold or graded — I use the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/ascension-cards">Ascension Cards guide</Link>.

Progression only needs one decision here: **do not confuse reaching the required level with being financially ready for the purchase.**

<h2 id="progression-stuck">
  When progression feels stuck, identify what the game is actually waiting for
</h2>

Several different problems produce the same feeling: the shop is open, money is moving, but nothing important seems to advance.

The quickest fix is to match the symptom to the gate.

**An objective is still displayed.** Read the task literally and complete the required action. The Card Table objective is a good early example.

**A license is visible but locked.** Keep serving customers and gaining shop XP. Furniture or cash cannot bypass a Shop Level requirement.

**The unlock is available but buying it would empty the register.** Keep the current store operating until the purchase and its first usable inventory can both be funded.

**Products are disappearing from shelves faster than they are replaced.** Spend on stock flow or labor before adding another system.

**Customers are waiting while I perform other jobs.** Assign staff to the task that repeatedly interrupts higher-value work.

**The floor no longer fits the shelves, tables, or equipment I actually use.** Expansion now solves a real problem instead of creating empty capacity.

**The Workbench is unlocked but Tetramon still goes nowhere.** Decide whether the missing piece is card quality, deck construction, a tested build, or match rules, then move to that part of the card system instead of buying unrelated shop equipment.

**Grading or pack work is consuming too much time or money.** Pause new submissions or automation purchases until the basic retail loop is stable again.

TCG Card Shop Simulator rarely rewards a rigid schedule of “buy this on day five, buy that on day ten.” Shop levels determine when options appear. The state of the store determines which of those options is worth paying for next.
