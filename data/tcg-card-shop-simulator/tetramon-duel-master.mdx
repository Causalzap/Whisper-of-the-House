<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Reach **shop level 7**, place the **Workbench**, and build a legal **50-card Tetramon deck** with no more than **4 copies of the same card name**. A duel starts with **500 Tamer Points**, a **5-card hand**, one mulligan, and **4 face-down Guardian cards**. Put Basics into the Fire, Earth, Water, and Wind areas, keep important Basics alive for a turn so they can evolve, and remember that the player going first cannot attack on the opening turn.

<h2 id="start-tetramon-duel">
  How do I start playing Tetramon Duel Master?
</h2>

Tetramon becomes playable once the shop reaches **level 7** and the Workbench is available. Before that point, most of my card decisions revolve around selling singles, filling the collection, and opening more packs. The Workbench changes that because the cards can finally be assembled into a deck and taken into an actual match.

The first route is straightforward:

1. Reach shop level 7.
2. Place the Workbench.
3. Build a deck with exactly **50 cards**.
4. Keep each card name at **4 copies or fewer**.
5. Find a customer waiting for a duel, or enter a tournament in the shop.
6. Draw the opening five cards and decide whether to use the mulligan.

The 50-card requirement is only the entry ticket. If the list keeps producing hands full of cards that cannot be played, the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/deck-builder">Tetramon deck builder</Link> can check the evolution structure and earlier-stage support without changing how the duel itself works.

If level 7 is still several shop upgrades away, the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">progression route</Link> covers what to prioritize before the Workbench becomes available.

<h2 id="opening-hand">
  What happens at the start of a Tetramon duel?
</h2>

Both players begin with **500 Tamer Points** and draw **five cards**. Before the first turn begins, there is one chance to redraw the opening hand.

I use that mulligan much more aggressively when the hand has no playable Basic. A later evolution may be powerful eventually, but it cannot help me establish an Elemental Area if the form underneath it has not reached the board.

After the opening hand is settled, **four cards from the top of the deck are placed face down in the Guardian Area**. I cannot choose those cards or plan around knowing which ones were moved there, so the opening five need to stand on their own.

That matters more than the deck size suggests. A 50-card deck does not mean all 50 cards are equally accessible from turn one; four are temporarily hidden as Guardians, while evolution cards may also be waiting for earlier forms before they become playable.

<h2 id="elemental-areas">
  How do the four Elemental Areas work?
</h2>

The board is divided into four combat positions:

**Fire, Earth, Water, and Wind.**

Tetramon cards carry values for those elements, and the relevant value matters when the card occupies that Elemental Area. Attack strength is not just one printed number on the card; the Tetramon's value in that element combines with the power currently built up in the area.

Each Elemental Area can receive **one new Basic Tetramon per turn**. Playing another Basic into an occupied area replaces the card already there rather than creating a second independent slot.

That replacement rule is easy to underestimate. If I have kept a Basic alive specifically so it can evolve next turn, covering it with another Basic throws away that setup. Before replacing a card, I check whether the current one is about to become more valuable than the card I am trying to play.

<h2 id="play-effects">
  Play Effects can matter before the card ever attacks
</h2>

Many Tetramon trigger a **Play Effect** when they enter the field. Those effects can change elemental power, draw cards, force discards, create shields, destroy field cards, interfere with evolution, or alter the current turn in other ways.

Because the effect resolves as part of playing the card, sequencing matters. A card that improves the damage of an element is more useful before the attack that intends to use that bonus; a draw effect may reveal a missing evolution piece before I commit the rest of the turn.

This is also why card price and rarity do not tell me enough about duel value. Two cards from the same class can solve completely different problems depending on their Play Effects.

When I am choosing which individual cards are worth building around, I compare them in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tier-list">Tetramon card tier list</Link>. During the match itself, the question is simpler: **what changes immediately when I play this card, and does that help the turn I am already taking?**

<h2 id="evolution">
  When can I evolve a Tetramon?
</h2>

A Basic Tetramon **cannot evolve on the same turn it enters the field**. It has to remain in play for one turn before a matching evolution can be placed on top of it.

That makes every important Basic a small investment. Playing it now may produce an immediate effect, but the larger payoff can depend on keeping that Elemental Area intact until the following turn.

Once the Basic is eligible, the correct evolution card can be stacked onto it. Some lines continue farther, while others branch into more than one possible next form.

That is why a later-stage card can be excellent and still feel useless in the wrong hand. If the required earlier form never reaches the field, the evolution stays stranded. When this keeps happening across several matches, I check the list itself in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/deck-builder">deck builder</Link> rather than blaming the draw every time.

<h2 id="attack-phase">
  Can the first player attack on turn one?
</h2>

No. **The player who goes first does not receive an Attack Phase on the opening turn.**

The first turn is therefore a setup turn rather than a failed attack turn. I use it to establish useful Basics, trigger Play Effects, and create positions that can evolve or attack more effectively once the restriction is gone.

From the following turns onward, attacks push the opponent's Tamer Points down from the starting **500** toward **0**. The Tetramon occupying an Elemental Area uses its matching elemental value together with that area's power, so building the board well before attacking can matter as much as the card selected for the attack itself.

Reducing the opponent to zero wins the duel, but damage also interacts with the Guardian Area on the way there.

<h2 id="guardian-area">
  What do Guardian cards do?
</h2>

The four face-down Guardian cards return to the player's hand as Tamer Points fall through four thresholds:

- **400 TP** — first Guardian
- **300 TP** — second Guardian
- **200 TP** — third Guardian
- **100 TP** — fourth Guardian

They are delayed resources rather than part of the starting hand.

This creates an important rhythm in longer matches. A strong hit moves the opponent closer to defeat, but crossing a Guardian threshold also gives that player another card. A player who has fallen behind may therefore gain more options as the match progresses.

After the fourth Guardian has returned at 100 TP, there is no fifth threshold waiting between 100 and 0.

I do not avoid damage just because it releases a Guardian, but I keep the threshold in mind when a hit is large enough to cross one. The opponent may begin the next turn with a new option that did not exist when I committed the attack.

<h2 id="first-duel">
  What should I watch for in my first customer duel?
</h2>

The first few customer matches are most useful when I pay attention to where the turns stop flowing.

A hand with no playable Basic points toward the deck rather than the board. An evolution that repeatedly sits unused suggests the earlier stages are not arriving often enough. A Basic that was ready to evolve but gets replaced by another card is a play mistake rather than a collection problem.

The other common trap is assuming that a rarer or more expensive card automatically improves the next match. Tetramon rewards cards that arrive in a usable order. A cheaper Basic that opens an evolution line can be more valuable at that moment than a powerful final form with nothing underneath it.

I usually change one clear problem at a time. If the opening hand keeps failing, I fix the list. If I have playable cards but keep wasting Elemental Areas, I change how I sequence the turn. If the deck works but I am unsure which individual card gives the better payoff, I compare the alternatives in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tier-list">card rankings</Link>.

<h2 id="customer-duels">
  How do I challenge customers to Tetramon duels?
</h2>

Customers waiting around the play area can be challenged directly once I have a deck ready.

These matches are the easiest place to learn what the deck feels like outside the Workbench. Within a few turns I can usually tell whether Basics appear reliably, whether evolution cards arrive in a sensible order, and whether I am preserving the Elemental Areas long enough to use the setup I built.

A customer duel also exposes mistakes that card collection alone cannot show. Opening more packs may give me more options, but it does not fix a list that already contains the right cards in the wrong proportions.

After a loss, I look at the turn that stopped working before changing the deck. If I could not begin an evolution line, that suggests one problem. If I had a complete board and still made weak attacks, that suggests another.

<h2 id="tournaments">
  Can I play in my own tournaments?
</h2>

Yes. The player can personally enter tournaments held in the shop and compete for the prize set for that event.

I prefer to use ordinary customer duels before entering with a newly assembled deck. Tournament matches will not repair the same structural problems that already appear against customers, and an awkward opening hand or broken evolution package becomes much more frustrating when it happens inside the event I wanted to win.

Once the list is producing playable openings and the main evolution lines are reaching the board consistently, tournaments become the better test of whether the deck can keep functioning across several meaningful matches rather than one quick customer duel.

<h2 id="tcg-arcade">
  How do I get the TCG Arcade achievement?
</h2>

**TCG Arcade** requires **50 Tetramon duel wins against customers**.

Those wins are separate from the older counters for customers simply using the shop's play tables. Letting NPCs play in the background does not replace the need to win the customer duels yourself.

I start pushing that total once I have a deck that can finish ordinary matches without repeatedly stalling. That way the 50 wins grow through actual testing instead of turning every early experiment into a slow achievement grind.

The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement tracker</Link> keeps the Tetramon wins alongside the other long 1.0 completion goals.

<h2 id="what-to-fix-next">
  If the deck still feels bad, fix the failure you can actually see
</h2>

After several matches, the problem is usually easier to identify than it was at the Workbench.

**The opening hand cannot establish a board:** check whether enough playable earlier stages are present.

**Evolution cards stay in hand for several turns:** check the support underneath those evolution lines.

**A good Basic keeps disappearing before it can evolve:** stop replacing that Elemental Area too early.

**The deck plays smoothly but individual cards feel weak:** compare the cards doing the same job rather than changing the entire list.

**The rules make sense but the 50-card list still bricks:** run the list through the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/deck-builder">Tetramon deck builder</Link> and look for missing or thin evolution support.

**The structure works and I want stronger individual choices:** use the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tier-list">Tetramon tier list</Link> to compare Play Effects, reliability, evolution access, synergy, flexibility, and printed stats.

I get better results from changing the reason a turn failed than from rebuilding the deck after every loss. Once the opening hand can begin a board, the evolution lines reach the field, and the Elemental Areas survive long enough to do their job, the match becomes much easier to read.
