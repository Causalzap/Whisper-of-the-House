<h2 id="start-tetramon-duel">
  How do I start playing Tetramon Duel Master?
</h2>

Tetramon changes what I do with cards once the shop reaches **level 7**.

Before that point, packs mostly feed three things: shop inventory, collection progress, and single-card sales. Once the **Workbench** is available, those same cards can become a playable deck.

The route is:

1. Reach shop level 7.
2. Unlock and place the Workbench.
3. Build a legal 50-card deck.
4. Keep enough Basic Tetramon and their evolution cards to make the deck playable.
5. Find a customer waiting for a match at the play tables, or enter a tournament.
6. Start the duel with a five-card hand and decide whether to use the one mulligan.

That is the first real breakpoint I use for Tetramon.

I would not sell every duplicate on sight once deck building is available. A second, third, or fourth copy may be more useful in the deck than its shop value suggests, because a legal deck can carry up to **four copies of the same card**.

If shop level 7 itself is still the blocker, the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">progression guide</Link> covers the store decisions that matter before Tetramon becomes a priority.

<h2 id="deck-building">
  Build 50 cards first; optimize the deck later
</h2>

A Tetramon deck contains **50 cards**, with a maximum of **four copies of the same card**.

For the first deck, I treat legality and consistency as separate problems.

The first question is simply:

**Can this deck actually put Basic Tetramon onto the board and evolve them?**

An expensive collection does not guarantee that.

A pile of high-rarity evolution cards can leave me with nothing useful to play if the matching Basic forms never arrive. On the other hand, filling the deck with only Basic cards gives me bodies but leaves part of the evolution system unused.

So the first pass through the Workbench is about checking relationships rather than market value:

- which cards are Basic Tetramon,
- which cards evolve from them,
- whether I own the required earlier form,
- and whether a branch has more than one possible evolution.

That last point matters because not every chain is perfectly linear.

Tetran is a useful example because its line can branch rather than forcing every copy through exactly the same next form.

That makes duplicate management more complicated than it was when cards were only collection pieces or merchandise. Two cards with similar sale value can have very different deck value if one completes a usable evolution line and the other currently has no supporting Basic in my collection.

<h2 id="opening-hand">
  The opening hand is five cards, with one chance to redraw
</h2>

Each player starts with **500 Tamer Points** and draws **five cards**.

Before play begins, I get one mulligan opportunity.

For the first few games, I use that redraw for one reason above everything else: I want an opening hand that can actually begin a board.

A hand full of evolution cards looks powerful but may leave me waiting for the Basic Tetramon they require. A playable Basic is immediately useful because the four Elemental Areas are where the duel starts taking shape.

After the opening hand is settled, **four cards from the top of the deck are placed face down in the Guardian Area**.

Those cards are not normal opening-hand resources. They return later when Tamer Points cross specific damage thresholds.

That means I begin each duel with fewer cards immediately available than the full deck size might suggest, and I cannot plan around knowing which four cards became Guardians.

<h2 id="elemental-areas">
  Fire, Earth, Water, and Wind decide where each Tetramon fights
</h2>

The board has four Elemental Areas:

**Fire, Earth, Water, and Wind.**

Tetramon cards show values tied to those elements.

This is where I stop reading a Tetramon card as a single attack number.

Its usefulness depends on the area it is fighting in, because attack strength combines the Tetramon's relevant value with the power of that Elemental Area.

A card that looks unimpressive when I only compare prices can therefore have a much clearer role once I look at the element values printed on it.

Each Elemental Area also limits how quickly I can flood the board.

I can summon only **one new Basic Tetramon into each Elemental Area per turn**. Playing another Basic into that occupied area replaces the previous card rather than giving me a second independent slot there.

That makes placement a commitment.

If the current Tetramon is one turn away from becoming eligible for an evolution I need, replacing it with another Basic can throw away that setup.

<h2 id="play-effects">
  Read the Play Effect before treating a card as raw power
</h2>

Tetramon can trigger a **Play Effect** as soon as they enter the field.

That means a card can change the current turn before it ever reaches the attack phase.

One example given for the system is a rare Fire Burpig whose Play Effect increases elemental power for that turn.

The practical consequence is that I read two things before placing a card:

**what happens immediately, and what I want that Elemental Area to look like afterward.**

A strong entrance effect can justify playing a card even when its printed values are not the largest in my hand.

It also makes sequencing relevant. If one Play Effect raises the power used by another attack later in the same turn, playing those cards in the wrong order wastes part of the effect.

I would not try to turn that into a fixed combo list yet. The useful first-game habit is simply to check the Play Effect before dropping the card into an area.

<h2 id="evolution">
  A Basic Tetramon has to survive a turn before it can evolve
</h2>

Evolution cannot happen immediately after a Basic enters the field.

A **Basic Tetramon must remain in play for one turn** before I can place its evolution card on top of it.

That creates a very clear setup turn.

If I play the Basic now, I am not just asking whether it contributes this turn. I am also asking whether I can keep that area intact until the next turn.

Once a Tetramon becomes eligible, the matching evolution card can be stacked directly onto it.

Evolution chains can continue farther when I have the required next form, so a Basic that survives long enough can become the foundation for a much stronger board position.

This is the main reason I avoid judging an opening hand only by rarity.

A later-stage card with no valid Basic underneath it is temporarily dead weight. A cheaper Basic that unlocks two possible branches can give me more decisions on the next turn.

<h2 id="attack-phase">
  The first player cannot attack on the opening turn
</h2>

After cards are placed and effects resolve, the duel moves toward the Attack Phase.

There is one important opening restriction:

**the player who goes first does not get an Attack Phase on the first turn.**

That makes the first turn much more about board setup than immediate damage.

When I am first, I would rather establish useful Basic Tetramon and prepare evolution lines than evaluate the turn by how much damage I failed to deal.

From the second turn onward, attack strength depends on the Tetramon and the Elemental Area it occupies.

The overall win condition is straightforward:

**reduce the opponent from 500 Tamer Points to 0.**

The route there is not completely linear, though, because dealing damage also activates the Guardian system.

<h2 id="guardian-area">
  Guardian cards give the losing player resources at four damage thresholds
</h2>

The four face-down Guardian cards become relevant when Tamer Points fall through:

**400, 300, 200, and 100.**

Crossing each threshold returns one Guardian card to that player's hand.

So a large hit does two things at once:

it moves the opponent closer to 0, but it can also give them another card to work with.

That is why I do not read the Guardian Area as four extra starting cards. They are delayed resources tied to taking damage.

The system also creates a built-in comeback effect.

A player who falls behind gains access to more of those hidden cards as the match continues. That does not erase the damage already taken, but it can expand the hand at exactly the point where more options are needed.

For the first duel, I keep the thresholds simple:

**400 → first Guardian**  
**300 → second Guardian**  
**200 → third Guardian**  
**100 → fourth Guardian**

Once all four have been taken, there is no additional Guardian threshold protecting the final 100 Tamer Points.

<h2 id="first-duel">
  For the first duel, fix unplayable hands before worrying about the perfect deck
</h2>

The first deck problem I expect is not a bad matchup.

It is a deck that technically contains 50 cards but does not produce enough useful sequences.

When the first few turns feel empty, I check the deck in this order:

**Too few playable Basics:** evolution cards are arriving without anything to evolve from.

**Evolution cards keep sitting in hand:** the deck may have too many later stages compared with their required earlier forms.

**One Elemental Area is easy to fill while others rarely have useful options:** the current card pool may be leaning too heavily toward a narrow group of values or evolution lines.

**Good Basics keep getting replaced before they evolve:** I am treating the Elemental Areas as disposable slots instead of preserving a one-turn setup.

**The hand looks expensive but does very little:** sale value and duel value are solving different problems.

That last one is especially easy to miss in a card-shop game.

The most valuable single in the display case is not automatically the card that fixes the deck.

<h2 id="customer-duels">
  Challenge waiting customers instead of treating every play table as passive income
</h2>

In 1.0, the player can personally duel customers rather than only running the shop around matches happening in the background.

Once the deck is ready, I look for a customer waiting to play and use that as the first test.

That is a much better deck check than opening another twenty packs and guessing whether the collection improved.

One duel can reveal whether the deck actually draws Basics, whether the evolution lines appear in a usable order, and whether I understand the four Elemental Areas.

If the deck cannot get through those fundamentals, buying rarer cards is not yet the fix.

<h2 id="tournaments">
  You can enter your own shop tournaments now
</h2>

Tournaments are no longer only something I organize for customers.

The player can enter a tournament personally and fight through the event for the prize that was set when the tournament was created.

That changes how I look at tournament setup.

Before 1.0, the economic question was mostly what event to host and what customers would do with the tables. Now there is also a deck question:

**Is my own deck ready to compete in the event I am hosting?**

I treat customer duels as the safer test bed first.

If a 50-card deck is still producing awkward hands or broken evolution lines against ordinary customers, putting it into a tournament only exposes the same problems in a more important match.

<h2 id="tcg-arcade">
  Customer duel wins also feed the TCG Arcade achievement
</h2>

Tetramon duels have their own completion target.

**TCG Arcade** requires **50 wins against customers**.

That is separate from the older achievements for customers simply playing games inside the shop.

So if I am aiming for 100%, I start taking customer duels once the deck is stable enough that the matches are productive instead of leaving all play-table activity to NPCs.

The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement guide</Link> keeps that 50-win target together with the rest of the long completion route.

<h2 id="before-building-meta-deck">
  Do not confuse collection rarity with a proven best deck
</h2>

The 1.0 card pool gives me enough information to build legal decks, follow evolution lines, and understand why elemental placement matters.

That is not enough to call one deck the best.

A real deck ranking needs repeated matches against different opposing boards, not a list of the most expensive pulls.

For now, I make changes after seeing a specific failure:

- add more playable starters when opening hands stall,
- rebalance an evolution line when later stages arrive too early,
- keep useful duplicates instead of automatically selling them,
- and test the revised deck against another customer before changing several things again.

That approach gives each change a reason.

Once a deck consistently reaches the board states I am trying to build, card quality and matchup-specific choices become much easier to judge.
