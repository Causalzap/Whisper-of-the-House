<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Reach **shop level 18** to unlock the **Ascension 32-count** license and **shop level 32** for the **64-count** license. Buy the license, order Ascension stock, then keep the copies your Tetramon decks actually need before opening, selling, or grading the rest.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="how-to-get-ascension-cards">
  How do you get Ascension Cards?
</h2>

Ascension becomes a normal shop product once its licenses open in **Stock Order**.

The two important breakpoints are:

- **Shop Level 18 — Ascension 32-count**
- **Shop Level 32 — Ascension 64-count**

Reaching the level only makes the corresponding license available. I still need to buy that license before the product becomes part of the shop's normal ordering loop.

That distinction matters when the level notification appears. If I have just reached 18 but spending on the license and first order would leave the store unable to refill products that are already selling, there is no need to force the purchase immediately. The license is a new option, not a requirement to empty the register.

Once the shop can absorb the purchase, I open **Stock Order**, move down to the Ascension products, buy the available license, then order enough stock for the shelf and whatever packs I actually intend to open.

If shop level itself is still the blocker, there is nothing to fix inside the Ascension menu. I keep the store running and earning XP until the required level arrives. The broader order of shop purchases stays in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">progression guide</Link>; here, the useful milestones are simply **18 for 32-count and 32 for 64-count**.

<h2 id="32-or-64">
  What changes between the 32-count and 64-count unlocks?
</h2>

The larger license changes how much Ascension stock I can move through the shop at once. It does not suddenly make the individual packs safer or more profitable to open.

That makes the two unlocks useful at different points in the save:

- **Ascension 32-count — Shop Level 18:** this is the first practical point where I can start stocking and opening Ascension in volume.
- **Ascension 64-count — Shop Level 32:** this makes more sense once the shop is already moving enough stock to justify larger orders.

At level 18, I would start small enough that Ascension does not consume the money needed for ordinary restocks.

By level 32, the shop usually has more reasons to buy in larger quantities: collection progress, repeated pack opening, valuable singles, grading candidates, and playable Tetramon copies can all pull from the same stock.

A larger count is therefore mostly a **capacity upgrade**.

It is not a signal that every pack should now be opened.

<h2 id="stock-ascension">
  Stock some Ascension before opening everything
</h2>

One easy way to turn Ascension into a cash problem is to unlock it, order a large quantity, then immediately open the whole shipment.

I separate the stock into two jobs:

**customer inventory** and **my own packs**.

In one established shop, Ascension stock was placed directly onto the sales shelf after ordering. A 64-count load could fill the available stock position, and additional product was kept behind it for later refills.

This matters most right after level 18.

Opening a pack converts predictable merchandise into an uncertain group of cards. That can be excellent for the collection, deck building, or a lucky expensive pull, but it no longer fills the shelf that customers were going to buy from.

When cash is still tight, the safer order is:

1. Buy the license.
2. Order Ascension stock.
3. Put enough on the sales floor.
4. Leave enough cash for the next ordinary restock.
5. Open the surplus.

Once the store is carrying a comfortable cash reserve, I can be much more aggressive about ripping packs.

<h2 id="open-or-sell">
  Should you open Ascension Packs or sell them?
</h2>

Ascension packs can produce very expensive cards, but one good opening session does not make every pack a guaranteed profit.

A later collection run produced several individual cards in the **$5,000–$11,000** range during the same Ascension opening session, along with Full Art and other visually distinct pulls.

The same kind of pack-heavy play can also drain the store when opening becomes the priority instead of selling.

So I use the reason I bought the packs to decide what happens next.

**I sell more packs when:**

- operating cash is low;
- shelves are already expensive to refill;
- another important license or expansion is coming;
- or I already have enough playable copies for the decks I use.

**I open more packs when:**

- the sales floor is stocked;
- the shop has enough money to survive bad pulls;
- I still need Ascension cards for the collection;
- I am looking for playable copies;
- or I want high-value cards that may be worth selling or grading individually.

For the exact cash-flow decision, I move back to the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money guide</Link>. Ascension adds another use for the same money; it does not replace the store's normal need for working capital.

<h2 id="keep-copies">
  How many Ascension cards should you keep?
</h2>

Once Tetramon deck building is active, I stop selling every duplicate automatically.

A legal deck can use up to **four copies of the same card name**, and a tested late-game collection deliberately kept **four copies** of cards that were still being used in the active deck.

That gives me a simple sorting rule:

**deck card → keep up to four useful copies first**

**collection-only card → keep whatever the collection goal needs**

**surplus card → sell, display, or grade**

The important word is useful.

I do not keep four copies of every Ascension card simply because the rules allow four. That creates a huge pile of inventory without improving the deck.

I first decide which archetype I actually play. The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/best-decks">tested Tetramon builds</Link> make that decision easier because cards such as Wolan, Mentos, Angis, Pygmy, Bird Pig, Hydron, and the supporting evolution lines have very different jobs.

Once I know which cards matter, the fourth copy can be more valuable in the deck than on the singles table.

<h2 id="ascension-and-decks">
  Do Ascension cards belong in Tetramon decks?
</h2>

They can.

Late-game play shows Ascension collecting and Tetramon deck building happening together rather than as completely separate activities. Packs were being opened specifically while the player was also looking for enough copies to replace cards in an existing deck with preferred Ascension versions.

That creates a useful priority when I open a valuable-looking card:

**check whether I need it for the deck before selling it.**

The mistake is easiest to make with a card that looks expensive.

A large market value encourages an immediate sale, but selling the only copy that completes an evolution line or an important four-copy core can make the deck worse for a relatively small cash gain.

I make the deck decision first, then the economic decision.

Cards that do not fit the build are much easier to turn into singles or grading candidates.

<h2 id="valuable-ascension">
  What should you do with an expensive Ascension card?
</h2>

A large pull gives me three realistic choices:

**keep it, sell it raw, or grade it.**

Keeping comes first when the card fills a real deck or collection gap.

Selling raw makes sense when the shop needs the money immediately.

Grading becomes interesting when the card already has enough value that a higher grade creates meaningful upside and the store can afford to wait.

Ascension cards can reach values where that difference is large enough to matter, but I do not send a card away only because the artwork or rarity looks impressive. The grading cost, turnaround, current raw price, and possible graded value all need to justify the delay.

The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/card-grading">card grading guide</Link> covers that decision, including when to sell raw, when faster grading is too expensive, and why regrading a good card can still lower its grade.

For Ascension itself, the decision is simpler:

**protect deck copies first, then decide what the surplus is worth doing with.**

<h2 id="ascension-collection">
  Ascension becomes a long-term collection project
</h2>

Once the shop can buy large Ascension orders comfortably, the system stops feeling like one new product line and starts becoming a collection sink.

Large batches can be opened repeatedly, valuable variants can be displayed or graded, and duplicates can be sorted around deck needs rather than simply sold as soon as they appear.

That also changes what counts as a successful pack.

An opening with no huge-money pull can still add:

- a missing card;
- another copy for a Tetramon deck;
- a better-looking version of a card I already use;
- grading inventory;
- or progress toward the game's long collection goals.

This is why I do not judge Ascension only by the most expensive card in a box.

The system feeds several late-game goals at once.

For achievement cleanup, the Ascension-specific pull requirements sit with the rest of the long collection counters in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement tracker</Link>. I would not change the shop's entire economy just to force one rare result earlier.

<h2 id="ascension-stuck">
  Why are Ascension Packs still unavailable?
</h2>

When Ascension seems missing, the first thing I check is the exact state of the license rather than buying unrelated furniture.

**The 32-count license is still locked:** the shop has not reached **level 18**.

**The 64-count license is still locked:** the shop has not reached **level 32**.

**The level requirement is met but the product still cannot be ordered:** check whether the license itself has actually been purchased.

**The product is available but the shop cannot afford a useful first order:** keep operating the existing store and come back with more working cash.

**The packs are being opened faster than the shop earns money:** stop opening for a few days and let Ascension function as merchandise instead.

**A valuable Ascension card disappeared from the planned deck:** check whether the copies were sold, displayed, or sent for grading before changing the build.

Those states look like one Ascension problem from the menu, but they need different fixes.

Level unlocks are solved by shop XP.

License access is solved in Stock Order.

Cash shortages are solved by the retail side of the business.

Missing deck copies are solved by protecting the cards before they leave the collection.

Once those four states are separated, Ascension becomes much easier to manage.
