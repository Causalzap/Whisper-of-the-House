<h2 id="where-to-start">
  What should I focus on first in TCG Card Shop Simulator 1.0?
</h2>

The opening shop does not need every available system running at once. It needs
enough stock to keep customers buying, enough time to finish the current task,
and enough spare cash that the next purchase does not immediately empty the
account.

That balance gets harder to read as 1.0 opens more possibilities. New product
licenses appear, play tables give customers another reason to stay, employees
can take over repetitive work, card-processing equipment starts competing for
floor space, and the Workbench eventually gives collected cards an entirely
different purpose.

I do not treat those unlocks as a shopping list. When several appear together,
I look at the store that is already running.

If a tutorial task is still active, I clear that first. If shelves are emptying
faster than I can refill them, another decorative or side-system purchase is
unlikely to help. If customers are moving through the shop but the cash balance
never seems to grow, the problem is probably not the next level unlock at all.

The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">progression guide</Link>{" "}
covers the decisions around shop-level gates, early tasks, expansion, staffing,
the level 7 Workbench, and the late Ascension unlocks. When sales are happening
but the money is not turning into usable working cash, the{" "}
<Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money guide</Link> is the
better place to check pricing, stock depth, license timing, pack opening, and
single-card sales.

<h2 id="shop-systems">
  The shop gets harder when several useful systems compete for the same hour
</h2>

During the first few days, most of the work is physical and obvious: open boxes,
stock shelves, serve customers, order replacements, and place whatever the
current task requires. Later, the store can be profitable while still feeling
more demanding because several worthwhile systems all want attention at once.

Storage can reduce messy inventory handling. Employees can remove checkout or
restocking work. Pack-opening equipment matters when opening cards manually is
taking a meaningful part of the day. Grading ties up cards and money for a
delayed return. Play tables, tournaments, and Tetramon all compete with retail
space for reasons that have nothing to do with selling another sealed product.

The useful question is not whether an upgrade is good in isolation. It is
whether the store is already doing enough of that activity for the upgrade to
change the day.

A pack opener has much more value after opening packs has become repetitive.
Storage matters more once boxes and spare inventory are making restocking
awkward. Another worker matters when the same manual task keeps dragging me away
from ordering, card work, or higher-value decisions.

That is why two shops at the same level can need completely different things.
One may need labor, another needs stock capacity, and another may finally be
stable enough that card collecting or Tetramon deserves more attention than the
retail floor.

<h2 id="packs-and-cards">
  Selling packs, opening packs, and keeping cards solve different problems
</h2>

Packs sit in the middle of the shop economy, collection progress, achievements,
rare pulls, single-card sales, and eventually Tetramon deck building. That makes
them more complicated than ordinary merchandise.

A sealed pack is predictable inventory: I can put it on a shelf and let a
customer buy it. Opening that pack gives up the guaranteed retail sale in
exchange for shop XP, collection progress, duplicate cards, rare-pull chances,
and singles that may be worth selling separately.

Once the Workbench is unlocked, duplicates gain another possible use. A second,
third, or fourth copy may improve how often an important Tetramon appears in a
deck, while an otherwise ordinary card can matter because it sits underneath an
evolution I actually want to play.

Because those outcomes are different, I change the decision with the state of
the store. If the shelves are struggling to stay stocked, sealed products still
have an obvious job. When normal retail inventory is secure, opening surplus
packs becomes easier to justify because collection progress, singles, rare
pulls, achievements, and deck building can all move at the same time.

For the money side of that choice, including when opening packs is hurting
working cash more than it helps, use the{" "}
<Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money guide</Link>. The very
large pack-opening and collection requirements are easier to track in the{" "}
<Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement tracker</Link>.

<h2 id="tetramon">
  Shop level 7 turns collected cards into playable Tetramon
</h2>

The **Workbench unlocks at shop level 7**, and that is where the meaning of the
card collection changes most sharply in 1.0.

Before the Workbench, a duplicate is usually merchandise, collection progress,
or a single card I may decide to sell. Afterward, the same duplicate can help
fill a 50-card deck, make an important card appear more consistently, or support
an evolution line that would otherwise be too thin to use comfortably.

Three different problems can show up from here.

If the binder is large and I do not know which individual cards are worth

It compares individual cards by their effects, reliability, evolution access,

If I already know which cards I want but the finished list keeps drawing

For the shop as a whole, the important change is simpler: after level 7, selling
every duplicate automatically can cost me cards that have more value in a deck
than they do in the display case.

<h2 id="grading">
  Grading can be useful early without becoming the shop's main priority
</h2>

Grading occupies an awkward position because it can appear while the store still
has much more immediate uses for both cash and cards.

Submitting a card costs money and removes it for a period of time. That delay is
much harder to absorb when the business is still short on merchandise or when
the same card could be sold immediately for cash. A small store can easily spend
too much attention on grading before the ordinary retail loop is strong enough
to support it.

Later, the tradeoff becomes easier. More cards are passing through the
collection, the shelves can run without constant emergency orders, and sending a
few cards away no longer interrupts the next day's operating money.

I therefore let grading run in parallel once the store can afford the delay. I
do not need to build the whole business around it, but I also do not want to
leave it untouched until everything else is finished.

There is a practical reason for starting before the very end: the completion
requirements eventually include **10, 100, and 500 Gem Mint cards**, a complete
**grade 1–10 set**, and selling a graded card for more than **$100,000**. Those
targets are large enough that some progress made during normal play can save a
lot of repetitive cleanup later.

The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement tracker</Link>{" "}
keeps those grading goals together with the other long counters.

<h2 id="achievements">
  Most achievements are easier when they grow beside normal shop progress
</h2>

The current game has **50 achievements**, and a large part of the list overlaps
with things the store is already doing: gaining shop levels, buying licenses,
hiring workers, expanding the floor, opening packs, collecting cards, selling
valuable singles, and getting customers to use the play area.

That means I do not start a fresh shop by trying to finish them one at a time.
Many counters will move naturally if I keep the business progressing.

The ones I watch more closely are the goals that can quietly fall behind while
the shop becomes more efficient.

Manual checkout is the clearest example. Giving the register to an employee may
make daily operations smoother, but it can also leave the player's own
**1,000-checkout** total barely moving. Grading creates the opposite problem:
the largest Gem Mint target is long enough that postponing the system entirely
can leave a large grind waiting at the end.

The late collection goals are also much bigger than normal early play suggests:
**4,000 collected cards**, **25,000 opened packs**, Ascension-specific pulls,
and **50 Tetramon duel wins against customers** all sit far beyond the first
successful shop.

I keep those numbers in mind without letting them dictate every early purchase.
When the shop is stable and I want to see what has actually fallen behind, the{" "}
<Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">50-achievement tracker</Link>{" "}
shows which counters still need deliberate attention.

<h2 id="what-to-do-next">
  What should I do next when the shop feels stuck?
</h2>

The fastest way I have found to diagnose a stalled save is to describe the
symptom before looking at the unlock menu.

**Several things just unlocked and I do not know which one to buy.**  
Check the{" "}
<Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">progression guide</Link>.
The next useful purchase is usually the one that removes a problem already
visible in the store rather than the newest item in the menu.

**Customers are buying, but the cash balance barely grows.**  
Move to the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money guide</Link>.
Pricing, replacement stock, license timing, pack opening, and unreliable
single-card income can make a busy shop feel poorer than it looks.

**The Workbench is unlocked, but I have no idea which cards are worth keeping.**  
Use the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tier-list">Tetramon card tier list</Link>{" "}
to compare individual cards before selling useful duplicates.

**I picked the cards I like, but the 50-card deck keeps producing bad hands.**  
Run it through the{" "}
<Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/deck-builder">Tetramon deck builder</Link>.
Repeatedly drawing later evolutions without their earlier stages is a deck
problem, not a reason to buy another shop expansion.

**The deck is ready, but the actual matches still feel confusing.**  
The{" "}
<Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tetramon-duel-master">Tetramon Duel Master guide</Link>{" "}
covers what happens after the duel starts, including Elemental Areas, evolution,
Guardians, customer matches, and tournaments.

**The shop itself works well and the remaining goals are huge counters.**  
Check the{" "}
<Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement tracker</Link>{" "}
before deliberately grinding packs, grading, collection totals, or customer
duel wins.

**Pack opening, grading, storage, or inventory work is taking over the day.**  
That is usually when specialized equipment and employees become more valuable
than another basic retail expansion.

TCG Card Shop Simulator becomes easier to read once every new unlock stops
feeling equally urgent. I want the shop to tell me what is missing: stock when
the shelves are empty, labor when repetitive work takes over, space when the
floor is genuinely crowded, card systems when the collection has a purpose for
them, and long-term cleanup only after the business can support it.
