<h2 id="where-to-start">
  What should I focus on first in TCG Card Shop Simulator 1.0?
</h2>

The opening store does not need every system working at once. It needs enough
product to sell, a way to serve customers without the shop falling behind, and
a clear answer to whatever task is currently blocking progression.

That sounds basic, but 1.0 starts layering new possibilities on top of the store
quickly. More merchandise appears, play tables begin pulling customers into a
second activity, employees can remove manual work, card systems expand beyond
simple collecting, and the Workbench eventually opens a completely different
use for the cards I have been pulling.

When several of those options appear together, I use the problem in front of me
to decide where to go next.

If I cannot tell what deserves the next purchase, the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">progression guide</Link> follows the shop from the opening tasks through the level 7 Workbench, expansion and staffing decisions, and the later Ascension milestones.

If customers keep buying but the balance refuses to grow, the problem is no
longer unlock order. The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money guide</Link> covers pricing, stock depth, license costs, pack opening, single-card sales, and the places where cash tends to disappear.

<h2 id="shop-systems">
  The shop gets harder when several systems start competing for your time
</h2>

The first days are mostly physical work: checkout, boxes, shelves, orders, and
whatever the current task asks me to place. Later, the difficulty shifts. The
store may be profitable, yet there are now too many useful things competing for
the same hour.

Employees can take over repetitive jobs. Storage reduces the need to improvise
around loose stock. Pack-opening equipment changes how much card processing can
be done manually. Grading removes cards for a period of time in exchange for a
different kind of value. Tournament and Tetramon systems create reasons to use
floor space for something other than merchandise.

That is the point where I stop assuming that the newest machine is automatically
the best upgrade. A pack opener is valuable when opening packs has become a
large part of the day. Storage matters when inventory handling is becoming
awkward. Another employee matters when a recurring task is preventing me from
doing something more important.

The shop can reach the same level with very different problems, so I get more
value from matching the system to the workload than from copying a fixed list
of purchases.

<h2 id="packs-and-cards">
  Selling packs, opening packs, and keeping cards are three different decisions
</h2>

Packs sit at the intersection of almost every major system in the game.

A sealed pack can be sold for predictable retail income. Opening it gives shop
XP, collection progress, duplicates, rare-pull chances, and cards that may be
sold individually. After Tetramon deck building becomes available, some of
those duplicates can also become useful deck pieces.

Because those outcomes serve different goals, I do not treat “which pack should
I buy?” as one universal question.

When the shop is still short on working stock, sealed product has an obvious
job: customers can buy it. Once the shelves are secure, opening surplus packs
can start serving collection, single-card sales, achievements, and deck
building at the same time.

The economic side of that choice is covered in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money guide</Link>. The long collection and pack-opening targets are in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement tracker</Link>.

<h2 id="tetramon">
  Level 7 is where your cards stop being only merchandise and collectibles
</h2>

The **Workbench unlock at shop level 7** gives the card collection a new
purpose in 1.0: building a playable Tetramon deck.

That changes duplicate management immediately. A card can now be worth keeping
because it completes a legal 50-card deck, supports an evolution line, or gives
another copy of a useful Tetramon rather than because its sale price is high.

The playable card game has its own rules: 50-card decks, four Elemental Areas,
evolution timing, Guardian cards, customer challenges, and tournaments. Those
mechanics are covered in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tetramon-duel-master">Tetramon Duel Master guide</Link>.

For the shop itself, the important breakpoint is simpler: once Workbench access
arrives, selling every duplicate automatically can work against a system that
did not exist in the same form before 1.0.

<h2 id="grading">
  Grading becomes useful before it becomes urgent
</h2>

Grading occupies an awkward middle ground because it can appear while the store
still has much more immediate uses for its money.

Submitting cards costs cash and removes those cards for a period of time. In a
small shop that is still struggling to keep merchandise available, that can
feel expensive for a result that does not help the next customer.

Later, the same delay becomes easier to absorb. The retail side is stronger,
more cards are passing through the collection, and grading can run in parallel
with everything else rather than competing with basic stock.

There is also a completion reason not to ignore it forever. The achievement
list eventually asks for **10, 100, and 500 Gem Mint cards**, a complete grade
1–10 set, and a graded card sale worth more than $100,000. The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement guide</Link> keeps those counters together so grading can be started early enough without turning the opening shop into a grading business.

<h2 id="achievements">
  Most achievements should ride on top of the shop rather than interrupt it
</h2>

The 1.0 list contains **50 achievements**, but many of them already overlap with
things the store needs to do: reach higher shop levels, unlock pack licenses,
hire workers, expand the floor, open packs, grow the collection, and attract
customers to play.

The dangerous achievements are the ones that can quietly fall behind.

Manual checkout is one example because assigning employees to the register can
make the store smoother while leaving the player's own 1,000-checkout counter
behind. Grading works in the opposite direction: the largest Gem Mint target is
so long that postponing the entire system can create a large final grind.

The new 1.0 goals also stretch well past the opening store, including 4,000
collected cards, 25,000 opened packs, Ascension-specific pulls, and **50
Tetramon duel wins against customers**.

The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">50-achievement tracker</Link> is the cleaner way to follow those totals without turning normal shop decisions into achievement farming from day one.

<h2 id="what-to-do-next">
  Match the symptom to the guide that solves it
</h2>

When the store feels like it has stopped moving, I use the symptom rather than
the shop level to choose the next step.

**I have several new unlocks and no idea which one matters.**  
Go to the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">progression guide</Link>. The issue is deciding which system actually removes the current bottleneck.

**Customers keep buying, but money never seems to accumulate.**  
Use the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money guide</Link>. Pricing, restock depth, license timing, opened packs, and unreliable single-card income are usually more relevant than another expansion.

**The Workbench is unlocked and I want to start using my cards in matches.**  
The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tetramon-duel-master">Tetramon Duel Master guide</Link> covers the first legal deck, Elemental Areas, Play Effects, evolution, Guardians, customer duels, and tournament play.

**The shop itself is running well and most remaining goals are large counters.**  
Open the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement tracker</Link> and check what is actually unfinished before starting another grind.

**Pack opening, grading, storage, or other card work is consuming most of the day.**  
That is the signal that specialized equipment and labor are becoming more
valuable than another round of basic retail expansion.

The game gets much easier to read once every new unlock stops looking equally
urgent. A strong save is usually not the one that buys systems fastest; it is
the one where each system arrives at roughly the same time the shop has a real
job for it.
