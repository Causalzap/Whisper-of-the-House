<h2 id="where-to-start">
  What should I focus on first in TCG Card Shop Simulator 1.0?
</h2>

A new shop does not need every unlocked system running at the same time. The opening hours become much easier once I separate **what is available** from **what the store needs right now**.

If a task is still active, I finish the exact action it asks for. When products are selling out faster than I can replace them, stock comes before another license. When customers are buying but the balance never seems to grow, I look at prices, replacement inventory, and where the cash is being spent before expanding again.

That creates a useful order of attention:

**active task → stock and checkout → working cash → space and staff → card systems and longer goals.**

Shop level still matters because it decides when new options appear, but two stores at the same level can need very different purchases. One may be short on inventory, another may be losing time at the register, while a third already runs smoothly enough to spend money on cards and collecting.

For shop-level gates, expansion timing, hiring, and the Workbench, see <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">what to unlock next</Link>. When the shop is busy but money is disappearing as quickly as it arrives, <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">the money and pricing route</Link> covers stock depth, markups, profitable products, card sales, pack opening, and cash-flow problems.

<h2 id="shop-systems">
  Buy equipment after the shop has a job for it
</h2>

The opening shop is mostly physical work: unpacking boxes, filling shelves, serving customers, replacing sold products, and placing whatever an objective requires. Later, several useful systems begin competing for the same money, floor space, and player attention.

Storage can reduce inventory clutter. Employees can take over checkout or restocking. Pack-opening equipment saves time once opening cards has become repetitive. Grading can turn valuable cards into more valuable ones, but the card and the grading money are unavailable while the submission is being processed.

An upgrade that looks powerful can still be premature. A pack opener has little urgency while I barely open packs. More storage does not solve a store whose real problem is an empty sales floor. Another room creates possibilities, but it also creates more space that may need shelves, products, and staff.

I spend first where the workload is already visible. That keeps upgrades working from the day they are purchased instead of sitting idle while the basic store still needs attention.

<h2 id="money-and-stock">
  Keep sales repeatable instead of chasing one large day
</h2>

The amount in the register can be misleading because part of it already belongs to tomorrow's inventory.

If packs, boxes, or accessories are selling quickly, I replace those products before treating the remaining money as upgrade cash. The store becomes much more stable once a successful day can be repeated without waiting for another lucky single-card sale.

Pricing works the same way. Products can sell above market value, but the best price is the one that keeps both margin and turnover healthy. A higher price stops helping when complaints increase and the shelf stops moving.

Large singles, customer-card deals, grading, pack pulls, and shop expansions can all produce or unlock much larger amounts of money later, but each also ties up cash in a different way. The detailed decisions are in <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">how to make money faster</Link>.

<h2 id="packs-and-cards">
  Decide whether a pack is merchandise or card inventory before opening it
</h2>

Sealed packs have a predictable job: customers can buy them directly from the shelf.

Opening them trades that predictable sale for several possible results at once. The cards can advance the collection, become singles, support achievements, produce valuable pulls, or eventually fill Tetramon decks.

That makes pack opening easier to justify after the retail side has enough stock to survive a bad batch.

During the opening days, I usually protect the packs customers still need. Once replacement inventory is secure, surplus packs can go toward cards without leaving the store empty.

Later, the choice becomes less about immediate survival and more about what I am trying to accomplish. A player collecting thousands of cards has a different reason to open packs than someone trying to fund the next product license, and a player finishing a Tetramon deck may care more about four useful copies than the market value of every pull.

<h2 id="tetramon">

Cards can now become part of a playable **50-card Tetramon deck**.

The practical change for the shop is simple: after level 7, automatically selling every duplicate can remove cards that would have been more valuable in a playable deck.

<h2 id="grading">
  Grade cards when the shop can afford to wait for them
</h2>

Grading can increase card value substantially, but it uses money now and returns the card later.

That trade is awkward in a small store. A valuable card may be an excellent grading candidate while still being the wrong submission today because the shop needs cash for stock, bills, or another purchase.

Once normal inventory is funded comfortably, grading becomes easier to run alongside the rest of the business.

There is also more to the decision than sending every expensive card away. Turnaround time, service cost, raw value, graded value, and regrading risk all matter. A Grade 9 may already be worth selling, while another card may have enough Grade 10 upside to justify another attempt.

For those decisions, including when to sell raw and when a regrade is worth the risk, see <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/card-grading">what cards are worth grading</Link>.

<h2 id="ascension">
  Ascension becomes a new stock and collection choice at levels 18 and 32
</h2>

Ascension does not wait until the extreme end of shop progression.

The **32-count Ascension license becomes available at shop level 18**, followed by the **64-count license at shop level 32**.

Reaching those levels does not mean I immediately spend everything on Ascension stock. Packs still compete with ordinary merchandise for cash, and opening them converts sellable inventory into uncertain card value.

Once the shop can comfortably support the stock, Ascension starts feeding several activities at the same time: retail sales, collection progress, valuable pulls, grading candidates, and Tetramon cards.

The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/ascension-cards">Ascension card route</Link> covers the 32-count and 64-count unlocks, how to stock the packs, when opening makes sense, how many playable copies to keep, and what to do with expensive pulls.

<h2 id="achievements">
  Let normal shop play carry as many achievements as possible
</h2>

TCG Card Shop Simulator currently has **50 achievements**, and many of them advance while the store is already growing.

Shop levels, licenses, employees, expansions, customers, pack openings, card collecting, grading, Tetramon, and expensive sales all contribute to different parts of the list.

The easiest achievements arrive naturally. The ones worth watching are the counters that can fall behind when the shop becomes more efficient.

Manual checkout is one example. Once employees take over the register, the player's own checkout count can stop growing quickly. Grading has the opposite risk: delaying it for too long can leave a large Gem Mint grind waiting much later.

Collection and pack-opening totals also become much larger than the opening hours suggest.

When the store is stable enough to start cleaning up long counters, the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">50-achievement tracker</Link> shows which requirements still need deliberate progress.

<h2 id="what-to-do-next">
  What should I do next when the shop feels stuck?
</h2>

The symptom usually tells me more than the newest item in the unlock menu.

**An active objective will not disappear.**  
Read the wording again and perform the exact requested action before buying something unrelated.

**Several products are constantly empty.**  
Stop optional spending, rebuild stock, and consider whether restocking labor has become the real bottleneck.

**Customers are shopping, but money never accumulates.**  
Check <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">pricing, stock depth, card sales, and cash flow</Link> before adding another expense.

**A new system just unlocked and I am unsure whether to buy it.**  
Use <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/progression-guide">the current shop bottleneck</Link> to decide whether the next purchase should be stock, labor, space, or something else.

**The Workbench is open, but I do not know which cards to keep.**  
Start with the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tier-list">Tetramon card comparisons</Link>.

**My 50-card deck is legal but still gives terrible hands.**  
Check the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/deck-builder">deck balance and evolution structure</Link>.

**I want a build that has already worked in real matches.**  
Compare the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/best-decks">tested Tetramon decks</Link> before rebuilding from scratch.

**The deck is ready but I keep making mistakes during matches.**  
Review <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/tetramon-duel-master">Duel Master rules and match decisions</Link>.

**A valuable card might be worth grading.**  
Compare the raw sale with the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/card-grading">grading cost, wait, and possible result</Link> before sending it away.

**Ascension packs have become available.**  
Check <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/ascension-cards">whether to stock, open, keep, sell, or grade the cards</Link> before turning the entire shipment into opened packs.

**The business runs smoothly and only huge counters remain.**  
Use the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement list</Link> to find the goals that normal play has not already finished.

The game becomes much easier to manage once every unlock stops feeling equally urgent. Empty shelves call for inventory, repeated manual work calls for labor or equipment, a crowded floor calls for space, and a healthy store finally gives card collecting, grading, Ascension, and Tetramon room to grow without starving the business.
