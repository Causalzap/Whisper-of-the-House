<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Grade cards with enough upside to justify the fee and waiting time. Sell cheaper cards raw when you need cash now, and use the slower grading service unless getting the card back sooner is worth the extra cost.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="how-to-grade">
  How do you grade cards in TCG Card Shop Simulator?
</h2>

Open **Grading**, start a **New Submission**, then add the cards you want to send away. When selecting cards for the batch, I check two things before confirming it: what the card is worth raw and how much money the shop will still have after the submission.

The grading screen then gives me different turnaround options.

An early submission showed a **12-day** service alongside faster choices. The faster services cut several in-game days from the wait, but the price climbed sharply enough that speed became a separate decision rather than an automatic upgrade.

The submitted cards are tied up until the order returns. That matters in a small shop. A card cannot help the singles table, provide immediate cash, or fill another use while it is sitting in a grading submission.

For that reason, grading becomes much easier to use once the store can pay for normal stock and still leave money available for a batch.

<h2 id="what-is-worth-grading">
  What cards are actually worth grading?
</h2>

Raw price is the first filter, but I do not use a fixed dollar cutoff.

A **$61 full art** was a good example of a card I would simply sell. Grading was available, but the shop needed the sale more than it needed another card locked away for several days. The card was priced at roughly **$75** after a markup and sold without creating another grading expense.

At the other end, a card around **$400 raw** had enough upside to justify the wait.

That submission returned as a **Grade 9** and showed a value of about **$825**. It was then listed at **$1,000**.

Before the result came back, the same card's Grade 10 price was shown at close to **$3,000**.

That does not mean I assume every expensive raw card will become a 10. It shows why I check the graded prices first: the difference between Grade 9 and Grade 10 can be much larger than the difference between raw and an ordinary graded result.

My practical check is:

1. Look at the raw value.
2. Look at the values shown for higher grades.
3. Compare the possible upside with the submission cost.
4. Ask whether the shop can leave that money and card untouched for the chosen number of days.

A card can have attractive graded prices and still be the wrong card to submit today.

<h2 id="grade-or-sell">
  Grade the card or sell it raw?
</h2>

The decision changes with the shop's cash position.

| Situation | What I do |
| --- | --- |
| Low-value card and the shop needs cash | Sell raw |
| Valuable card with a large graded-value jump | Grade |
| Good grading candidate but restocking is underfunded | Sell or wait |
| High-value card that is not needed for current expenses | Grade |
| Card needed for an active Tetramon deck | Keep enough playable copies first |
| Grade 9 with a huge Grade 10 premium | Consider regrading only if I can accept losing value |

One transaction made the cash-flow side especially clear.

A valuable customer card was bought for roughly **$520**. Grading it was considered, but too much money was already tied up elsewhere and other submissions were still several days from returning. Instead of sending another card away, it was put straight on the singles table at about **$806**.

The immediate sale was more useful than chasing a better theoretical return later.

That is the point where grading and normal shop economics meet. When shelves, bills, licenses, or the next restock are competing for the same money, the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/money-guide">money decisions</Link> come first.

Grading works best with money I can afford to leave unavailable.

<h2 id="grading-speed">
  Is faster grading worth the extra money?
</h2>

Usually, I pay for speed only when the return itself has an immediate purpose.

One batch presented a faster option at **$1,000** while a slower choice was **$200**. Larger batches can push premium grading much higher; another submission reached a **$12,000** premium option.

Those are batch examples rather than universal fixed prices, because the submission itself changes the total.

The important comparison is the same every time:

**How much am I paying to recover these cards a few days sooner?**

For ordinary resale, waiting is often fine. The shop continues operating while the submission runs, and the saved money can stay in inventory.

Expedited grading becomes easier to justify when:

- the card has very high resale value;
- the shop already has plenty of operating cash;
- the return is needed for a specific short-term goal;
- or several valuable cards are being held up by one batch.

It becomes hard to justify when the faster fee is competing directly with tomorrow's merchandise.

A **12-day** wait sounds long early in a save, but losing several hundred or several thousand dollars from operating cash can slow the store more than waiting does.

<h2 id="batch-submissions">
  Send batches instead of grading one card at a time
</h2>

The first grading experiment used a single card just to see how the system worked.

That quickly exposed an inefficient part of sending one card alone: the submission still has a turnaround period, while the rest of the collection continues producing more grading candidates.

Once I know I will grade several cards, I prefer to build a batch.

That keeps me from repeatedly checking the menu, paying for speed simply because another card appeared, or creating several overlapping submissions that all tie up different pieces of the collection.

I still leave cards out when I have a reason.

A valuable single that should be sold today stays out.

A card whose graded upside is too small stays out.

And after playable Tetramon becomes part of the save, I keep enough copies of important cards before sending duplicates away. A legal deck can use up to four copies of the same card, so grading the only copies supporting an active build creates a different problem. The <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/best-decks">tested Tetramon builds</Link> show which cards are doing real work in those decks.

<h2 id="regrading">
  Can regrading improve a card?
</h2>

Yes, but the new grade is not guaranteed to move upward.

A resubmitted **Grade 9** successfully returned as a **Grade 10** in one batch. Another regraded card stayed where it was.

That makes regrading tempting when Grade 10 carries a large premium, especially on a valuable card.

It can also go the wrong way.

A card being pushed toward Grade 10 later came back as a **Grade 8 instead**.

That result changes how I treat Grade 9 cards.

I do not automatically resubmit every 9.

First I compare the current Grade 9 value with the Grade 10 value. Then I ask whether the extra upside is large enough to justify another fee, another wait, and the possibility that the card returns lower.

A cheap Grade 9 usually does not need that gamble.

An expensive card where Grade 10 is worth several times more is a much more interesting candidate, provided the shop does not need the Grade 9 sale immediately.

Repeated attempts can work, but there is no reason to pretend they are free progress. Every attempt consumes time and money, and a lower result may move the card farther from the value I already had.

<h2 id="when-to-stop-regrading">
  When should I stop trying for Grade 10?
</h2>

The easiest stopping point is when the current grade already solves the reason I graded the card.

If I wanted resale profit and Grade 9 already doubled the raw value, taking the sale can be better than chasing the perfect slab.

If the card is mainly for the collection, the answer may be different. A player deliberately filling specific grades can even want results other than 10.

The achievement list also gives grading a long-term role, including Gem Mint collecting and a complete grade range. I track those requirements in the <Link href="/tcg-card-shop-simulator/achievements">achievement checklist</Link> rather than turning every profitable Grade 9 into another regrade attempt.

For normal store play, I stop when the expected upside is no longer worth risking the current card.

<h2 id="grading-mistakes">
  Why does grading sometimes make the shop feel poorer?
</h2>

Grading can increase card value while still making the current day harder.

That happens when too much cash and too many valuable cards are locked in submissions at the same time.

The warning signs are practical:

- normal products cannot be restocked comfortably;
- several submissions are still days away;
- valuable singles that could have sold today are all unavailable;
- faster services are being used simply because the shop needs its money back;
- or another purchase has to be delayed because grading consumed the buffer.

At that point I stop submitting cards and let the existing batches finish.

The system becomes much stronger once it runs alongside a healthy store instead of trying to fund the store by itself.

A good pull does not have to be graded immediately. Sometimes the right move is to sell it, rebuild the cash reserve, and wait until the next valuable card appears under better conditions.
