<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
After the disaster, restore **power and the underground elevator first**, then rebuild the **steel and silicon production chains** before spending heavily on expansion. Put extractors on the iron and silicon deposits, run them through Single Regolith Refineries with the correct oxide recipes, and feed the output into the existing smelting furnaces.

Once those materials are moving, unlock conveyors, find titanium with the Ore Detector, and push into component production. If progress stalls, check **power, refinery recipes, science, and machine connections** before building more modules.

<h2 id="early-game-order">
  Best Beginner Build Order After the Disaster
</h2>

The first real challenge in The Crust is not learning how to place a building. It is recovering from the disaster without turning the base into six unfinished projects at once.

Power, mining, research, contracts, titanium, automation, and later Hope-2 all begin competing for the same drones and resources surprisingly quickly. I get much better results by rebuilding one working production backbone before treating every new unlock as something that needs to be built immediately.

My opening order is:

1. Restore enough surface power to run the recovery infrastructure.
2. Repair the elevator and connect it to the electrical network.
3. Build the drone charging station and regain underground access.
4. Convert **five drones** into mining drones when the objective asks for them.
5. Restart iron and silicon extraction.
6. Build two Single Regolith Refineries and select the correct oxide recipes.
7. Get steel and silicon through the smelting furnaces.
8. Add conveyors to the busiest short production routes.
9. Research the Ore Detector and locate titanium.
10. Start component production, then expand into trade and later campaign systems.

That sequence matters because the game can give the impression that research unlocks are the main form of progress. In practice, a newly researched machine is useless if steel is empty, the extractor has no electricity, or drones are still carrying every intermediate resource by hand.

The tutorial leaves behind enough infrastructure to recover, so I use what survived instead of trying to recreate the original base exactly.

<h2 id="restore-power">
  Restore the Elevator Before Adding More Production
</h2>

Immediately after the disaster, electricity is the first bottleneck I solve because the useful production equipment is underground.

The recovery path begins with solar panels and batteries, followed by the elevator and drone charging station. Both need to be connected to the power network; having generation somewhere on the base is not enough if the elevator itself is isolated.

This is a common early failure point because the building can exist on screen and still contribute nothing. Before spending resources on another panel or replacing a module, I select the stalled machine and trace its electrical connection back toward the network.

Power planning also has a much longer horizon than the first few minutes suggest. A lunar night lasts roughly **14.5 Earth days**, so a base that consumes almost all available solar output during daylight has very little margin once generation disappears.

I prefer to reach stable production with some battery reserve intact. When electricity gets tight, shutting down a low-priority production module for a while is cheaper than letting the entire recovery chain fight over insufficient power.

<h2 id="steel-production">
  How to Get Steel Production Running Again
</h2>

Steel is where the post-tutorial recovery can become confusing.

The game tells you that steel production needs to resume, but the damaged base does not present it as a single “make steel” building. The chain starts farther upstream.

First, place an **Extractor on the iron deposit** and make sure it has electricity. Then send that material into a **Single Regolith Refinery** and select the **iron oxide** recipe. The refinery is a mono-refinery, so it only produces one oxide type at a time; simply constructing it does not guarantee that the correct output is selected.

From there, the processed material goes to the existing **Smelting Furnace**, which completes the early steel chain.

In practical terms, I read the route as:

**iron deposit → Extractor → Single Regolith Refinery set to iron oxide → Smelting Furnace → steel**

Silicon follows the parallel route from its own deposit and refinery.

The game explicitly moves you through two extractors and two refineries during this recovery sequence, then identifies the result as a working steel production chain. 

This also explains the frustrating situation where steel appears to be impossible to make even though the furnace is already sitting underground. The furnace is usually not the missing piece. I check the extractor, refinery recipe, power connection, and material flow first.

If one refinery is accidentally left on the wrong oxide recipe, adding another furnace only creates another machine with nothing useful to process.

<h2 id="smart-concrete">
  Keep Smart Concrete Running, but Do Not Let It Delay Steel
</h2>

Smart Concrete is available through the Pressing Module and requires only regolith, which makes it tempting to lean on it heavily during recovery.

It is useful. Walls and construction consume enough of it that having a supply ready saves time, and regolith is abundant. The Pressing Module is also good enough for the opening even though the game makes clear that it is not the most efficient long-term method.

The priority issue is timing.

When steel and silicon are still offline, construction material is not the only thing stopping progress. A large pile of Smart Concrete cannot replace the metal production needed for machinery and later components, so I keep the Pressing Module supplied without diverting all available drones into regolith hauling.

Once steel is stable, increasing concrete throughput becomes much easier to justify.

<h2 id="research-order">
  Research What Removes the Current Bottleneck
</h2>

The research tree is divided into three science fields:

- **Fundamental** — logistics, communication, and drone improvements.
- **Engineering** — mining and production.
- **Social** — colonist living and working systems.

Certain technologies draw on more than one field, and laboratories can work independently. That flexibility becomes useful later; early on, it can scatter limited science across technologies that do not help the current objective.

For the recovery phase, I bias research toward the production chain:

**Underground Mining → Extractors → Single Regolith Refineries → Conveyors**

The exact next click matters less than the rule behind it: if steel is still offline, research that improves a future colonist system is not solving today's blocker.

<h2 id="automate-production">
  Use Conveyors Where They Remove Repeated Drone Trips
</h2>

A recovered factory can still feel slow even when every required machine technically works. The usual reason is logistics.

Mining drones handle extraction, while regular drones are also busy with construction and resource delivery. Once several machines begin requesting materials at the same time, those short repeated trips between an extractor, refinery, and furnace become expensive in drone time.

The first conveyor route worth building is therefore a compact production chain rather than a base-wide network.

**Extractor → Refinery → Smelting Furnace**

The game introduces conveyors at exactly this point because the steel chain already works; the next gain comes from removing repetitive hauling and increasing throughput.

There are two reasons I resist covering the whole base in belts immediately. First, long conveyor routes cost credits while the company is still financially fragile. Second, conveyor direction matters. If a belt reaches both modules but nothing moves, check that it leaves the refinery's output and enters the furnace's input before rebuilding the route. A large network built before the layout settles only creates more places for that mistake to hide.

Drones remain valuable even after the first conveyors go in. They can excavate, work directly on deposits, support construction, and handle jobs that are not tied to one fixed production line.

The useful balance is straightforward: conveyors take predictable repeated movement; drones keep the flexible work.

<h2 id="steel-not-producing">
  If Steel Is Not Producing, Check the Chain in This Order
</h2>

When steel sits at zero, I work upstream instead of staring at the furnace.

**1. Is the iron extractor powered?**  
Extractors need an electrical connection. If the machine exists but is not mining, trace the network before changing anything else.

**2. Is the refinery receiving material?**  
An extractor that works does not help if nothing reaches the next module.

**3. Is the Single Regolith Refinery set to iron oxide?**  
It can only produce one oxide type at a time. A wrong recipe leaves the rest of the chain waiting.

**4. Can the oxide reach the Smelting Furnace?**  
Early on drones can handle the trip. After conveyors are available, check belt direction and module input/output points.

**5. Is electricity failing somewhere else in the chain?**  
Several production modules coming online together can push an already weak grid over the edge.

This is why “I have no steel” is often not solved by constructing more steel-related buildings. One disconnected or incorrectly configured step is enough to stop the whole chain.

The same diagnostic method works for silicon: start at extraction and follow the resource one module at a time.

<h2 id="ore-detector">
  Scan for Titanium After the Basic Factory Is Stable
</h2>

Titanium becomes important when the opening shifts from restoring existing production to manufacturing more advanced parts.

Research the **Ore Detector**, connect it to electricity, and use it to scan the surrounding regolith. The detector consumes battery charge when it runs, and a larger scan area costs more power. The game explicitly warns that an aggressive scan can leave the base short on electricity. 

That makes timing more important than simply unlocking the detector.

If the batteries are already falling and several new production modules have just come online, I use a smaller search or wait for a healthier power window. Finding titanium immediately is not useful if the scan destabilizes the equipment that is supposed to process it.

After titanium is located, extract it and feed **titanium together with steel into the Components Factory**. That is the first point where the production chain rebuilt after the disaster starts supporting more advanced manufacturing, and it is another reason not to treat steel as a temporary tutorial objective.

<h2 id="science-bottleneck">
  A Stalled Objective May Be a Science Problem, Not a Resource Problem
</h2>

The Crust frequently puts the visible symptom one step away from the actual bottleneck. A building appears unavailable, so it feels like a construction problem; the real issue may be that its technology is still waiting on Fundamental, Engineering, or Social science.

Before adding more production, I check three things:

1. Has the required technology finished?
2. Is it waiting for a science type I am barely producing?
3. Is another queued technology consuming the points needed by the current objective?

This becomes especially noticeable when the campaign starts asking for communications technology while Engineering projects are still running. A player can have plenty of raw materials and still wait because the relevant science income is near zero.

When one technology gates the next objective, I move it forward in the queue and let less urgent research wait. Parallel research becomes much more comfortable once the base has multiple laboratories and stronger science income.

<h2 id="credits">
  Spend Credits to Remove a Bottleneck, Not to Hide One
</h2>

Later in the opening, the Control Center gives you more ways to trade money for speed. Resources can be purchased, ready-made equipment becomes available through Crust Solutions, and external laboratories can supplement research.

I use those options when the purchase eliminates a specific delay. Buying one missing resource to finish a critical module can be sensible; repeatedly buying the same material because its production chain is broken is usually a warning that the factory needs fixing.

Credits also disappear through infrastructure and logistics, so an apparently healthy balance can shrink quickly once conveyors, electrical expansion, shipments, and new construction overlap.

The detailed shipment rules belong to the <Link href="/the-crust/contracts-guide">contracts and market guide</Link>{"."} At the beginner stage, the important lesson is simply to avoid selling or buying resources without checking what the next construction objective needs.

<h2 id="when-opening-ends">
  Hope-2 Is the Point Where the Opening Build Order Stops Being Universal
</h2>

Once Hope-2 enters the story, the base no longer has one obvious development path.

Research, habitation, communications, exploration, and contracts begin drawing from the same economy, and the decisions you make start changing which systems deserve priority. At that point, “build the next unlocked machine” becomes a much weaker rule than “fund the branch I just committed to.”

If you are deciding whether to shift toward the rescue operation or preserve the alternative commercial route, use the <Link href="/the-crust/hope-2-choice">Hope-2 choice guide</Link>{"."}

The early-game build order has done its job once steel, silicon, titanium access, research, power, and basic logistics are dependable. From there, I stop treating the base as something that needs to be rebuilt and start treating it as an economy that has to support whichever objective comes next.
