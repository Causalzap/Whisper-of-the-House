<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Build the underground Living Quarters with enough room to expand, connect its
**Electronic Board**, and make sure the room is ventilated. Then establish the
life-support chain in this order:

**ice → Ice Extractor → Ice Melter → water → Electrolysis Plant → oxygen**

After life support works, complete the resident setup with **8 beds, a
Hydroponic Farm, and a Kitchen**. When the game confirms that the quarters are
ready, continue the current objectives instead of rebuilding the room if
colonists do not appear immediately.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="build-living-quarters">
  Build the Living Quarters around the systems colonists actually need
</h2>

The Living Quarters objective looks simple when it first asks for an underground
room. The expensive part comes afterward. Power has to reach the enclosure,
oxygen has to reach the people inside it, and the same floor area soon needs to
hold sleeping, food, and support facilities.

The early objective calls for a **15×15 room**, and later asks for **8 beds**.
That is enough to give me a useful minimum, but I still excavate beyond the
bare outline when the surrounding rock allows it. A room that only just fits
the first furniture becomes awkward as soon as the Farm, Kitchen, ventilation,
and later colonist services begin competing for the same space.

I also keep the sleeping side away from noisy production where possible.
Moving a wall early costs little compared with trying to reorganize a finished
habitation block after people are already living there.

<h2 id="power-living-quarters">
  A powered base does not automatically mean a powered room
</h2>

One of the easiest mistakes here is looking at healthy batteries and solar
generation outside and assuming the Living Quarters already have electricity.

They do not.

The enclosed room needs an **Electronic Board** connected to the main electrical
network. I check that connection before troubleshooting beds, oxygen equipment,
or anything else inside the room. If the board has no working link to the grid,
adding more interior modules only gives me more inactive equipment.

There is also a base-wide limit that can masquerade as a housing problem:
**CPU capacity**. When I have the materials but the game will not let another
required module go down, I check CPU before changing the room layout.

If power and CPU are already unstable across the whole colony, I fix that layer
first. The broader early-base recovery route is covered in the
<Link href="/the-crust/beginners-guide"> The Crust beginner guide</Link>.

<h2 id="ventilation">
  Ventilation is what makes oxygen useful beyond a single supplied space
</h2>

Oxygen production alone is not the entire habitation system.

Rooms need ventilation so life support can distribute **oxygen and heat**
through the occupied space. This becomes especially important if I divide the
underground habitation area into more than one room. An additional enclosure
can exist physically and still remain uninhabitable until it is tied into that
ventilation network.

Because of that, I plan the vents while the room is still relatively empty.
Trying to thread life support around a finished Farm, Kitchen, beds, and
workstations is much more restrictive.

If an oxygen source is operating but another room still behaves as though it
has no life support, the vent connection is the first place I look.

<h2 id="find-ice">
  Life support starts at the ice deposit
</h2>

The objective talks about oxygen often enough that it is tempting to work
backward from the Electrolysis Plant. The actual resource path starts farther
upstream.

I find an **ice deposit**, place an **Ice Extractor**, and route that supply into
an **Ice Melter**. Only after the Melter is producing water do I have the input
needed for oxygen production and the Hydroponic Farm.

This is where I avoid overbuilding. If one working extraction line is already
keeping the water side supplied, a second Ice Extractor does not solve an
objective that is waiting for ventilation, beds, or food.

The useful chain is:

**ice deposit → Ice Extractor → Ice Melter → water**

Once water is reaching the next system, I move forward.

<h2 id="oxygen">
  Turn water into oxygen before increasing resident capacity
</h2>

With water available, the **Electrolysis Plant** becomes the important module.

The build interface shows the oxygen process using **100 water to produce 60
oxygen**, which makes a weak water supply easy to expose once habitation starts
drawing from it. A full water tank beside an empty oxygen reserve is therefore
not proof that life support is complete; the conversion step still has to be
working and powered.

At this stage the essential utility chain is:

**ice → water → oxygen → ventilation**

I want that stable before adding more resident capacity. Eight beds are useful
only if the room can keep eight people supplied with the systems underneath
them.

A short water interruption is also capable of creating problems in more than
one place. Oxygen and food both depend on that side of the infrastructure, so
when several resident systems weaken together, I trace backward toward water
instead of treating each symptom independently.

<h2 id="beds-food-kitchen">
  Finish the first habitation objective with beds, food, and a Kitchen
</h2>

Once the room has power and working life support, the objective moves to the
things the first residents will use every day.

The initial target is **8 beds**, plus a **Hydroponic Farm** and a **Kitchen**.

The Farm needs access to water, so its presence alone tells me very little. I
watch whether the resource chain feeding it is actually stable. The Kitchen
then gives the colony the food-service side of the room rather than leaving
stored food disconnected from the residents who need it.

This is also the point where the extra excavation from the beginning pays off.
Beds are easy to squeeze into a corner; a Farm and Kitchen are much harder to
relocate once every wall is occupied.

I leave some floor space unused even after the eight-bed requirement is met.
The next phase starts adding work, hygiene, and health demands, and those
systems matter more than making the first room look finished.

<h2 id="ready-for-colonists">
  “Living Quarters ready” and “colonists have arrived” are separate states
</h2>

This distinction saves a lot of unnecessary rebuilding.

When the game confirms that the **Living Quarters are fully equipped and ready
to accept colonists**, I treat the construction objective as complete. If no
worker appears immediately, I do not add more beds, duplicate the oxygen chain,
or enlarge the room again.

Colonist availability comes through the wider progression around personnel and
missions. In one normal progression sequence, the quarters reached their ready
state first and the incoming miners became available afterward.

So when I see the ready message, my next move is to continue whatever personnel
or story objective is active and let that progression resolve.

If I have **not** received the ready message, then I stay in the habitation
chain and find the missing requirement. If I **have** received it, the problem
has moved beyond room construction.

The larger campaign sequence around the first colonists is covered naturally in
the <Link href="/the-crust/walkthrough">1.0 story and progression walkthrough</Link>.

<h2 id="after-colonists-arrive">
  The room's priorities change once people are actually living there
</h2>

The first arrivals expose needs that were easy to ignore while the room was
empty.

A **Fundamental Science Lab** becomes valuable because colonists can work there
to produce Fundamental Science rather than simply consuming housing resources.
At that point I am assigning people to useful jobs, not just proving that the
base can keep them alive.

Health and comfort begin mattering at the same time. Showers, toilets, and
medical capacity are easier to add if the original room was not packed wall to
wall with beds. I leave room for those services before the first group arrives
rather than discovering later that the only free tiles are on the opposite side
of the habitation block.

The **Medical Block** becomes especially important once I have specialists I
cannot easily replace. If someone is already losing health, squeezing out one
more production upgrade is less valuable than giving the colony enough
treatment capacity to keep that worker alive.

This is also where I stop thinking of the quarters as one checklist. Food,
hygiene, health, workplace access, and room comfort can fail independently
after people move in.

<h2 id="living-quarters-stuck">
  Diagnose the point where the chain stops instead of rebuilding the whole room
</h2>

When the objective refuses to move, I follow the dependency from upstream to
downstream:

1. Is the **15×15 habitation area** actually complete and usable?
2. Is the **Electronic Board** connected to the base grid?
3. Is CPU capacity preventing new construction?
4. Is an **ice deposit** being extracted?
5. Is the **Ice Melter** producing water?
6. Is the **Electrolysis Plant** powered and supplied?
7. Is ventilation carrying oxygen and heat into the occupied room?
8. Are all **8 beds**, the Hydroponic Farm, and the Kitchen complete?
9. Has the game already displayed the **ready for colonists** state?

The answer tells me where to spend resources.

No water means the problem is upstream of oxygen and food. Working oxygen with
an unfinished Kitchen means there is no reason to expand the ice field. A ready
room with no workers means construction is finished and I should follow the
personnel progression instead.

That separation is more useful than judging the room by how complete it looks.
A beautifully furnished habitation block can still be one disconnected utility
away from working, while a plain room with the right dependencies can already
be ready for its first crew.
