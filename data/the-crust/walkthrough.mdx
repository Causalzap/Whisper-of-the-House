<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Get the base stable enough to stop interrupting story objectives, then push **Hope 2 → life support and Living Quarters → colonists → the lunar investigation chains**. Once several missions open at the same time, prioritize anything that unlocks a new location, specialist, research route, or transport system before spending scarce materials on optional contracts.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="campaign-route">
  The 1.0 campaign is a dependency chain, not a straight quest list
</h2>

The hardest part of progressing The Crust is rarely finding a quest marker. It is recognizing which objective actually unlocks the next layer of the colony.

The campaign begins fairly linearly, but that changes once Hope 2, colonists, surface exploration, contracts, and outside organizations are all active at once. At that point I can have several perfectly valid objectives on the screen while only one or two of them actually move the story forward.

My route is to keep the colony just stable enough that the next story task can finish. I do not wait for a beautiful factory before leaving the base, and I do not empty storage into every contract that appears. A story delivery that needs the same carbon fiber, titanium, modular parts, or advanced components as the next required building gets priority.

If the factory itself is still collapsing before any of this works, the <Link href="/the-crust/beginners-guide">beginner guide</Link> covers the opening production problems in more detail. This walkthrough starts from the point where the question becomes **what should I advance next?**

<h2 id="stabilize-first">
  Stabilize power, slag and one reliable production route first
</h2>

Before chasing every signal on the lunar map, I want three things working without constant intervention:

**steady power, clear refinery outputs, and enough storage that one blocked belt does not stop the material I need for the next mission.**

Slag is one of the easiest early problems to underestimate. A refinery can look like it is short on ore when the real problem is that its waste output is backed up. I keep a visible exit for slag from the moment a new refining line goes down rather than trying to repair the layout after titanium or silicon has already stopped.

Power deserves the same treatment. Story progression begins adding surface equipment, labs, habitation systems, vehicles, and eventually colonist infrastructure faster than the first power layout was designed to support. I leave some CPU and power headroom instead of spending both limits the moment they become available.

Contracts are useful here because they turn production into cash, but I treat them as funding rather than the campaign itself. If a timed story objective and a commercial order are competing for the same advanced material, the story route wins. The <Link href="/the-crust/contracts-guide">contracts guide</Link> covers deadlines, reputation and when a lucrative order is actually worth accepting.

<h2 id="hope-2">
  Hope 2 is the first major story gate
</h2>

The Hope 2 chain begins turning the campaign from ordinary lunar industry into an investigation.

Once the navigation materials arrive, Crust wants the coordinates preserved and the flight recorder destroyed. That is the first point where I stop clicking through objectives automatically, because inspecting the black box before destroying it reveals information that changes how the disaster looks.

I keep this walkthrough focused on the route rather than repeating the choice itself. The immediate decision and its consequences are covered in the <Link href="/the-crust/hope-2-choice">Hope 2 choice guide</Link>.

What matters for progression is what comes afterward. The recovered information starts opening coordinates, wreck sites, and questions about whether the original disaster was what Crust claimed it was.

From here, I keep a rover available. Sending every vehicle back to routine resource work can create an unnecessary delay when the next investigation appears several sectors away.

<h2 id="life-support">
  Build water and oxygen before treating Living Quarters as finished
</h2>

The story soon asks the colony to become a place people can actually survive.

The useful sequence is:

**find ice → extract it → melt it into water → provide oxygen → ventilate the room → finish the habitation requirements.**

The living area itself is only the shell. The campaign asks for a room large enough for the first group, then beds, food production and a kitchen before the colony is genuinely ready for workers. A **15×15 room and eight beds** are useful numbers to plan around for this stage, but the room still needs working life support.

I build the ice and water chain before decorating the room. A finished bed has no progression value if oxygen is still the missing dependency.

Once the room is technically ready, I continue other objectives instead of sitting at maximum speed waiting for people to appear. The arrival of workers is tied into the broader progression around recruitment, missions and specialist access; room readiness alone is not a guarantee that eight people instantly materialize.

For the room layout, ventilation, beds, food, showers, toilets and the common “everything is built but nobody is arriving” problem, use the <Link href="/the-crust/living-quarters">Living Quarters guide</Link>.

<h2 id="first-colonists">
  The first colonists change what the base is allowed to do
</h2>

Colonists are more than another resource meter. They unlock work that machines and drones cannot replace.

A Fundamental Science Lab is one of the first major examples. Once people begin arriving, I want at least one useful specialist contributing to science instead of filling the colony with whoever happens to be cheapest.

That is also when the weak parts of the habitation plan become visible. Food can exist in storage while colonists are still hungry. A room can have beds while lacking enough showers, toilets, medical capacity, or comfort. Those are not reasons to stop the campaign and rebuild everything, but they become expensive if ignored while several story missions are running.

My threshold is simple: if colonist needs are only reducing efficiency, I keep progressing. If health, hunger, or missing medical support starts threatening the specialists required for expeditions and labs, habitation becomes the next job.

<h2 id="atlantis">
  Treat the Atlantis rescue as a logistics problem first
</h2>

The Hope 2 survivors eventually make contact from the **Atlantis complex**.

This is where the campaign tests whether the factory can support something other than itself. The survivors need resources and repairs, and the clean route is to prepare the shipment before dispatching the transport rather than sending an almost-empty vehicle and discovering halfway through the chain that the missing material is still being produced.

I also keep some titanium and advanced parts out of ordinary sales around this point. Several midgame objectives begin asking for exactly the materials that are slowest to replace.

The rescue chain is worth doing promptly because it feeds directly back into the larger investigation around Hope 2, Commander Moore, and the real cause of the disaster.

The important progression point is that **the story continues after the decision**. Pick a side, reserve the production capacity its project will need, and keep moving.

Before sending the expedition, I check two things: the transport has enough range for the route, and the base is not depending on that same vehicle for an urgent delivery.

There is no benefit in reaching the story location quickly if a five-day shipment fails because its only truck was reassigned halfway across the moon.

<h2 id="commander-moore">
  Search around Hope 2 when the Commander Moore objective opens
</h2>

The wreck investigation later reveals that **Commander Arthur Moore** is not where the records suggest he should be.

That creates a separate search around the Hope 2 wreckage. I use the rover for the scans and keep following the search markers rather than assuming the first damaged suit or debris field ends the task.

This is a good moment to send the rover back to base if its cargo is filling up. Keeping every recovered resource aboard indefinitely only makes the next long-distance investigation harder to schedule.

<h2 id="midgame-parallel">
  Midgame missions start running in parallel
</h2>

After Hope 2 and the first colonists, The Crust stops behaving like a campaign where one quest always cleanly replaces the previous one.

At the same time I may have:

- an FES laboratory waiting for contact,
- an MDR signal on the lunar surface,
- a commercial deadline,
- an Aurora delivery,
- a story expedition,
- colonists asking for missing services,
- and research finishing back at the base.

This is where route priority matters more than quest order.

I use this hierarchy:

**new system unlock → main investigation → specialist or research access → colony emergency → optional contract → ordinary resource pickup.**

That order keeps the account opening new capabilities. A recovered mining system or a new science partnership often makes several later jobs easier, while one more random cache rarely changes what the colony can do.

<h2 id="mdr">
  Recover the MDR before trying to scale surface mining manually
</h2>

The **Mobile Drilling Rig** is one of the most important midgame unlocks because it turns surface resource extraction into a repeatable system rather than a series of small manual trips.

Once recovered, the MDR can operate away from the base and mine large quantities of regalith autonomously. The next bottleneck immediately becomes transport.

That is the point where Cargo Trucks and the Cargo Dock start paying for themselves. I do not keep sending a truck out and manually babysitting every return if the dock can turn the same route into an automated loop.

The exact unload behavior, route setup, and “truck arrived but nothing is unloading” problem belong in the <Link href="/the-crust/cargo-terminal">Cargo Terminal and Cargo Dock guide</Link>.

For the walkthrough, the important sequence is simply:

**recover MDR → assign resource area → provide Cargo Truck → build Cargo Dock → let the route feed processing.**

Once that works, I stop using my expedition vehicles as improvised bulk freight whenever possible.

The game explicitly frames **one laboratory at a time** as enough. That is useful because advanced production is already pulling on carbon fiber, metals, electronics, and specialist labor.

I choose the lab that advances the research bottleneck currently stopping the base. If power is the limit, I do not spend the next delivery chasing an unrelated comfort upgrade. If a required factory is still locked behind engineering work, that becomes the useful destination.

At the obelisk, the important thing is to let the analysis chain finish before treating it like a normal pickup.

The intercepted signal is encoded. Preliminary analysis comes first, then decryption, then the contact with Above Continuity pushes the investigation into a more controlled research operation. That later step asks for a portable research station, and secrecy becomes part of the mission rather than flavor around it.

I keep enough advanced materials available for this chain because stopping halfway to rebuild basic production breaks the pace of several linked objectives.

Once this section opens, I also become more selective with ordinary contracts. Cash is still useful, but the account should already have working industry; what moves progression now is completing the research and story dependencies that industry was built to support.

<h2 id="when-to-return-base">
  Return to the base when the next mission is waiting on production, not habit
</h2>

Long-distance expeditions create a temptation to keep sweeping nearby markers before coming home.

I return when one of three things happens:

**the next story step needs specialists or resources from the base, the vehicle is carrying enough material that losing more time has little value, or a timed delivery is about to compete for the same transport.**

Otherwise, I chain nearby investigations together.

That saves a surprising amount of dead travel in the middle of the campaign. A rover already near a wreck site is more valuable checking the next scan point than driving home empty because the base happens to have finished another building.

The same rule works in reverse. I do not send a rover across the map for a small cache while the story objective beside the base is waiting for that exact vehicle.

<h2 id="progression-stalls">
  If the story stops moving, check the missing dependency before researching randomly
</h2>

When a mission appears stuck, I run through the dependency behind it.

If a **building will not start**, I check CPU, power, construction materials, and whether it is still only placed in Planning Mode.

If a **shipment will not leave**, I check whether every required resource is actually inside the dispatch system rather than sitting on a conveyor or in the wrong storage.

If a **surface resource route stalls**, I check the MDR assignment, Cargo Truck, Cargo Dock and unload path.

If **colonist research is not advancing**, I check whether the required specialist exists and whether the lab is staffed and powered.

If a **story location cannot be investigated**, I check whether the objective wants a rover, drones, a cargo vehicle, or an actual expedition with specialists.

Those checks save more time than buying a random research node and hoping the mission updates afterward.

<GuideCallout title="Keep a story reserve">
By the middle of the campaign I keep a small reserve of carbon fiber, titanium products, modular components and cash instead of selling everything above current demand. Story missions increasingly ask for advanced materials on short notice, and rebuilding a missing production chain is usually slower than carrying a modest surplus.
</GuideCallout>

<h2 id="what-next">
  What should I do after the obelisk chain?
</h2>

By this point the colony should have moved far beyond the opening survival loop. Colonists are working, outside laboratories can contribute research, the MDR can support large-scale surface mining, and the campaign has shifted from proving the lunar base can function to investigating what actually happened on the Moon.

I keep following the story-marked investigations from here while maintaining enough production capacity to satisfy the advanced deliveries they request. Optional contracts are useful when the factory has spare output; they stop being useful when they consume the same components as the next research or story step.

The late campaign also begins paying off choices made much earlier, including Aurora and the different ways I handled Crust-sensitive information. I treat those consequences separately from the basic progression route rather than assuming every earlier decision is automatically an ending condition.

For now, the important rule remains the same one that gets the colony through the middle of the game:

**when several objectives are open, advance the one that changes what the colony can do next.**
