<h2 id="where-to-start">
  Where to start in The Crust 1.0
</h2>

The first question I ask is not which objective appeared most recently. It is
whether the base can afford to support that objective without breaking the
systems underneath it.

During the opening, that usually means restoring dependable power, steel,
silicon, construction materials, science, and logistics. Once those are
replaceable rather than one-time stockpiles, the campaign becomes much easier
to read because a new mission no longer forces the whole factory to stop.

From there, the progression broadens roughly like this:

**base recovery → Hope-2 → habitation and colonists → Rover exploration →
larger lunar logistics → outside research partnerships → deeper story
investigations**

That is not a rigid build order. Different contracts, resource deposits, and
story decisions can overlap, but the same rule survives all of them: the next
valuable investment is usually the one that changes what the company is able to
do.

If the damaged base itself is still the problem, the
<Link href="/the-crust/beginners-guide"> beginner guide</Link> owns the detailed
opening route. Once the base is functional and the question becomes where the
campaign goes next, follow the
<Link href="/the-crust/walkthrough"> 1.0 story and progression walkthrough</Link>.

<h2 id="what-is-blocking-you">
  Identify the blocker before expanding the factory
</h2>

Several very different problems can look like “progression is stuck” in The
Crust.

A refinery with no output may actually be waiting on upstream extraction,
power, or waste handling. A Living Quarters objective can look visually
finished while water, oxygen, ventilation, or one required facility is still
missing. A contract can remain active after launch because the shipment went to
the wrong destination. A Rover can reach the coordinates and still fail to find
the objective because the LPS search is only halfway complete.

I separate those problems into five categories:

- **production blocker** — the required material cannot be replaced fast enough;
- **base-capacity blocker** — power, CPU, storage, or logistics is limiting everything else;
- **story blocker** — a mission, investigation, visit, or decision has not been completed;
- **transport blocker** — the resource exists, but the Rover, truck, capsule, or delivery route is wrong;
- **people blocker** — the next system needs colonists, specialists, habitation, or research staff rather than another machine.

That diagnosis usually saves more time than adding another production module.
The Crust frequently puts the visible symptom one step away from the real cause.

<h2 id="hope-2">
  Hope-2 is the first major fork in how the campaign uses your resources
</h2>

Hope-2 is where the colony stops feeling like a self-contained factory.

The emergency introduces rescue obligations, sensitive information, off-base
locations, and decisions that compete directly with industrial expansion. From
this point onward, the base is no longer building only for itself; it is
supporting people and objectives elsewhere on the Moon.

I avoid treating the decision as a small dialogue choice because the practical
effect is resource allocation. Committing to a rescue or investigation thread
means preserving materials, transport capacity, and research time for the work
that follows.

The decision itself, including the immediate tradeoff around Hope-2 information,
belongs in the
<Link href="/the-crust/hope-2-choice"> Hope-2 choice guide</Link>. The campaign
route after that decision stays in the
<Link href="/the-crust/walkthrough"> walkthrough</Link> so the same story chain
is not explained twice.

<h2 id="surface-progression">
  Surface exploration becomes a second progression layer
</h2>

Once the Rover matters, progress starts moving back and forth between the base
and the lunar surface.

The factory builds the equipment, power, research, and supplies. Surface
missions then return information, locations, new systems, or story progression
that changes what the factory should build next.

The **Cargo Terminal** is the first good example. Its coordinates are not a
normal exact waypoint; the Rover has to use the LPS to narrow the location
before the site can be investigated. If that objective is the current blocker,
the full search sequence is in the
<Link href="/the-crust/cargo-terminal"> Cargo Terminal and Rover LPS guide</Link>.

Later, surface work grows beyond one hidden location. The MDR changes how
resources can be collected away from the colony, longer expeditions demand more
support, and several research or story sites can be active at the same time.

That is why I stop judging vehicles only by whether they are available. A Rover
or Cargo Truck that is technically idle may still be the asset required by the
next progression objective.

<h2 id="habitation">
  Colonists turn empty underground floor into a resource
</h2>

Factory buildings can often be squeezed into awkward corners. Habitation is far
less forgiving.

Once colonists enter the progression, the base needs room for life support,
sleeping, food, hygiene, medical care, and work. A colony that used every
underground tile for production can discover too late that free space was part
of the economy.

I start reserving a larger continuous area before habitation becomes urgent.
That decision is more valuable than perfect early factory symmetry because the
room has to expand from a simple enclosure into a functioning living space.

The detailed build order—Electronic Board, ventilation, ice, water, oxygen,
**8 beds**, Farm, Kitchen, and what to check when the room says it is ready but
colonists have not appeared yet—is owned by the
<Link href="/the-crust/living-quarters"> Living Quarters guide</Link>.

Once people arrive, the problem changes. Colonists can work in science and other
specialist roles, while health, comfort, hygiene, and profession development
start competing with pure industrial output for attention.

<h2 id="contracts-and-money">
  Contracts are useful when the factory already knows what it can spare
</h2>

Money can remove a lot of friction in The Crust, but trade becomes dangerous
when I use it to hide a weak production chain.

A one-time purchase that completes an important building can be excellent value.
Buying the same missing component every few minutes usually means extraction,
refining, throughput, or logistics needs a permanent fix.

Selling has the opposite risk. Inventory is not really surplus if an accepted
contract, the next story building, or a major production upgrade already needs
it.

Contracts add one more constraint: delivery capacity. Later **Urgent Contracts**
offer stronger rewards and reputation, but the short deadline makes transport
speed just as important as the amount sitting in storage. A factory can have
enough material and still lose the job because one truck, too few drones, or a
slow loading system cannot move it in time.

For shipment setup, landing destinations, Online Market decisions, urgent work,
reputation, and `Not enough reputation` problems, use the
<Link href="/the-crust/contracts-guide"> contracts and market guide</Link>.

<h2 id="research-partnerships">
  FES laboratories turn surplus industry into targeted research
</h2>

The three FES laboratories become one of the more useful midgame systems because
they connect lunar exploration with research rather than simply adding another
factory chain.

The important decision is not to feed all three constantly. One laboratory at a
time is enough, so I use the system to attack the research bottleneck that is
currently holding back the colony.

If power is preventing expansion, I value the research path that helps that
problem. If an advanced production building or expedition technology is the
blocker, that becomes the better destination.

This is also a good example of why mature midgame play is less about maximum
output and more about allocation. The same advanced materials may be wanted by
a laboratory, a story objective, a contract, and the next factory upgrade. The
correct recipient changes with the current bottleneck.

For now, FES cooperation remains part of the broader progression route rather
than needing a separate page. Its search intent becomes worth splitting only if
players consistently need help choosing a lab, locating all three facilities,
or comparing their research paths.

<h2 id="midgame-priority">
  Midgame is easier when every open objective does not get equal priority
</h2>

By the time colonists, contracts, Rover missions, research facilities, and MDR
logistics are all active, the objective list becomes a poor priority list.

I use four levels instead.

**First: unlocks and story gates.**  
A required technology, investigation, decision, or building that opens the next
system has the highest leverage.

**Second: failing shared infrastructure.**  
Power, CPU, water, logistics, storage, or another base-wide constraint can slow
several objectives at once.

**Third: commitments already accepted.**  
Timed contracts and operations that already have resources or vehicles assigned
should be finished before another optional project starts consuming the same
capacity.

**Fourth: convenience and long-term growth.**  
Extra production, redundancy, market speculation, and achievement cleanup are
stronger after the first three categories are under control.

This works better than a single permanent build order because the same base can
reach the midgame with very different shortages.

<h2 id="story-investigation">
  The central mystery gradually becomes as important as the factory
</h2>

Hope-2 starts as an emergency, but its information eventually connects to a much
larger investigation.

The flight recorder, explosion site, Commander Moore, outside laboratories,
distant signals, confidential assignments, and the black obelisk gradually
change the purpose of exploration. At that stage, a Rover trip is often valuable
because of what it reveals rather than what it brings home.

The
<Link href="/the-crust/walkthrough"> story walkthrough</Link> owns the route
through those investigations so the Hub does not become a second mission guide.
The useful Hub-level rule is simpler: once a story objective reveals a new
location, system, or research relationship, I usually resolve it before
spending several cycles clearing ordinary resource markers.

Information becomes a progression resource of its own.

<h2 id="later-searches">
  Later-game questions worth watching
</h2>

Several search needs become more important once the campaign economy is stable,
but they do not all need separate pages immediately.

**FES laboratory choice** can become its own decision page if choosing between
the complexes develops meaningful long-term tradeoffs beyond the current
research-allocation problem.

**Aurora** is another clear branch. It currently fits inside the campaign route,
but a dedicated Owner Page makes sense if players begin searching specifically
for Malachite vs IEF outcomes rather than simply asking what happens next.

**Planning Mode** is useful, but most current problems are better answered inside
construction and progression pages instead of isolating one interface mechanic.

**Laser Gun** is a much stronger candidate for a dedicated page once the full
event route, success and failure conditions, resource requirements, and related
achievement outcomes can all be covered from start to finish.

**Endings** should remain separate from the normal walkthrough once the final
branches and triggers are clear. Story decisions can matter before the finale
without every earlier choice automatically becoming an ending condition.

The purpose of keeping these topics here is to give them a natural place in the
cluster without creating thin pages before the search problem is large enough
to deserve ownership.

<h2 id="achievements">
  Achievement cleanup is better after the colony can absorb the cost
</h2>

The Crust has **28 achievements**, but many of the early ones arrive naturally
through a healthy campaign while the expensive or branch-sensitive goals can
pull the colony away from useful progression.

I let normal objectives carry the achievement count during the opening and
midgame. Large economic targets, long-duration goals, vehicle counts, profession
progress, full-map exploration, and event-specific achievements are easier to
clean up once power, production, logistics, and money are no longer fragile.

The complete requirements stay in the
<Link href="/the-crust/achievements"> 28-achievement guide</Link> rather than
being repeated across the Hub.

<h2 id="next-step">
  What should you do next?
</h2>

If you are still repairing the original factory, stay with the
<Link href="/the-crust/beginners-guide"> beginner progression route</Link>.

If the base works but the mission list has become difficult to read, move to the
<Link href="/the-crust/walkthrough"> 1.0 walkthrough</Link> and follow the
current story gate.

When one specific system is blocking you, use its Owner Page instead:
<Link href="/the-crust/hope-2-choice"> Hope-2</Link>,
<Link href="/the-crust/cargo-terminal"> Cargo Terminal</Link>,
<Link href="/the-crust/living-quarters"> Living Quarters</Link>, or
<Link href="/the-crust/contracts-guide"> contracts</Link>.

The colony becomes much easier to manage once every new objective is judged by
one question:

**Does this open the next stage, or is it asking the current base to do more of
something it can already do?**

I spend aggressively on the first kind. The second can usually wait until the
factory has spare capacity.
