In The Crust 1.0, rebuild a base that can reliably produce **steel, silicon,
construction materials, science, and power** before spreading resources across
every new objective.

After that, let the next commitment decide the build order: **Hope-2 can redirect

When several objectives compete for the same resources, solve the one blocking
the next progression step first.

<h2 id="where-to-start">
  Where to Start in The Crust 1.0
</h2>

The opening becomes much easier once I stop reading the objective list as a
queue of equally urgent jobs.

After the disaster, the company has electricity to restore, an underground
factory to reconnect, steel and silicon to produce again, research to restart,
contracts beginning to appear, and eventually an emergency on Hope-2. Every one
of those systems can consume drones, credits, science, power, or construction
materials before the previous one is fully stable.

The progression shape I use is:

**recover the base → stabilize production → restore research and trade → make
the Hope-2 decision → restore lunar communications → prepare for people →
support longer-range expeditions**

It is a framework rather than a rigid build order. The important checkpoint is
whether the current base can support the next commitment without breaking the
systems that are already running.

For the opening itself, the
<Link href="/the-crust/beginners-guide"> beginner progression guide</Link>{"."}
covers the detailed recovery route from power and the elevator through steel,
silicon, conveyors, titanium, and component production.

<h2 id="stable-base">
  Know When the Recovery Phase Is Actually Over
</h2>

I consider the damaged-base phase finished when routine construction no longer
requires constant intervention.

That does not mean every machine is automated or every research branch is open.
It means the essentials are dependable enough that another objective can draw
resources without collapsing the factory behind it.

A useful early checkpoint is:

- the elevator and underground production are powered;
- steel and silicon can be replaced as they are spent;
- Smart Concrete is available without consuming all available logistics;
- research is producing the science needed for current technologies;
- drones are not spending all of their time carrying materials between machines;
- the grid has enough margin for another meaningful project.

A base that passes those tests has options. A base that fails two or three of
them is still recovering, even if the interface is already offering contracts,
new research, or story objectives.

That distinction prevents one of the easiest mistakes in The Crust: interpreting
a new unlock as a command to build immediately.

<h2 id="hope-2">
  Hope-2 Is Where the Campaign Stops Having One Obvious Build Order
</h2>

The Hope-2 emergency call changes the nature of progression because the choice
affects what the company is expected to support next.

Helping the damaged station commits the base toward rescue preparation.
Living Quarters become relevant, communications have to be restored, and CRUST
provides support for the operation. The competing FASM opportunity instead
leans into the economic and industrial side of the recovery, including an
upfront payment.

The decision is therefore less about choosing a dialogue response and more
about deciding where the next block of resources goes.

If the rescue thread is the priority, I start preserving capacity for housing,
science, communications, and off-base work instead of pouring every spare
material into optional manufacturing upgrades.

If the economic route matters more for that run, the FASM opportunity has real
value precisely because the company is still rebuilding.

The consequences and immediate tradeoff are covered in the
<Link href="/the-crust/hope-2-choice"> Hope-2 choice guide</Link>{"."}

<h2 id="inside-and-outside-base">
  From the Rover Onward, Progress Happens Both Inside and Outside the Base
</h2>

Once communications work begins, The Crust stops behaving like a factory game
where every important problem sits within the walls of the base.

The Rover introduces a second progression loop. The base has to manufacture,
research, and supply what an off-base operation needs; the operation then finds
information, locations, or infrastructure required to move the campaign
forward.

The Cargo Terminal is the first place this becomes easy to misunderstand.
CRUST supplies approximate coordinates rather than a precise waypoint, so
reaching the general area does not reveal the destination automatically. The
Rover's LPS has to narrow the direction and then expose the hidden site at close
range.

The complete search belongs in the
<Link href="/the-crust/cargo-terminal"> Cargo Terminal and Rover LPS guide</Link>{"."}
Here, the more important lesson is what happens afterward: the campaign begins
expecting a base capable of supporting increasingly distant operations.

That changes how I value production capacity. A factory that works only while
every vehicle, drone, and resource remains at home is not yet ready for the
next stage.

<h2 id="habitation">
  Living Quarters Turn Spare Underground Space Into a Strategic Resource
</h2>

Industry is comparatively forgiving about layout. If another refinery fits
somewhere and logistics can reach it, the problem is usually solvable.

Habitation is less flexible.

Once people are coming to the base, underground space has to support a powered
room, water, oxygen, sleeping capacity, food production, work facilities, and
medical support. A base designed with machinery packed into every available
tile can suddenly discover that empty floor was a resource too.

That is why I begin preserving larger uninterrupted areas once Hope-2 makes
habitation relevant. The detailed dependency chain—Electronic Board, ice,
water, oxygen, beds, Hydroponic Farm, Kitchen, Fundamental Science Lab, and
Medical Block—is covered in the
<Link href="/the-crust/living-quarters"> Living Quarters guide</Link>{"."}

Once those rooms are operational, colonists stop being a construction problem
and become a longer-term workforce system. Jobs, professions, performance, and
career progression matter more from that point onward than the walls that got
them into the base.

<h2 id="money-and-contracts">
  Use Trade to Support the Factory, Not to Replace Its Weakest Chain
</h2>

Credits matter because The Crust repeatedly gives you ways to exchange money for
time: market purchases, equipment, logistics, outside services, and contracts
can all speed up a run.

The danger is solving a recurring production shortage with recurring spending.

I use market purchases when one transaction removes a specific delay. If the
same resource is going to be missing again on the next construction job, the
better investment is usually extraction, refining, throughput, power, or
logistics.

Selling has the same dependency on context. The Online Market reacts to supply,
so unloading large quantities can push the price down, while selling material
that an accepted contract or story build needs can create a much more expensive
delay than the sale was worth.

Contracts add another layer because the shipment itself has to be configured
correctly. The requested cargo, capsule settings, and landing location are not
the same thing.

The <Link href="/the-crust/contracts-guide">contracts and market guide</Link>{"."}
covers the Landing Platform, Online Market, shipment setup, destination checks,
and the case where a delivery looks broken even though the correct resource was
already produced.

<h2 id="diagnose-progress">
  When Progress Stops, Follow the Dependency Instead of Adding More Buildings
</h2>

A large part of The Crust's difficulty comes from symptoms appearing one system
away from their causes.

A refinery can appear to be the problem when the extractor feeding it has no
power. A Living Quarters objective can look finished while water or oxygen is
missing. A contract can remain active after the correct cargo leaves because
the capsule went to the wrong destination. A Rover can reach approximate
coordinates without finding the hidden location because the LPS search is not
finished.

Whenever an objective stalls, I trace the requirement backward before expanding
the base.

The question is:

**What exact condition has not changed yet?**

If a machine has no input, adding another copy of that machine is rarely the
answer. If research is the gate, surplus steel will not unlock it. If the
landing location is wrong, another production cycle only creates a second
shipment with the same unresolved logistics problem.

This also applies to CPU capacity. Once the game prevents new construction or
additional operations because the base has reached its computational limit,
that is a base-wide constraint rather than evidence that the current objective
needs more materials.

The fastest fixes in The Crust are often smaller than the factory expansion a
stalled objective initially seems to demand.

<h2 id="far-side-expedition">
  The Far-Side Expedition Is the First Major Test of the Whole Base
</h2>

After communications begin recovering, CRUST starts preparing an operation
toward the **far side of the Moon**.

The requirements show how much the game has widened since the opening. The
expedition needs **navigation data for transport** and a **life-support system
for its operators**, while the base still has to manufacture and move the
equipment that makes the operation possible.

This is the stage where I care less about peak output and more about resilience.

Can the factory continue working while a vehicle is away?

Can a construction project consume materials without emptying the next
production chain?

Can science finish the expedition technology while the rest of the base still
has useful research running?

Can habitation support people without pushing power or other infrastructure
into failure?

If the answer is no, I strengthen that weak link before committing more assets
to the expedition. The far side rewards a company that can sustain several
systems at once, not one that can produce an impressive amount of a single
resource for a few minutes.

<h2 id="story-information">
  Later Story Choices Make Information a Resource Too
</h2>

The Hope-2 storyline gradually stops being only a rescue problem.

Navigational material becomes important to the far-side operation, CRUST places
restrictions on sensitive information, and the flight recorder introduces a
decision over whether to follow the instruction to destroy the data or examine
it first.

Examining the recorder reveals incomplete data around the disaster and points
toward the far side, including coordinates connected to further research. CRUST
also notices that the information was examined, which makes the choice feel
very different from routine objective dialogue.

This is one of the places where a manual save is useful if I care about
comparing outcomes. By this stage, story choices can affect more than the next
line of conversation; they interact with access to information, corporate
cooperation, and the direction of the investigation.

The same thread begins connecting locations such as the **Atlantis Research
Complex**, the meteorite impact area, and research storage to the broader lunar
mystery.

<h2 id="achievement-progress">
  Let Achievements Follow the Base Until the Campaign Is Stable
</h2>

The Crust has **28 Steam achievements**, but they do not all belong to the same
part of a run.

The early achievements overlap heavily with normal progression: finishing the
tutorial, building a Single Regolith Refinery, surviving the first year,
excavating, depleting veins, and expanding production all happen naturally in a
healthy campaign.

The expensive and branch-sensitive goals are different. **Heroes Never Die**
requires all **12 Hope-2 survivors**. The Laser Gun alone accounts for five
achievements, including both a successful and failed event outcome. Other goals
ask for millions of credits on balance, very high company capitalization,
10 vehicles, 10 Fuel Generators, a colonist reaching maximum profession level,
50 in-game years, and a scan of the entire Moon.

Those are much easier to clean up once the company can afford them without
sacrificing campaign progression.

The <Link href="/the-crust/achievements">28-achievement tracker and completion guide</Link>{"."}
keeps the full list, requirements, hidden achievements, and long-term cleanup
in one place.

<h2 id="priority-rule">
  When the Objective List Gets Crowded, Use This Priority Order
</h2>

By the middle of the campaign, there is rarely a moment when only one system
wants attention. I resolve those conflicts with four levels of priority.

**Progression blockers come first.**  
If one technology, survey, construction job, or decision is preventing the next
major stage from opening, that is usually where resources have the most value.

**Failing infrastructure comes next.**  
Power, CPU, water, logistics, or another base-wide system can turn every other
project into a slow project. Stabilizing the underlying system often fixes
several objectives at once.

**Committed work follows.**  
Accepted contracts, active timed requirements, and resources already promised
to a current operation deserve attention before speculative expansion.

**Optional growth comes last.**  
Extra production capacity, convenience upgrades, achievement counters, and
unused redundancy are useful when the first three categories are already under
control.

This is more reliable than trying to preserve one “best build order” across the
entire campaign. The Crust keeps changing what the company is responsible for,
so the right next build changes too.

What remains constant is the penalty for starting too many unfinished projects
with the same limited pool of drones, science, credits, power, and materials.

<h2 id="later-game">
  What the Base Is Growing Toward
</h2>

The opening systems keep expanding long after their first objective is
complete.

Colonists eventually matter for work and profession development rather than
simply occupying Living Quarters. Lunar exploration grows from nearby Rover
searches into far-side operations and eventually full-surface resource
coverage. Company growth moves beyond surviving the next bill toward market
capitalization measured in hundreds of millions and eventually a billion
dollars.

The disaster investigation also becomes increasingly important. Confidential
CRUST assignments, the Hope-2 flight recorder, distant research facilities,
and the Laser Gun all pull the campaign away from straightforward factory
recovery and toward larger decisions about what the company is supporting and
why.

By then, the strongest base is not necessarily the one with the most machines.
It is the one that can absorb a new obligation without everything else stopping.

That is the progression habit I keep throughout the run: maintain a dependable
core, identify the current blocker, and expand only far enough to make the next
stage sustainable.
