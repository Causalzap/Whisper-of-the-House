<h2 id="how-contracts-work">
  A contract is not finished when the resources are produced
</h2>

The production requirement is only the first half of a contract. The second
half is getting the correct cargo into the correct shipment and sending it to
the destination named by the job.

Once I have accepted a contract, I open the **Control Center**, go to
**Contracts**, select the job under **Active**, and use **Send Resources**.
From there I load the requested amount and check every delivery setting before
letting the capsule leave.

The route is:

1. Accept the contract.
2. Open its **Active** entry.
3. Choose **Send Resources**.
4. Load the requested resource and quantity.
5. Check the capsule configuration.
6. Match the landing location to the contract.
7. Resolve anything still flagged beside **Send**.
8. Launch the shipment.

That last verification is worth a few seconds. If the required material is
already sitting in storage, the biggest remaining risk is no longer production;
it is sending a correct load through the wrong logistics setup.

<h2 id="unlock-trading">
  Get the Landing Platform and Online Market running before planning around trade
</h2>

Early on, credits can look like a problem that selling resources should solve.
That only becomes dependable after the colony has the infrastructure needed to
move goods off the Moon.

The **Landing Platform** supplies the physical logistics side, while **Online
Market** research opens normal buying and selling through the Control Center.
Until those systems are working, a warehouse full of saleable material is still
just inventory.

I let iron, steel, silicon, Smart Concrete, power, and basic research stabilize
before leaning heavily on trade. The first factory is still trying to build the
systems that make future contracts easier, so selling away every apparent
surplus often creates a more expensive shortage a few minutes later.

If those early production chains are still repeatedly choking on slag, power,
storage, or missing research, the <Link href="/the-crust/beginners-guide">beginner guide</Link> is the better place to fix the base before turning contracts into a regular income source.

<h2 id="urgent-contracts">
  Urgent Contracts pay more because the delivery window is the real test
</h2>

Urgent Contracts become available once companies start approaching the colony
directly rather than relying only on the normal contract flow. Their attraction
is straightforward: **better rewards and reputation**.

The cost is time.

These offers expect delivery much faster than a comfortable normal order, so I
judge them from the logistics backward rather than from the reward forward. If
the required material is already in storage and the shipment path is free, an
urgent job can be excellent. If I still need to unlock a factory, manufacture
several intermediate components, or wait for a long transport chain, the reward
does not matter much because the deadline is already working against me.

A useful warning sign is the number of trips required. If an order needs
something like **eight hauling cycles** and the first trip has already consumed
a noticeable part of the available time, I stop pretending the remaining seven
will somehow become faster. At that point the limiting resource is throughput,
not inventory.

The same logic applies when the only truck or delivery path is already committed
elsewhere. A contract can be fully supplied on paper while still being
impossible to complete in time.

<h2 id="reputation">
  Reputation decides which contracts are available later
</h2>

Urgent work is also tied to reputation.

Completing those jobs improves the company's standing, while consistently
failing to meet expectations or rejecting direct requests makes companies less
eager to keep offering them. Later on, some offers can be blocked outright with
a **Not enough reputation** message.

That changes the value of an easy contract. A job with a modest reward can still
be useful when the production line is already mature and the delivery is nearly
guaranteed, because reputation opens access to better opportunities later.

The opposite is true for an oversized urgent order. Taking it only because the
reward looks large, then discovering halfway through that the delivery system
cannot keep up, risks more than the immediate payout.

I would rather build reputation through contracts the current colony can deliver
cleanly than use every new offer as a stress test.

If the UI shows **Contract limit exceeded**, I clear or finish existing contract
commitments before changing production. More stock does not create another
active slot.

<h2 id="online-market">
  The Online Market is for surplus and one-time shortages, not every production problem
</h2>

The Online Market behaves differently from a contract.

A contract pays for meeting a defined resource, amount, and destination. The
market lets me buy and sell without that fixed job structure, but prices respond
to supply and demand.

Before selling, I subtract everything the colony has already promised to use:
active construction, accepted contracts, the next important production upgrade,
and story objectives that consume the same material. What remains after those
commitments is the surplus I am comfortable putting on the market.

This matters most with shared materials such as steel, titanium products, Smart
Concrete, and advanced components. A healthy storage count can disappear very
quickly when a new factory, power expansion, and contract all begin pulling from
the same line.

Buying resources is strongest in the opposite situation: one purchase removes
a specific delay immediately.

If a required building is waiting for a handful of components and buying them
saves a long research or manufacturing detour, I spend the credits. When the
same input has to be purchased repeatedly, the market is no longer solving the
problem; it is financing a production chain that still needs to be fixed.

<h2 id="market-vs-contract">
  Reserve contract cargo before deciding what the market can have
</h2>

Contracts and market sales compete for the same storage, which is where a
profitable-looking decision can quietly sabotage another one.

Once I accept a contract, I mentally remove its required quantity from available
inventory. That stock is already committed even if it is still physically
sitting in the warehouse.

Only the amount above that reserve is considered for market sale.

The rule becomes more important after several advanced chains come online. A
single titanium or component line may simultaneously be feeding construction,
story progression, and outside orders. Selling because the current market price
looks attractive can turn a nearly complete contract into another production
wait.

The campaign itself gets the same protection. If the next required building is
waiting for a scarce advanced component, I keep that material out of optional
trade until the progression dependency is covered. The broader order of those
story objectives is handled in the <Link href="/the-crust/walkthrough">1.0 story and progression walkthrough</Link>.

<h2 id="contract-not-completing">
  Correct cargo can still fail if the destination is wrong
</h2>

The most frustrating contract stall happens after the factory has apparently
done everything correctly.

The requested resource is ready. The capsule leaves. The contract remains under
Active.

When that happens, I look at the **landing location first**.

A shipment sent to the wrong destination does not become valid just because its
cargo matches the contract. Producing the same resources a second time only
repeats work that was never the real problem.

My check order is:

1. Does the landing location match the contract destination?
2. Am I sending from the correct **Active** contract?
3. Does the capsule contain the exact requested resource and quantity?
4. Is the capsule configuration valid?
5. Is any required action beside **Send** still unresolved?

Production comes after those checks, not before.

If the cargo is already available but the job will not register, another
extractor or refinery cannot repair a delivery configuration error.

<h2 id="delivery-throughput">
  Later contracts are often limited by loading speed, not factory output
</h2>

A mature base eventually reaches a point where the material exists faster than
the contract system can move it.

That is when logistics upgrades become more valuable than another copy of the
same production module.

**Cargo drones** are flexible and can help move materials into the shipment
system, while the **Flight Control Center** can automate capsule loading through
its conveyor output. Once contract volume grows, that automation removes a lot
of the manual handling between “resource produced” and “shipment ready.”

I watch where the queue is forming.

If storage stays empty because the factory cannot produce enough, I improve
production. If storage is full while capsules wait for loading, the factory has
already done its job and the investment belongs on the logistics side.

This distinction matters especially for urgent work. A giant stockpile does not
help a short deadline when cargo reaches the launch system too slowly.

<h2 id="when-to-accept">
  I accept the contract only after identifying what it will compete with
</h2>

The reward number is one part of the decision. The more useful question is:
**what has to slow down for this contract to finish?**

A contract drawing from a mature line with full storage may cost almost nothing
except shipping time. The same payout becomes much less attractive when its
resources are also needed for a new lab, a power upgrade, colonist
infrastructure, or the next story building.

Before accepting, I look at four things together:

- how much of the requested material already exists;
- how quickly the factory replaces it;
- how many delivery cycles the job needs;
- what else is waiting for the same resource or vehicle.

Deadlines make those dependencies visible very quickly. When the timer is short,
a contract that starts from zero production has a much worse chance than one
whose cargo is already buffered.

This is also why I sometimes leave a technically affordable contract untouched.
Being able to manufacture the resource is not the same as having enough spare
capacity to promise it.

<h2 id="buy-resources">
  Buy a missing input when it removes one known delay
</h2>

Credits are most useful when they turn a blocked objective into immediate
progress.

If I need one advanced material for an important building and the colony would
have to research and construct an entire new chain just to produce that small
amount, the market can save a great deal of time. The same applies to equipment
that is needed once but would require several intermediate unlocks to manufacture
locally.

The calculation changes when the shortage repeats.

Buying the same input for the next contract, then the next building, then the
next upgrade is evidence that the colony is consuming more than it can replace.
At that point the permanent fix is extraction, refining, throughput, power, or
storage.

I use credits to bridge a gap. I do not plan an entire production economy around
continually buying the same missing resource.

<h2 id="contract-rhythm">
  Contracts work best when they fit around the factory instead of steering it
</h2>

Once several contract types are available, I give resources an order of claim.

**Story and committed construction come first. Accepted contracts take the next
reserve. The Online Market gets what remains.**

Urgent Contracts are the exception only when their cargo is already nearly ready
and the short deadline can be met without dismantling that order.

Before every launch, I check destination and shipment configuration one last
time. Before every new acceptance, I check whether the previous jobs are already
using the logistics capacity I am about to promise again.

That rhythm keeps contracts profitable without making the entire colony chase
whatever offer happened to appear most recently. A stable factory should make
contracts easier; the contracts should not force a complete production rewrite
every time one arrives.
