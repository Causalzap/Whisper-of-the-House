<h2 id="how-contracts-work">
  How to Complete a Contract Without Wasting the Shipment
</h2>

Once trade comes online, contracts look simple: produce the requested resource,
load it into a capsule, and send it to Earth. The part that causes trouble is
that the cargo requirement and the delivery setup are checked separately.

For an accepted job, I use this route:

1. Open the Control Center and go to **Contracts**.
2. Select the job under **Active**.
3. Choose **Send Resources**.
4. Load the requested resource and amount.
5. Confirm the capsule configuration.
6. Match the landing location to the contract destination.
7. Clear any unresolved actions beside the Send button, then launch.

That destination check matters more than it first appears. A capsule can be
full of exactly the right material and still fail to advance the contract if it
goes to the wrong place.

The action list beside Send is useful precisely because it catches that kind of
mismatch. When one line is still unresolved, I fix the configuration shown
there rather than guessing at the production side of the problem.

<h2 id="unlock-trading">
  The Landing Platform and Online Market Are Both Required
</h2>

Trade is not an instant solution the moment credits become uncomfortable. The
physical shipment still needs somewhere to leave the Moon.

The **Landing Platform** provides that logistics connection, while **Online
Market** research opens normal buying and selling through the Control Center.
Until those pieces are ready, stockpiling material purely because I intend to
sell it does nothing for the current cash shortage.

That timing changes how I use the early factory. Iron, steel, silicon, Smart
Concrete, research, and construction are still consuming resources while the
trade infrastructure is coming online. I keep those chains healthy first, then
start treating excess inventory as something the company can monetize.

<h2 id="online-market">
  Sell Real Surplus, Because the Market Price Can Move Against You
</h2>

The Online Market supports both buying and selling, but it is not a fixed-price
shop.

Resource prices react to supply. Sending more of the same material onto the
market pushes its value down, which means a large stockpile does not
automatically translate into an equally attractive large sale.

Before selling, I mentally reserve what the base already has a use for:
construction that is underway, an accepted contract, the next production
upgrade, or a story objective that consumes the same material. Only what
remains after those commitments is genuine surplus.

This is especially important with materials shared by several systems. A
storage number can look comfortable until a Components Factory, new power
infrastructure, and an active contract all begin drawing from it at once.

Buying works in the opposite direction. One market purchase can be excellent
value if it removes a specific blocker immediately. If I am buying the same
input every few minutes because the factory cannot replace it, the market is
masking a production problem rather than solving one.

<h2 id="market-vs-contract">
  A Market Sale and a Contract Delivery Are Not Interchangeable
</h2>

Both systems move resources away from the Moon, but they answer different
questions.

A **market sale** asks whether the current price makes a surplus resource worth
selling.

A **contract** asks whether the company can deliver a particular resource,
amount, and destination under the terms of that job.

That distinction matters whenever inventory is tight. If an accepted contract
needs a resource already sitting in storage, I reserve the contract quantity
before deciding how much can safely go to the market. Otherwise a profitable
sale can create a shortage in a job the company has already committed to.

The reverse problem is just as expensive: accepting several attractive
contracts while one factory chain supplies all of them. The reward numbers may
look good individually, but together they can turn the same steel, titanium, or
Smart Concrete line into a permanent bottleneck.

<h2 id="contract-not-completing">
  If the Contract Does Not Complete, Check the Landing Location First
</h2>

One of the easiest contract mistakes to misread as a bug happens after the
production work is already finished.

The requested resource exists. The capsule leaves. The contract stays active.

In a Version 1.0 run, that exact situation turned out not to be a production
failure at all. The material had been sent to the **wrong location on Earth**.
The shipment effectively behaved like the wrong delivery even though the cargo
itself was correct.

When a contract refuses to advance, I inspect the logistics in this order:

1. Does the **landing location** match the destination named by the contract?
2. Am I sending from the correct **Active** contract?
3. Does the capsule contain the exact requested resource and amount?
4. Is the chosen capsule configuration valid?
5. Is anything beside the Send button still marked as incomplete?

Only after those checks do I return to production.

If the requested material is already sitting in storage, another refinery,
extractor, or production cycle cannot fix a destination error. That distinction
saves both time and resources.

<h2 id="when-to-accept">
  Accept a Contract Based on the Bottleneck, Not the Reward Number
</h2>

The most profitable-looking contract is not always the best one to take.

What matters first is the resource that limits the base right now. A contract
drawing from a mature line with full storage is easy income; one that consumes
the same material required by the next campaign build can delay progression far
more than the payment is worth.

Titanium is an obvious example once component production opens. Steel and Smart
Concrete can create the same problem because they feed several different kinds
of expansion.

So before accepting another job, I look at three things together: current
inventory, replacement rate, and what the next committed objective needs.

A deadline makes that calculation stricter. Once I have accepted a time-sensitive
delivery, the requested production chain temporarily moves ahead of optional
expansion. I would rather finish one promised shipment cleanly and restore the
factory's previous priority afterward than leave several half-filled contracts
competing with construction for the same drones and materials.

This is also why I leave some attractive offers alone. A contract that is
profitable on paper can still be bad timing.

<h2 id="buy-resources">
  Buy Resources When the Purchase Removes a One-Time Delay
</h2>

Outside supply becomes more useful as the company grows. The Control Center can
provide resources, equipment, and other shortcuts before every relevant
production chain is mature.

The best purchase is one that closes a known gap immediately.

If a critical module is waiting for one missing input and establishing that
resource from scratch would take much longer than the purchase, spending credits
can keep progression moving. The same logic applies to ready-made equipment
when the alternative is a large research and manufacturing detour for something
the base only needs once.

Recurring shortages are different. If the next construction job is going to
need the same missing material again, another purchase merely postpones the
decision to fix extraction, refining, conveyors, power, or throughput.

That is the point where I stop asking whether I can afford the resource and
start asking why the factory cannot supply it.

<h2 id="contract-rhythm">
  The Contract Rhythm That Keeps the Base From Chasing Its Own Tail
</h2>

Once trade becomes routine, I stop treating every contract as a separate
emergency.

I keep a simple rhythm: first protect resources already committed to campaign
construction, then reserve what accepted contracts need, and sell only the
surplus left after both. When a contract is ready to ship, I confirm its
destination and configuration before letting the capsule leave.

That order prevents two common losses at once. The company does not sell away a
resource it was about to need, and it does not manufacture a second batch for a
contract whose first batch was simply sent to the wrong place.

Contracts become much more useful once they fit around a stable factory rather
than constantly forcing the factory to change direction.
