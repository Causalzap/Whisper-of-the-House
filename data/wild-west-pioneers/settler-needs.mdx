  within that household's usable range, then keep Satisfaction high enough to

A **60 Satisfaction** house beside another household that could reach **80**
was the point where this system stopped looking like a simple happiness meter
to me.

Basic survival gets a household started, but it does not determine the whole
result. Housing quality, Whims, nearby objects, and later services can all
change how satisfied a household becomes. That matters beyond keeping a number
green: low morale can push residents toward leaving, while a settlement with
high Satisfaction can attract new pioneers.

The order I use is simple:

**cover basic needs → read the household's Whim → satisfy it within range →
improve housing or services when the current setup becomes the limit**.


<h2
  id="basic-needs"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Satisfaction Starts With Shelter and Food
</h2>

The first warning arrives before decorations matter. Jesse reports that the
settlers are exhausted, their morale is falling, and they may leave unless the
town starts taking care of them.

The first checks are **shelter and food**. Keeping people housed and fed allows
Satisfaction to recover; only after those basics are covered does the game
start pushing harder into Whims.

This distinction matters when Satisfaction looks like the problem but the real
failure sits earlier in the settlement. Decorations cannot compensate for a
town that simply has no food.

<h2
  id="whims"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Whims Appear After the Household Has Been Established
</h2>

Once the basic needs are under control, inhabited homes begin producing
**Whims**. An exclamation marker appears over a house after it has been standing
for a while, and opening that household shows what its residents currently want.

The early requests are deliberately small and personal. One household wanted a
**fence** beside the home.

  <Image
    src="/images/wild-west-pioneers/wild-west-pioneers-whim-fence.webp"
    alt="Wild West Pioneers household Whim asking for a fence to increase resident satisfaction"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    A fence request is a household Whim: place a qualifying fence close enough to that residence and the request can be fulfilled.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Other homes asked for **lighting**, **animals**, or eccentric decoration. The
important part is that these are household-specific requests rather than a
single town-wide build order.

That changes how I spend on them. I open the house first, read what the current
Whim actually asks for, and only then place the object. Filling the settlement
with random decorations before a household requests them wastes money and
space without solving the specific Satisfaction issue in front of you.


<h2
  id="whim-range"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  The Right Item Still Has to Reach the Right House
</h2>

A matching object somewhere in town is not enough. Whim fulfillment uses a
local range around the residence.

This is most obvious with requests such as **Eccentric**, where a suitable
decorative object can satisfy the household only when it is placed within the
relevant area. Lighting works the same way conceptually: the house asking for
light needs the qualifying lamp or lantern close enough to count for that home.

That creates a useful placement rule:

**read the Whim → choose the qualifying object → place it for that household,
not merely somewhere in the settlement.**

Later housing-related Ideas can improve this system directly. In the run shown
here, **Part of the Community** increased Whim fulfillment range by **10%**.

That kind of upgrade becomes more useful once the housing block is dense enough
for nearby objects and services to serve several homes. During the opening, I
would still solve the actual requested Whims first rather than spend Journey
Points on range simply because the Idea is available.


<h2
  id="service-whims"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Some Whims Eventually Require More Than a Decoration
</h2>

The cheap early Whims are not the whole system.

A later household wanted a **Hairstyle**. That request could not be solved by
dropping another fence, animal, or lamp beside the house; it pointed toward the
**Barber Shop**.

  <Image

    alt="Wild West Pioneers Hairstyle Whim leading to the Barber Shop while the settlement lacks enough planks"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    A Hairstyle Whim can turn a household request into a real construction decision because it depends on the Barber Shop.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

This was also a good example of when **not** to satisfy a Whim immediately. The
Barber Shop needed **35 planks** in that run, while the settlement only had
**10** available.

Spending the next batch of planks on one household request would have competed
with larger settlement priorities. I would let that Whim wait until plank
production recovers unless Satisfaction is already becoming a broader
population problem.

The useful distinction is cost:

- a fence or lamp can be a cheap local fix;
- an animal or specialty object may need a specific purchase or unlock;
- a service-based Whim can require an entire building and a meaningful amount
  of processed material.

When the required service itself is still unavailable,
<Link href="/wild-west-pioneers/progression-guide">check its current Idea or progression requirement before rebuilding the residential district</Link>.


<h2

  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Better Housing Raises the Ceiling, Not Just the Population Count
</h2>

Whims are only one part of household Satisfaction.

Later in the same settlement, one house showed **60 Satisfaction**, while a
better housing level showed a potential of **80**. That makes housing quality
relevant even after the immediate shelter objective has been completed.

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    A better housing level can support a higher Satisfaction result; in this settlement the comparison was 60 versus a potential 80.

Housing Ideas can change capacity as well. **A Little Cramped** increased a
home from **5 residents to 7**, adding two more pioneer spaces. That is useful
for growth, but it also means the same district is supporting more people and
can put more pressure on food and services.

I treat housing upgrades as a capacity decision rather than an automatic
purchase. Extra beds are valuable when the town needs workers and can feed
them. They are much less useful when the current residents are already outside
service range or the food economy is struggling.


<h2
  id="newcomers"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  High Satisfaction Turns Into Population Growth
</h2>

The payoff for keeping residents satisfied is not just a happier existing
population.

The game explicitly connects high settlement Satisfaction with attracting more
people. Later, that showed up as an actual newcomer event: pioneers arrived
because they had heard about the settlement's **happy life**, and accepting the
group added **five more people**.

    alt="Wild West Pioneers newcomer event triggered by the settlement's happy life with five new pioneers available"
    width={1600}

  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    High Satisfaction can attract newcomers; this arrival offered five additional pioneers.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

That sounds purely positive until the population arrives.

Five new workers can solve a labor shortage, but they also need somewhere to
live and enough food to keep the same Satisfaction loop intact. In a later
growth phase, the settlement was already down to only **one free housing
space**, so another population event would immediately create new housing
pressure.

Before accepting growth as free labor, I check three things:

- **free housing capacity**;
- **food supply and Food Shop coverage**;
- **whether the extra workers have jobs that justify the additional demand**.

When those three are ready, Satisfaction becomes one of the cleanest ways to
turn a stable settlement into a larger workforce.


<h2
  id="satisfaction-stalled"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When Satisfaction Stops Rising, Read the Household First
</h2>

A stalled Satisfaction number does not always call for another building.

Start with the specific home. A missing basic need sends you back to food or
service access. A Whim marker tells you to inspect the household request and
its range. A Hairstyle-style request may reveal that the next improvement
depends on a service building rather than another decoration.

Only after those checks do I spend heavily on housing upgrades or additional
services.

That order keeps a cheap fence from turning into an unnecessary construction
project, while still giving the settlement a clear route from basic survival
to higher Satisfaction and steady population growth.
