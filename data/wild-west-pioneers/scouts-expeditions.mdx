  <GuideQuickAnswer title="How Do You Send Scouts and Start a Second Settlement in Wild West Pioneers?">

  Build the **Scout Cabin**, free **five pioneers**, and load canned food into
  their expedition before choosing a destination on the World Map. Later, use
  the **Settler Base** when you are ready to send people and startup resources
  to a permanent second settlement.

  </GuideQuickAnswer>
</section>


<div className="my-4 text-sm text-slate-400">
  Last updated: September 25, 2026.
</div>


Scouting is the first time Wild West Pioneers asks the settlement to spend
people and supplies on something that happens entirely away from town. A new
production building keeps its workers inside the economy, while a scouting
party temporarily removes them. Five pioneers can be a meaningful share of the
first town's workforce, and canned food becomes part of the travel cost as
well.

That makes the Scout Cabin a transition point rather than an automatic
"send now" button. I wait until those pioneers can leave without logging, food,
or logistics collapsing behind them.


<h2
  id="first-expedition"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  The First Expedition Costs Five Pioneers
</h2>

Open the **Scout Cabin** once construction is complete. The first scouting
group shown here requires **five pioneers**, and that number matters more than
the button itself.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/wild-west-pioneers/wild-west-pioneers-scouting-expedition-requirements.webp"
    alt="Wild West Pioneers Scout Cabin panel requiring five pioneers and canned food for a scouting expedition"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    priority={true}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The first scouting party takes five pioneers out of the settlement workforce and also needs travel food.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Five workers pulled from a small opening population can empty a Logger's Cabin,
Kitchen, Warehouse, or another building that is currently keeping the town
stable. I would rather reduce a nonessential workplace deliberately than
discover afterward that the expedition has taken the people supporting the
main food or wood chain.

If the Scout Cabin itself has not unlocked yet, the blocker is still
progression.
<Link href="/wild-west-pioneers/progression-guide">Follow the current Journey requirement until Scout Cabin becomes available</Link>.


<h2
  id="scout-rations"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Owning Canned Food Is Not the Same as Loading the Expedition
</h2>

The next failure state is easy to misread because settlement inventory and
expedition inventory are not the same thing. Scouts use **canned food as
rations**, but simply having that food in town does not prepare the party to
leave.

In one later trip, the settlement had **52 canned food** available and the
scouting party still could not depart. The missing step was assigning that food
to the expedition itself.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/wild-west-pioneers/wild-west-pioneers-scout-rations.webp"
    alt="Wild West Pioneers Scout Cabin expedition with canned food added as travel rations"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Canned food must be loaded into the expedition before the scouts can travel; settlement stock alone does not prepare the trip.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The distinction is simple: **available pioneers solve the team requirement;
loaded canned food solves the travel requirement.** A settlement that barely
produces enough canned food for itself is not ready for constant expeditions
either. When scouting rations keep competing with the town's basic supply,
<Link href="/wild-west-pioneers/production-chains">fix the resource flow before turning canned food into a permanent expedition expense</Link>.


<h2
  id="world-map"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  From Scout Cabin to World Map
</h2>

Once people and rations are ready, the action moves away from the settlement.
The route is:

**Scout Cabin → create expedition → World Map → select the scouts → choose a
location → send**

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/wild-west-pioneers/wild-west-pioneers-scout-world-map.webp"
    alt="Wild West Pioneers World Map with a scouting expedition selected and a destination ready to be explored"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    On the World Map, select the scouting party first, then choose the destination you want them to investigate.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The party travels rather than appearing at the destination immediately. Early
on, I follow the location tied to the active objective before spending limited
rations on unrelated markers. Scouting eventually opens plenty of places worth
visiting, but the first few trips are still competing against a young
settlement for workers and supplies.


<h2
  id="explore-location"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Arrival Does Not Complete the Location
</h2>

A World Map marker becomes a small interactive scene after the scouts arrive,
and that is where exploration actually happens. Move across the location and
look for **people, gatherable resources, and objects connected to the current
objective**.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/wild-west-pioneers/wild-west-pioneers-scout-location-interactions.webp"
    alt="Wild West Pioneers scouting location with people and gatherable objects that can be clicked during exploration"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    World locations are interactive scenes rather than automatic reward screens; people and objects in the environment can both matter.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The Margaret location shows the pattern clearly. Talking to the people there
moves the objective forward, while nearby gatherable items can also be brought
back for the settlement. Other locations use the same idea in less obvious
ways: one treasure trip required finding a shovel before digging could
continue, while another outpost advanced the wider story after the relevant
remains were inspected.

When the scouts have arrived and nothing seems to happen, I search the scene
before returning home. The missing step may be a person or object at the
location rather than another unlock back in town.


<h2
  id="what-scouting-brings-back"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A Scouting Trip Can Return More Than Story Progress
</h2>

Exploration is not only a way to reveal the next marker. Depending on the
location, scouts can return with **resources, money, weapons, or other useful
items** collected during the trip, while locations can also introduce new
people and push broader objectives forward.

A completed expedition therefore has value in two directions: it expands the
map and can return something useful to the settlement that funded the trip.
The five pioneers matter here too, because finishing the location puts those
workers back into ordinary settlement jobs.

Before sending the same party out again, check its rations. A newly available
destination does not automatically mean the expedition is resupplied.


<h2
  id="settler-base"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Settler Base Turns Exploration Into Permanent Expansion
</h2>

Scouts visit a location and return. A **Settler Base** makes a much larger
commitment by creating a group that can leave the first town and establish
another settlement at a designated site.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/wild-west-pioneers/wild-west-pioneers-settler-base.webp"
    alt="Wild West Pioneers Settler Base panel used to form a group for establishing a new settlement"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Settler Base is the handoff from temporary exploration to sending population and supplies away permanently.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

In the run shown here, the settler setup could not proceed while only **15
pioneers** were available. Once the settlement reached **20**, the group could
be prepared, and the setup also called for **20 cash and 20 canned food**.
Those numbers make the readiness test fairly clear: a town that only functions
because every worker is already assigned does not have surplus population for
expansion, and the same is true when canned food or construction materials are
already under pressure.

By the time Settler Base becomes practical, I want the original settlement to
be producing enough surplus that losing people and startup resources creates a
smaller economy, not a crisis.


<h2
  id="settler-resources"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  The Second Settlement Starts Before the Group Leaves
</h2>

Settler Base allows you to choose **resources for transport** before departure,
and that setup matters because the destination may not resemble the first town
at all.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/wild-west-pioneers/wild-west-pioneers-settler-expedition-resources.webp"
    alt="Wild West Pioneers Settler Base resource selection for supplies being transported with a settler group"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The settler group can carry startup resources, letting the original town support the first stage of the new settlement.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

I use that transport list as a preview of the next town's opening economy.
Storage, housing, water access, and the first practical production buildings
all need materials, so sending a group with the bare minimum may get twenty
people to the site while leaving them unable to build what that terrain
actually demands.

A useful startup package is worth delaying departure for, especially when the
destination was chosen for one valuable resource rather than because it has a
balanced opening environment.


<h2
  id="sandstorm"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Sandstorm Shows Why the Second Town Needs Imported Support
</h2>

The Sandstorm settlement makes the value of preparation obvious. The group
arrives because the location is strategically useful for **gold**, not because
it offers another comfortable version of the first map.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/wild-west-pioneers/wild-west-pioneers-second-settlement-sandstorm.webp"
    alt="Wild West Pioneers settler group arriving at Sandstorm to establish the second settlement"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Sandstorm offers a reason to expand through gold, but its opening wood and food situation is much weaker than the original settlement.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

On arrival, the immediate terrain check revealed **very little forest and very
limited obvious food** around the initial settlement area, while water also had
to be considered again rather than assumed from the first town's setup. The
question is no longer "How do I copy my first settlement?" but "What does this
location lack, and which of those gaps should the original town cover?"

At Sandstorm I would inspect, in roughly this order:

- the resource that justified settling there;
- water access;
- local wood;
- local food;
- the imported stock available to bridge whatever the site cannot provide yet.

The same spatial rules from the first settlement still help, but the answer will
not be the same layout.
<Link href="/wild-west-pioneers/best-settlement-layout">Build the new footprint around the resources that actually exist at that location</Link>.


<h2
  id="settlement-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  The Goal Is a Network, Not Two Identical Towns
</h2>

The second settlement makes more sense once you stop expecting both towns to
produce the same things. Settler Base already introduces resource movement as
part of expansion, while the later **Logistics Center** is tied to
transportation between settlements.

That changes the economic role of the original town. It can send startup
materials into a harsh location, while the new settlement develops around the
resource that made the trip worthwhile in the first place. At Sandstorm, that
reason is gold.

Basic survival still comes first: people need housing, water, food, storage,
and enough labor to operate the new town. Beyond that baseline, copying every
production building from the first settlement only makes sense when the local
economy actually needs it. Multiple settlements become valuable because their
resource strengths are different.


<h2
  id="scouting-flow"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  From the First Scout Trip to the Second Town
</h2>

The whole expansion sequence can be read as one continuous change in
commitment:

**five pioneers leave temporarily**

→ **the World Map opens**

→ **locations reveal people, resources, and objectives**

→ **the scouts return**

→ **a suitable settlement site becomes meaningful**

→ **Settler Base sends population and startup resources permanently**

→ **the second town develops around its own resource conditions**

That sequence is the real difference between scouting and expansion. Scouting
spends a small part of the first settlement to learn what is outside it;
settling spends a much larger part of the first settlement because you have
finally found somewhere worth building for the long term.
