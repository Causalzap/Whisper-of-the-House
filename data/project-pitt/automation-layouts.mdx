<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
I get more reliable automation in Project P.I.T.T. by treating every route as four parts:

**source → movement → containment → catch**

When something fails, I change the part that is actually failing instead of adding more Fans everywhere.

For Ducks, I start with a straight lane, staged airflow, and a wide catcher.

For Cash Registers, I expect a heavier object that catches edges more easily. I fix the landing and transition first, then use Slick Panels when friction is the actual problem.

My rule is:

**watch one product fail → identify the first bad contact → change one thing → run another batch → stop once the route no longer needs babysitting**

The helper below currently supports **Ducks** and **early Cash Register automation**. I do not use it for Piñatas or Anomalies yet.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="how-i-use-the-helper">
  I diagnose one visible failure at a time
</h2>

I get the best result from the helper when I describe the problem I can actually see.

I do not select “jam” just because there is a pile at the end of the route.

I watch one fresh product from the source until it fails.

If it stops in the middle of a clear lane, I am looking at movement.

If it reaches the destination and flies past it, I am looking at the catch.

If it hits the same corner sideways every time, I am looking at geometry.

If the product path is fine but the Fan or Panel itself moves, I am looking at hardware stability.

That is why I change **one thing at a time**.

If I move two Fans, raise a wall, rotate a Panel, and add a catcher in the same test, I may get a better result but I no longer know which change solved the problem.

My test loop is:

**stop or slow the source**

→ **clear enough products to see the route**

→ **run a small batch**

→ **watch the first repeatable failure**

→ **make one change**

→ **run the same test again**

<h2 id="four-parts">
  I build every early route around source, movement, containment, and catch
</h2>

The easiest layouts for me to repair are the ones where each section has one obvious job.

**Source** is where the product enters the system.

**Movement** is what pushes it toward the Pit.

**Containment** keeps it from leaving the intended lane.

**Catch** absorbs the final speed and keeps a successful delivery from turning into an overshoot.

If one section is doing three jobs badly, I separate it before I buy more tools.

A Fan should not need to launch a product, correct a bad angle, hold it inside the lane, and stop it at the destination.

That usually means the route is asking airflow to fix geometry.

<h2 id="start-straight">
  I make the route straight before I make it compact
</h2>

A straight route is easier to tune than a chain of diagonals.

When I moved an early Duck setup back toward a straight shot, I could immediately see where the airflow started, where the walls mattered, and where the catcher needed to sit.

I only add a turn when the room actually forces me to.

Every hard-angle transition creates another place where a Duck can bounce unpredictably or a Cash Register can catch an edge.

That does not mean diagonal layouts are always bad.

<h2 id="overshoot">
  Overshoot means I fix the final push or the catch
</h2>

If the product already reaches the destination, I do not solve the problem by adding more propulsion.

I check the final section.

My first questions are:

- Is the last Fan still pushing at full strength when the product reaches the target?
- Is there a backboard opposite the direction of travel?
- Is the catch zone wide enough for the speed of the product?
- Is the product bouncing off a hard surface and back out?

I usually change the final Fan before I touch the middle of the route.

If the product reaches the right place but hits too hard, I add Cushion at the impact point.

A Cushion Panel does not replace containment.

If a product lands softly but still has an open side to escape through, I fix the side wall rather than adding more Cushion.

<h2 id="undershoot">
  Undershoot is either a force problem, a friction problem, or a bad transition
</h2>

A product that stops short does not automatically need another Fan.

I watch **where** it loses momentum.

If it barely leaves the source, I improve the first airflow stage.

If it travels cleanly and dies on a long flat section, I look at friction.

If it reaches a corner and stops there, I fix the corner.

That distinction matters most with Cash Registers.

Slick Panels are useful when the route already points the right way and the floor itself is eating momentum.

I do not cover the whole room with Slick just because it is unlocked.

If the product is hitting an edge before it reaches the Slick section, that purchase does nothing for the real bottleneck.

<h2 id="jams">
  I treat a jam as a geometry problem until a clean test proves otherwise
</h2>

Once a route is packed with products, the pile creates its own physics.

Products block airflow, collide with each other, and make a working section look broken.

So I do not tune the route while it is fully clogged.

I clear the pile, then run a small batch and look for the **first repeatable choke point**.

The common causes are:

- a corridor that narrows too quickly;
- a hard turn immediately after a fast section;
- a low roof that gives bouncing products no recovery space;
- two streams merging into one narrow entrance;
- a Fan or Panel sitting partly inside the travel path.

My order is:

**clear the pile**

→ **run one or two products**

→ **inspect the first choke point**

→ **fix geometry or obstruction**

→ **test again**

→ only then add more airflow if the clear route still stalls

This is the diagnostic pattern the Layout Helper is built around.

<h2 id="containment">
  I contain the failure zone, not the entire warehouse
</h2>

Containment solves three different problems.

A **side wall** stops lateral escapes.

A **roof** stops products that bounce over the walls.

A **catcher or backboard** stops products that overshoot the destination.

I only build the containment I have evidence I need.

A full sealed tunnel costs more Panels, makes the inside harder to inspect, and can create a tighter jam when several products arrive together.

For Ducks, I often start with side walls and a catcher.

I add a roof after Ducks prove they can escape upward.

For Cash Registers, I am more likely to cage a fast merge or catch section while leaving slower parts of the route open.

<h2 id="sticky-tools">
  Sticky Tools solve moving hardware, not bad product flow
</h2>

I use Sticky Tools when the layout itself will not stay where I put it.

I do not buy Sticky Tools because I want more throughput.

I buy them because repeated physical movement is making the route impossible to tune consistently.

Once the pieces stay fixed, I can finally tell whether the product path itself needs a change.

This is also when grid alignment becomes more useful, but I still prefer fewer hard-angle transitions.

A perfectly snapped bad route is still a bad route.

<h2 id="duck">
  Ducks are the stable baseline for the helper
</h2>

Ducks are the product I trust most for this early diagnostic model.

They give me enough repeated tests to judge:

**overshoot**

**undershoot**

**jams**

**escaping the route**

**tools moving**

**a route that is too chaotic**

My default Duck layout is intentionally simple:

**automatic source**

→ **straight movement**

→ **staged airflow**

→ **local containment**

→ **wide catch**

If that route feeds continuously, I do not keep adding pieces just because I unlocked them.

A simple Duck line is also useful later because it can keep feeding while I work on more valuable products.

<h2 id="cash-register">
  Cash Register recommendations are experimental because the geometry is less forgiving
</h2>

Cash Registers are where the same general rules need more caution.

The product is larger and more angular, so a transition that is harmless for Ducks can become the exact place Cash Registers stack.

The first thing I check is not Fan strength.

It is the handoff.

Can the Cash Register enter the next section without clipping an edge?

Can two registers arrive close together without blocking each other?

Does the register reach the Slick section cleanly before friction becomes the issue?

When the route is already aligned and a repeatable section still stalls, stronger local airflow can make a clear difference.

<h2 id="when-not-to-buy">
  I do not buy a tool until I can name the problem it solves
</h2>

This is the spending rule I use while tuning automation.

**I buy another Fan** when a clear, aligned section consistently loses momentum.

**I use Cushion** when the product reaches the correct place but the impact or rebound is the problem.

**I use Slick** when a properly aligned transport section is losing momentum to the surface.

**I add walls** when the product leaves sideways.

**I add a roof** when the product leaves upward.

**I use Sticky Tools** when the hardware itself will not stay fixed.

If I cannot finish the sentence:

> “I am buying this because ______ keeps happening here,”

I usually wait.

That prevents a bad layout from becoming an expensive bad layout.

<h2 id="when-i-stop">
  I stop optimizing when the route can run without me
</h2>

I do not need every early layout to be mathematically perfect.

I consider a route good enough when:

**there is no growing jam**

**I am not repeatedly rescuing products by hand**

**the same escape point is not failing over and over**

**the source can keep running without overflowing the first transition**

**the route feeds enough product to keep progression moving**

At that point I move on.

I come back when a new unlock directly changes the answer — Sticky Tools, Slick Panels, a stronger Fan, or a new product that forces me to redesign the shared space.

I do not rebuild a working line just because I can make it prettier.

That is also why the helper currently stops at Ducks and early Cash Registers.

Piñatas and Anomalies create different problems, and I would rather leave those options out than pretend the same early layout rules are enough.
