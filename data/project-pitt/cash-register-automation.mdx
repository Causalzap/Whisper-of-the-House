<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
My reliable Cash Register route is:

**Cash Register source → soft landing → controlled handoff → Slick transport → local airflow → wide catch → Pit**

I do **not** copy my Duck tunnel.

Cash Registers are boxier, catch edges more easily, and punish bad transitions much harder than Ducks.

My buying rules are:

**Cushion Panel — $1,000** when the landing is rebounding or throwing registers out.

**Slick Panel — $2,000** when a correctly aligned floor section is visibly stealing momentum.

**Industrial Fan — $11,000 in one run** only when a clear, aligned section still stalls.

I stop upgrading production when the automatic machine is creating registers faster than the route can clear them.

<h2 id="why-duck-layouts-fail">
  I do not copy my Duck geometry
</h2>

Cash Registers changed the way I build the same warehouse.

A Duck can roll, bounce, and squeeze through a route that looks slightly wrong.

A Cash Register is much less forgiving.

It can catch an edge, arrive sideways, wedge into a narrow transition, or lose all momentum at a bad handoff.

So my Cash Register route starts wider and simpler.

I want:

**one clean landing**

→ **one obvious direction**

→ **as few hard turns as possible**

→ **a wide final catch**

If I already need several diagonal corrections before the first register reaches the main lane, I rebuild the entrance.

I would rather spend money on one good transition than keep buying Fans to force bad geometry to work.

For general overshoot, undershoot, jam, and airflow diagnosis, I use the <Link href="/project-pitt/automation-layouts">Automation Layout Helper</Link>.

This page stays focused on Cash Register-specific decisions.

<h2 id="manual-test">
  I use manual Cash Registers to learn the route before I scale it
</h2>

When Cash Registers first become available, I make a few manually.

I use those first products as a test bench.

I watch:

**where the register lands**

**whether it rebounds**

**whether it catches an edge**

**where it loses momentum**

**whether the final catch can actually hold it**

I do not plan the factory around one fixed product value because Cash Register value changes as upgrades increase.

The useful lesson is simpler:

once Cash Registers become valuable enough that bad landings and lost products matter, I stop treating transport as an afterthought.

That is when I build the route around their actual behavior.

<h2 id="soft-landing">
  I separate the landing from the transport
</h2>

My preferred first transition is:

**Cash Register lands**

→ **Cushion absorbs the hit**

→ **Piston or Fan moves it sideways**

→ **Slick carries it into the main lane**

This works better for me than asking one Fan to solve both problems at once.

If the landing is bad, I fix the landing.

If the register leaves the landing cleanly but stalls later, I work on the transport.

That keeps the route easy to diagnose.

I buy Cushion when the same impact is:

- causing a hard rebound;
- throwing registers out of the lane;
- damaging them immediately after production;
- making the next register land on top of the first one.

I do **not** buy Cushion when the register is simply dying on a long flat section.

That is not an impact problem.

I buy Slick when a Cash Register:

- enters the section cleanly;
- is already pointing the correct way;
- repeatedly slows on a flat run;
- would let me remove an unnecessary Fan stage.

I do not keep extending the Slick floor when the register is piling up **before** it reaches that section.

If the object cannot enter the low-friction lane correctly, more low-friction floor does nothing.

I do not wait until the route looks perfect.

The automatic source exposes the real bottleneck.

If several registers accumulate, I watch where the **first growing pile** forms.

That is the section I fix.

I do not upgrade production while that pile is still getting larger.

Paying to create the same blockage faster is not progress.

<h2 id="manual-auto-merge">
  I merge manual and automatic output only after both entrances work
</h2>

I like feeding the manual and automatic sources into the same main lane.

I do not merge them immediately.

My order is:

**test automatic entrance alone**

→ **test manual entrance alone**

→ **confirm both can reach the shared lane**

→ **run both together**

Two good entrances can still create one bad merge.

If the route only fails when both sources are active, I inspect the merge before I rebuild the whole factory.

<h2 id="industrial-fan">
  I spend $11,000 on a stronger Fan only at a proven bottleneck
</h2>

Stronger Fans started to make sense for me during the Cash Register stage.

In one setup, Industrial Fans cost **$11,000 each**.

That is expensive enough that I want a specific reason before I buy one.

I do not put a large Fan at the source just because Cash Registers are heavy.

I wait until:

**the route is already aligned**

**the register is facing the right direction**

**the same clear section repeatedly loses momentum**

Then stronger airflow is solving a real problem.

If the register is hitting a wall sideways, more force usually makes the collision worse.

If it is already moving correctly and simply runs out of momentum, the Fan is useful.

<h2 id="piston">
  I use the Piston as a controlled handoff
</h2>

The Piston is most useful to me as a short transfer tool.

I like:

**Cushion landing**

→ **Piston push**

→ **Slick transport**

I do not rely on it as a long-distance launcher.

That is not a weakness in this build.

A predictable short push is easier to tune than a giant launch.

If the register never reaches the Piston, I fix the landing.

If the Piston pushes it into a wall, I fix the angle.

If it leaves the Piston correctly and stalls later, I work on the transport section.

Each part keeps one job.

<h2 id="containment">
  I cage the dangerous sections instead of sealing the whole route
</h2>

Cash Registers can bounce out of fast transitions, so I use containment where the route earns it.

The sections I watch most are:

**the source**

**the merge**

**high-speed transitions**

**the final catch**

I start with side walls.

If registers are escaping upward, I add height locally.

I do not automatically build a sealed tunnel from the machine to the Pit.

A full cage costs more, hides the exact failure point, and can become too tight when several registers arrive together.

I would rather have a route I can still reach and repair.

<h2 id="damage">
  I only care about damage when it changes the logistics
</h2>

I do not try to make every Cash Register arrive untouched.

If a register takes a small hit and still reaches the Pit reliably, I usually leave that section alone.

I care when the impact causes a transport problem:

**hard rebound**

**escape**

**pileup**

**bad handoff into the next section**

That is when Cushion earns its cost.

A slightly rough unattended line is more useful to me than a beautiful impact-free route that needs another hour of building.

<h2 id="when-to-upgrade">
  I upgrade production only after the route survives the current rate
</h2>

The automatic machine can become expensive as I keep upgrading it.

In one Phase 2 factory, pushing the Cash Register automatic machine toward its late upgrades cost almost **$400,000**.

That same run had already fed about **1,078 Cash Registers** by the time the next phase was close.

I do not treat either number as a requirement.

I use them as a reminder that it is easy to keep spending on Cash Registers after the route has already done enough.

I buy another production upgrade when:

**the entrance clears reliably**

**the merge is stable**

**the transport is not building a growing pile**

**the extra output will actually reach the Pit**

If production is already outrunning transport, I stop.

<h2 id="phase-3-warning">
  I stop polishing the Cash Register factory when Phase 3 is close
</h2>

This is the most important stop decision on the page.

Once Phase 2 is nearly finished, I stop buying cosmetic improvements.

The Phase 3 transition can damage the room and tear through the factory I just built.

<h2 id="when-to-stop">
  I stop spending when both sources can run without babysitting
</h2>

My final Cash Register test is simple.

I turn on the automatic machine.

I add manual registers.

I watch the landing, merge, strongest transport section, and final catch.

I consider the line finished when:

**there is no growing source pile**

**the landing does not repeatedly throw registers away**

**the merge can handle both sources**

**the same transport section is not stalling every batch**

**the catch does not need constant cleanup**

**throughput is enough to keep Phase 2 moving**

Once those are true, I stop spending.

The next big investment is more valuable after the game changes the stage than inside a Cash Register factory that is already doing its job.
