<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Anomalies are the first product where I stop thinking in terms of one machine feeding one lane.

The Anomaly workbench needs **one Duck + one Cash Register + one Piñata**. Once all three are loaded, activating the workbench produces **one Anomaly**.

My reliable way to handle it is:

**keep the three older product lines alive → stage one of each ingredient near the Anomaly workbench → use the helper to hold the workbench button if I have it → move finished Anomalies out before the next batch starts bouncing around the room**

The first Anomalies I produced showed a value of about **$1 million each** before later upgrades and combo bonuses changed the payout.

I do not try to build a perfect fully automatic three-input factory immediately. The workbench itself is not a true automatic producer, and later Anomaly crates give me a much better way to push bulk money and combos.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="when-anomalies-unlock">
  I move to Anomalies when the game opens the next product channel
</h2>

Anomalies arrive late enough that I already have working Ducks, Cash Registers, and Piñatas before I touch the new workbench.

That matters because the new product consumes the old product chain instead of replacing it.

When the Anomaly channel unlocked in my run, the game immediately gave me a very different problem: the new machine was waiting for ingredients instead of simply producing on its own.

<h2 id="recipe">
  The recipe is one Duck, one Cash Register, and one Piñata
</h2>

The workbench confused me at first because pressing it with an empty input did nothing.

The missing step was the recipe.

I load:

**1 Duck**

**1 Cash Register**

**1 Piñata**

Then I activate the workbench and it produces one Anomaly.

That is why I do not dismantle the older lines when Anomalies unlock.

My Duck line can be simple.

My Cash Register line does not need to be maxed.

My Piñata line does not need the highest possible combo.

They only need to keep supplying enough product that I am not standing beside the Anomaly machine waiting for one missing ingredient.

If Piñatas are still exploding all over the room before I can get one to the Anomaly workbench, I fix that supply first in the <Link href="/project-pitt/pinata-automation">Piñata Automation guide</Link>.

<h2 id="stage-inputs">
  I stage small ingredient buffers instead of merging three full factories
</h2>

The first thing I wanted to build was a giant three-way automated merge.

I stopped myself.

Three products with different shapes, speeds, and failure modes all trying to enter the same small workbench is much harder to debug than three short supply buffers.

My simpler setup is:

**Duck buffer on one side**

**Cash Register buffer on another**

**Piñata buffer with enough room that it does not burst against the machine**

Then I feed one of each into the workbench.

I only increase the buffer size when I am genuinely waiting for ingredients.

If five Ducks are already sitting there while I am always waiting for the next Piñata, the Duck side does not need another production upgrade.

The Piñata side is the bottleneck.

This is the first factory where I judge upgrades by **ingredient balance**, not just products per minute.

<h2 id="intern-substitute">
  The helper automates the button, not the recipe
</h2>

The `Intern Substitute` is useful here because it can hold the manual workbench button for me.

It does not turn the Anomaly workbench into a normal automatic product machine.

This is an important distinction.

If the helper is attached and nothing happens, I do **not** assume it is broken.

I check the three inputs first.

That was exactly the failure that caught me: the workbench looked like it was not working until I realized it was missing the required product mix.

My order is:

**check Duck**

→ **check Cash Register**

→ **check Piñata**

→ **then check the helper**

That is much faster than moving the helper around every time the machine pauses.

<h2 id="do-not-over-automate">
  I do not build a giant three-input conveyor unless I am actually waiting on manual loading
</h2>

Anomalies tempt me into overengineering because I already have three mature product systems.

But a huge merge only pays off if loading the workbench is the thing slowing me down.

If I can keep a small ingredient pile beside the machine and make Anomalies as fast as I need them, I leave it alone.

I spend on a bigger system only when:

**one ingredient is arriving consistently late**

**the workbench spends real time idle with the other two ingredients ready**

**manual loading is taking me away from the rest of the factory**

**finished Anomalies are clearing the area without building a pile**

If the workbench is already surrounded by bouncing Anomalies, more input is not useful.

I clear the output first.

<h2 id="anomaly-physics">
  I give finished Anomalies more room than I first think they need
</h2>

Finished Anomalies are much more chaotic than the recipe makes them sound.

Once several are loose, they can bounce hard enough that adding more Fan force makes the area worse.

I had to back a Fan away because the force was simply too high.

I do not build the output chute as tightly as my Duck lane.

A narrow tunnel can turn one bad rebound into a pile of products hitting each other.

I prefer a wider clear zone immediately after the workbench, then a controlled push once the Anomaly has room to settle into the direction I want.

If the whole area becomes impossible to read, I stop production and clean it before testing again.

A chaotic pile is not useful diagnostic information.

<h2 id="vacuum-and-cleanup">
  I use cleanup tools because loose late-game products become their own bottleneck
</h2>

By the time Anomalies unlock, the room can contain Ducks, Cash Registers, Piñatas, Candy, loose tools, and Anomalies at the same time.

That clutter matters.

An Anomaly line can appear broken when the real problem is another product sitting in the path.

I use the stronger cleanup tools to clear loose products from the testing area before I make another change.

I also keep the Anomaly output zone away from the places where Piñata Candy normally lands.

The less mixed debris I have around the workbench, the easier it is to see whether the actual Anomaly route is working.

<h2 id="upgrade-priority">
  I upgrade the ingredient that is starving the workbench
</h2>

I do not upgrade Ducks, Cash Registers, and Piñatas evenly.

I watch the Anomaly machine.

If I always have Ducks and Cash Registers waiting but no Piñata, I put the next money into Piñata production.

If Piñatas and Ducks are stacked beside the machine but Cash Registers are late, I improve that line.

If all three ingredients are always waiting, I do not need more input production.

At that point I look at:

**Anomaly production speed**

**Anomaly value**

**output clearance**

That is a much cleaner upgrade order than maxing every old product just because another level is available.

<h2 id="when-to-buy-anomaly-crates">
  Once Anomaly crates are available, I stop trying to make the workbench do all the bulk farming
</h2>

The biggest change comes when I can buy Anomaly crates.

At that point I have another way to put a lot of late-game product into the factory without crafting every Anomaly through the three-input workbench.

A late-game setup used automatic striking machines to break the crates and let their contents fall into the Pit.

In one late-game stress test, I bought **20 Anomaly crates** and fed them through the breaker setup.

That run pushed the combo to **200**.

I do not treat that as a guaranteed payout or a required setup. It shows why I stop spending hours trying to turn the three-input workbench into a perfect high-volume factory once crates become practical.

The crate farm has a different problem: it can flood the area quickly.

I only scale it when the breaker zone is already contained and the products can fall toward the Pit instead of collecting around the machines.

<h2 id="what-anomalies-are-for">
  I keep a few Anomalies aside when I start following the hidden route
</h2>

Anomalies are not only a money product.

They also become relevant to the hidden progression above the main factory.

That changed how I manage the output.

Once I am ready to leave normal production and follow that route, I stop dumping every Anomaly immediately.

I keep a few accessible instead of burying them inside the largest combo farm.

The full sequence belongs to the <Link href="/project-pitt/200kg-platform-what-next">200 kg Platform and 0% guide</Link>. The useful Anomaly-side decision is simply this:

**if I am about to work on the 200 kg / 0% branch, I keep at least a couple of Anomalies where I can reach them.**

That saves me from going back downstairs and restarting the entire ingredient chain after I have already climbed into the hidden area.

<h2 id="when-to-stop">
  I stop optimizing Anomalies when supply, output, and my next objective are all covered
</h2>

I do not try to make the Anomaly factory beautiful.

I stop when:

**I can produce one Anomaly whenever I need one**

**no single ingredient constantly starves the workbench**

**the output area is not filling with a growing bounce pile**

**the helper can run the workbench without me babysitting the button**

**I have a bulk crate option when I want money or combos**

**I can keep a few Anomalies aside for late-game progression**

At that point, another elaborate conveyor is usually not solving a real problem.

If my goal is money, I use the bulk setup.

If my goal is the hidden route, I take the Anomalies I need and leave the factory.

That is the point where I stop treating Anomaly production as the destination and start using it to finish the rest of the run.
