<h2 id="reach-real-cards" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Necrobinder gets easier when the deck reaches its real cards faster
</h2>

The first thing I notice on Necrobinder is how quickly bad starter draws become expensive.

In one A10 Underdocks start, I had the option to chase a four-Elite route immediately. The stronger opening was much less dramatic: remove two Strikes first, then plan to remove a third at the next shop.

That left only one Strike.

<Image

  alt="Necrobinder removing two Strike cards at the start of an A10 run"
  width={1600}
  height={900}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

That decision matters because Necrobinder already has better ways to spend a draw than repeatedly finding basic attacks.

Osty can turn summon-related cards into defense and pressure. Unleash can turn a developed Osty into damage. Doom gives another route to ending fights. Draw, energy, and Ethereal pieces can disappear after doing their job.

A Strike does none of those things.

I still check what the deck can actually survive before deleting everything. An early Negative Pulse can solve area damage. Calcify may improve an Osty-based plan. Bodyguard can immediately make a rough defensive matchup more manageable.

The removal is valuable because it clears space for those cards, not because a tiny deck is automatically correct.

<h2 id="first-floors" className="scroll-mt-24">
  The first few floors tell me whether Osty is a shield, a weapon, or both
</h2>

Necrobinder runs can look completely different after only a few rewards.

One early run gave me enough tools to make Osty bigger while also generating block around it. In another, the draw pushed harder toward Ethereal cards and a much thinner deck. A third had enough energy to support Eradicate as a separate kill condition.

That is why I avoid deciding that “this is a Doom run” or “this is an Osty run” after one reward.

The first useful question is more practical:

**What happens when the enemy attacks before my payoff is ready?**

If Osty already absorbs enough pressure, I can spend more rewards on damage or draw.

If a fight is exposing Necrobinder directly, a modest defensive pickup may be more important than another synergy piece.

A good example came in a run where the deck was one point of block short of getting the interaction I wanted. That single missing point turned an otherwise strong setup into a painful turn.

Necrobinder can generate absurd late-run numbers, but the run usually dies much earlier than that when the middle turns are neglected.

<h2 id="do-not-force-doom" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Doom is strongest when the deck already has time to let it work
</h2>

Doom can end fights without asking Necrobinder to win through ordinary attack damage, but that does not make every Doom card an automatic pick.

I value it more when the rest of the deck already survives long enough for the effect to matter.

That usually means one or more of these are already true:

- Osty is absorbing meaningful damage;
- Weak or block is buying extra turns;
- draw is reliable enough to find the setup again;
- the deck has another answer for enemies that need to die immediately.

Without that support, stacking more Doom can simply make the deck slower.

I have had fights where Doom handled the finish cleanly while Osty and block bought the time. I have also had runs where Eradicate or Unleash became the real kill button and Doom stayed secondary.

The mechanic is a tool, not the identity of the run.

<h2 id="remove-until-it-hurts" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Card removal stays good until it starts deleting cards with an actual job
</h2>

Necrobinder can take deck thinning much farther than I am comfortable doing on many other characters.

One run found Forbidden Grimoire and immediately changed how I valued shops. Instead of paying repeatedly for removal, the relic could remove a card floor by floor.

That made the plan simple: clear the remaining Strikes first, then start looking at weak Defends and other cards that no longer contributed.

  src="/images/slay-the-spire-2/slay-the-spire-2-necrobinder-forbidden-grimoire-remove.webp"
  alt="Necrobinder using Forbidden Grimoire while shrinking the deck"
  width={1600}
  height={900}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

Later in the same run, the deck had fallen to **14 cards** during combat, and cards that had been useful earlier were becoming removable because the core sequence was now doing all the work.

The target was not “make the smallest deck possible.”

The target was:

**reach Reanimate, Banshee's Cry, Unleash, draw, and the other real engine pieces with fewer dead turns.**

That distinction keeps removal from becoming automatic.

At one shop, paying for another removal would have been wasteful because Forbidden Grimoire was already doing that job for free. The gold was more valuable elsewhere.

This is the point where Necrobinder rewards me for looking at the mechanism behind the deck rather than following a rule such as “always remove.”

<h2 id="when-to-stop-removing" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A card survives the cut when losing it creates a new problem
</h2>

Once the weak starters are gone, I slow down.

Bodyguard might look replaceable until the next fight exposes a block shortage.

Fetch may seem redundant until the deck needs a reliable way to recover one specific card.

A mediocre-looking card can remain correct because it covers a matchup the main engine handles poorly.

I use a simple test before the next removal:

- What card am I trying to draw instead?
- Does removing this improve that probability enough to matter?
- Which incoming fight gets worse without it?
- Am I paying gold for something the deck can already remove another way?

If I cannot answer the first question, the removal is probably cosmetic.

Necrobinder has enough self-thinning, Ethereal effects, exhaust-like behavior, and temporary setup that raw deck size does not tell the whole story.

<h2 id="route-around-power" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A strong Necrobinder route starts with what the deck can exploit right now
</h2>

There are runs where Necrobinder can become unusually aggressive with Elites.

One A10 deck reached Act 2 with enough energy and enough ways to stabilize Osty that the map offered a very tempting line:

**shop → four Elites.**

The shop came first, which mattered. It gave the run a chance to fix a weakness before committing to the dangerous section.

That route was not selected because “Necrobinder likes Elites.”

It worked because this particular deck had enough tools to extract value from them.

When I read a map, I look for the same escape logic I use elsewhere, but Necrobinder adds another question: **does the next reward amplify something I can already sustain?**

A relic-heavy line can snowball very hard if Osty, energy, draw, and removal are already functional.

If those foundations are missing, extra Elites simply test the unfinished part of the deck sooner.

For the actual node-by-node route layer, I leave the full pathing data to the <Link href="https://spiregenius.com/map">SpireGenius Map & Pathing Guide</Link>. Here, the useful Necrobinder judgment is whether the deck has enough stability to convert Elite risk into relic power.

<h2 id="greed-has-a-number" className="scroll-mt-24">
  I accept greed when I can put a number on what I am buying
</h2>

Necrobinder has runs where spending HP is completely reasonable, but I want the exchange to be explicit.

One fight gave me a good example.

An enemy had taken roughly **100 gold**. Recovering it required delaying the kill and accepting a small amount of damage.

The final trade was about **3 HP for 100 gold**.

That is a decision I can defend.

100 gold can change a shop. Three HP may be replaceable before the next dangerous fight. The deck also had enough control over the encounter to make the risk understandable.

The same greed becomes bad when the upside is vague.

Taking an extra Elite because “another relic would be nice” is not the same as risking a few HP for a known 100-gold recovery.

Necrobinder already has enough moving parts. I prefer greed that comes with a visible reward and a visible failure condition.

<h2 id="osty-buys-time" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Osty is most valuable when the extra time changes what my hand can do
</h2>

A large Osty can make Necrobinder feel much safer than her HP total suggests.

That safety is useful because several of her strongest turns need sequencing rather than raw speed.

Sometimes I want another turn to find Unleash.

Sometimes I am storing energy.

Sometimes a Doom target is already handled and the correct play is simply making sure the rest of the fight does not fall apart.

In one run, repeated Pull Aggro-style effects kept improving Osty while the deck waited for its damage piece. Even poor draws mattered less because the defensive engine was already functioning.

The danger is over-investing after that job is solved.

If Osty already survives the important hit, making Osty even larger may be less useful than adding draw, Weak, or a second way to kill.

That is where cards such as Friendship, Defy, Reanimate, or a relevant potion become contextual rather than universal picks.

One shop illustrated this cleanly: Bone Flute, Friendship, Defy, and a Block Potion all had immediate value because the current deck was using Osty as part of its defensive structure. Prowess was less attractive because the part of its payoff the deck cared about was narrower.

<h2 id="second-kill-condition" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A second way to kill is worth more when the first one can bottom-deck
</h2>

This is where Eradicate became useful in one of the A10 runs.

The deck already had a working route to damage, but the key card could arrive too late. That created a specific weakness: a strong deck could still lose time waiting for the bottom of the draw pile.

Eradicate gave the run another way to convert stored energy into a finish.

The numbers escalated quickly.

With Lethality active, one sequence reached **8 × 24 = 192 damage**.

Later, the same engine pushed much farther.

That is not evidence that every Necrobinder should take Eradicate. It shows why the card was good **in that deck**: energy storage, draw, and Lethality were already present.

The payoff arrived after the infrastructure.

That order is important.

Taking a huge X-cost finisher before the deck has excess energy makes it a promise. Taking it after the energy engine is already working turns it into an answer.

<h2 id="energy-needs-an-outlet" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Extra energy matters only when the deck can turn it into something decisive
</h2>

Necrobinder can generate enough energy that “more energy” stops being the problem.

Borrowed Time and Ice Cream produced one of the clearest examples.

Stored energy carried forward, Borrowed Time increased the available pool again, and Eradicate converted that reserve into repeated damage.

At one point, a sequence reached **26 × 9 = 234 damage** but still did not kill the target.

That calculation changed the turn.

The correct play became block, preserve the setup, and finish later rather than force an incomplete lethal.

The next cycle pushed the number higher.

This is the kind of turn where Necrobinder rewards arithmetic more than instinct. When the finisher scales with a resource I am banking, I count before committing.

If the target survives, I want enough defense left to see the next turn.

<h2 id="potions-preserve-lines" className="scroll-mt-24">
  I spend potions to protect a winning line, not to make every fight perfect
</h2>

Strong Necrobinder decks can make me too protective of potions.

That becomes dangerous when I start taking unnecessary damage just because the deck is capable of winning eventually.

The better rule is to preserve the line that matters most.

Sometimes that means drinking a potion immediately.

Sometimes a later boss matchup makes one potion much more valuable, so I accept a small amount of damage now.

One A10 sequence had both choices in the same stretch. A potion could end a dangerous turn immediately, but the upcoming fight valued that resource more highly, and the deck still had enough block to survive without it.

That was a real trade because both outcomes were visible.

Saving potions blindly is different. A potion carried into a death screen solved nothing.

<h2 id="temporary-relic-plan" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Temporary relic value can change the route after the deck is already strong
</h2>

One unusual run became dependent on keeping specific relics active.

Pocket Watch and Ice Cream were doing much more than Stone Calendar, and Ice Cream had become central to the Eradicate plan.

That forced a different type of map calculation.

The question was no longer just how many Elites I could beat.

I had to count **how many combats remained before the relic rotation would cost me the engine I wanted for the boss**.

The route was counted node by node.

Once the remaining safe combat count was known, the path changed to preserve Ice Cream long enough to matter.

That is a very Necrobinder kind of decision: the deck can become so specialized that routing stops being about generic value and starts being about protecting the exact mechanism that wins.

The <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/ascension-guide">Ascension guide</Link> owns the broader A1–A10 pressure curve. On Necrobinder, I care about the narrower version: when high-difficulty routing must protect a specific engine instead of maximizing every reward.

<h2 id="rest-when-engine-is-done" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Once the engine is good enough, HP becomes the upgrade
</h2>

A completed Necrobinder engine can make another Smith very tempting.

There is always another card that gets cheaper, blocks more, summons more, or improves the next cycle.

Yet one run reached a point where the offensive engine was already excellent and the next failure condition was simply dying.

The rest site healed **22 HP** in that situation.

Even though it slightly overhealed, resting was still the safer way to protect the run through a dangerous stretch.

Later, another run entered a boss with **42 HP and three potions** after spending much of the Act shrinking and upgrading the deck.

Those numbers matter more to me than squeezing one last theoretical percentage out of an upgrade.

When the deck already has a kill condition, enough draw, and enough energy, HP is often the remaining resource that cannot be replaced mid-fight.

<h2 id="when-the-deck-is-finished" className="scroll-mt-24">
  The finished Necrobinder deck usually needs fewer new ideas
</h2>

At the strongest point of one thin-deck run, almost every card had been upgraded.

Another had reached a point where new rewards were being skipped because they slowed the route back to the cards already winning fights.

That is the signal I look for.

The deck is finished when another card has to prove it improves one of these:

- first-cycle consistency;
- block on a bad draw;
- access to the main finisher;
- a known boss matchup;
- or recovery when the main plan fails.

Anything else is competing with cards that already earned their place.

One run eventually produced a **533-damage Unleash** and finished with **nine Elite kills**.

That number is the ceiling, not the lesson.

The useful part happened much earlier: removing weak draws, routing through the shops that mattered, upgrading the cards that actually carried combat, and stopping once the engine no longer needed help.

<h2 id="why-necrobinder-runs-collapse" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Most failed Necrobinder runs feel slow before they feel weak
</h2>

When the character looks powerful on paper but the run keeps bleeding HP, I usually find one of these problems.

**Too many starter-quality draws remain.** The deck owns good cards but reaches them too late.

**Osty has no clear job.** Summon cards are being added, yet the extra body is neither buying enough time nor converting into meaningful damage.

**Doom is being treated as a mandatory theme.** The deck keeps taking slower payoffs while the hallway fights still demand immediate answers.

**Energy is accumulating without a decisive outlet.** Extra energy feels strong until the hand has nothing worth spending it on.

**The route is priced for a finished deck.** Four Elites can be correct for one Necrobinder run and fatal for another.

**Removal has gone too far.** A thin deck is fast, but cutting the last reliable block card can expose a matchup that the damage engine cannot race.

**The finisher has only one access point.** If the winning card sits at the bottom of the deck, the run needs draw, recursion, or a second kill condition rather than another copy of a card that only works after the engine is already online.

Necrobinder becomes much more stable once every card has a timing attached to it.

Early cards keep the run alive.

Middle cards make Osty, Doom, draw, or energy reliable.

Late cards turn that reliability into a finish.

The runs that feel broken usually reached that state by removing friction first, not by finding the broken payoff first.
