<h2 id="arrows-aim-and-impaled" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why can't The Fool shoot another Arrow?
</h2>
The Fool begins each combat with one Arrow and 6 Aim from Song Bow. Shooting an Arrow spends that Aim on the shot and leaves the Arrow stuck in its target, making the enemy Impaled. You cannot simply fire the same Arrow again while it is stuck.

Nock is a zero-cost way to add 5 Aim before a shot. It improves the damage of the next Arrow, but it does not retrieve an Arrow that is already Impaled.

  src="/images/slay-the-spire-2/slay-the-spire-2-fool-nock-aim-setup.webp"
  alt="Nock showing Gain 5 Aim while The Fool prepares an Arrow attack"
  width={1728}
  height={904}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

To recover it, hit the Impaled enemy three times with attacks, kill that enemy, or use an effect that specifically Dislodges an Arrow. Those are three different ways to restore your next shot. A card that Shoots while you have no available Arrow will not produce its normal shot, so check the Arrow indicator before spending Energy on it.

The order of attacks matters immediately. If Foolish Shot can kill a nearly defeated enemy, shooting first may return the Arrow upon the kill and leave it available for another target. If the enemy will survive, consider whether the other attacks in your hand can hit it enough times to retrieve the Arrow before you draw or play your next Shot card.

An early high-Ascension run illustrated this distinction in a multi-enemy fight. The player chose which attack would finish the first enemy specifically so that Foolish Shot and a second Arrow card could both be used in the same sequence. Simply taking the largest available hit first would have left the Arrow unavailable at the wrong moment.

Before firing, check three things: whether the target will die, whether you have enough follow-up hits to Dislodge, and which Shot deserves the Aim you have accumulated. That short check prevents a surprisingly large number of wasted turns.
<h2 id="first-card-rewards" className="scroll-mt-24">
  What should The Fool pick in Act 1?
</h2>
The Fool's starting deck contains five Strikes, four Defends, and Foolish Shot. Its single powerful starting Shot gives the opening deck damage, but the rest of the hand can be awkward when an enemy attacks hard while the Arrow is unavailable.

A useful first reward either makes your next fight easier on its own or combines with something already in the deck. Wah Hammer is a straightforward damage option when you can keep enemies Impaled. Cho Firm helps a vulnerable opening by producing substantial Block when the Arrow is ready. Cho Ready can protect a turn while preparing more Aim for a future shot. Ki Whip creates Energy for the next turn rather than pretending to fix the current one.

These cards do different jobs. Taking Cho Firm is not an admission that an Arrow deck has failed; it is a way to avoid losing the HP needed to build one. Taking Wah Hammer does not mean every later reward must contain an Arrow. It simply means the starter Shot now has a follow-up attack that can benefit from the Impaled target.

<h2 id="arrow-aim-build" className="scroll-mt-24">
  When does an Arrow and Aim build become worth committing to?
</h2>
An Arrow deck starts to become convincing when a powerful Shot and a way to improve that Shot appear together. Rapid Shot is a clear example: it fires several hits, making accumulated Aim far more valuable than on a single-hit attack. That is a reason to consider supporting it with additional Aim, not a reason to fill the deck with every card that Shoots.

In a successful Arrow-focused run, the player found Rapid Shot early, upgraded it to reduce its Energy cost, and then built around drawing and strengthening that particular attack. The important decision came after the engine had started working: more Arrow cards were no longer automatically better, because a different Shot could consume the available Arrow or Aim before Rapid Shot was played.

The run repeatedly held back other attacks to preserve the large Shot. In one combat, Rapid Shot+ showed 34 damage on each of four hits. That output came from the interaction between the chosen Shot and its supporting cards, not from a generic rule that Rapid Shot is always the strongest reward.

Look at your actual hand before deciding to add a second Shot. If you frequently draw two Arrow attacks but can only retrieve one Arrow, you may have added competition rather than damage. If you have a single strong Shot but cannot reach it consistently, card access may improve the deck more than another damage modifier.

The same reasoning changes how you spend an upgrade. Reducing the cost of the attack you intend to fire repeatedly may give you more usable turns than upgrading an unrelated card with a larger printed damage increase.
<h2 id="melody-and-motif" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Should you build around Melody or keep playing Arrow damage?
</h2>
Wah, Cho, and Ki cards provide three different Melodies. You gain the corresponding Melody for the turn when you play a card bearing that name, and other cards reward you for having or gaining those Melodies. Wah tends toward damage, Cho toward defense, and Ki toward card access or Energy, though individual cards can do more than one job.

The strongest reason to invest in Melodies is that your existing cards start paying you for actions you already wanted to take.

Motif illustrates this well. Once it is active, gaining Melodies draws more cards, making it easier to find further Melody cards and continue the turn. In a successful run, Motif was upgraded and used alongside Ki Chant and several Melody cards to find a large Arrow attack repeatedly. The deck was not choosing between Melody and Arrow damage; Melody supplied access to the Arrow payoff.

Upgrading Motif reduces its cost from 2 Energy to 1 Energy without changing the draw effect. This makes the Power easier to play on a turn when the deck can already trigger several Melodies.

But Motif is a much worse early pickup if the deck barely has any Melody cards and cannot safely spend Energy on the Power. An A10 run passed up the temptation to treat Motif as the immediate solution because there was not enough support for it yet. That is the difference between finding an engine and carrying an unfinished engine through Act 1.

The defensive side is equally important. Cho Firm can provide large Block without asking for a rare combo. Once Song Path is present, gaining Cho Melody can add more Block to a turn already spent defending. Stretch makes a good Block card better, particularly when the card gains Block more than once.

Choose the Melody route when your cards already produce meaningful Melody triggers and you have something that rewards them. Do not chase all three Melodies every turn just to light up the interface. If the reward for doing so is not in your deck, you have spent cards and Energy to accomplish very little.
<h2 id="sadness-joy-and-ecstasy" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Is the Sadness and Joy build actually good?
</h2>
Sadness is not free value. It is an unplayable card that Retains in your hand. Generating more Sadness without a way to use or remove it creates a real hand-space problem. The build becomes interesting when its payoffs are already present rather than merely hoped for.

There are several different ways to turn that drawback into something useful:
- Anguish turns the number of Status cards created during combat into increasing attack damage.
- Ecstasy converts a Sadness in hand into Joy at the start of your turn, giving a way to turn retained clutter into usable resources.
- Brighten Up generates Joy immediately but also adds Sadness, so it is not a costless burst turn.
- Growth removes Sadness from hand and converts those cards into Stretch, supporting defense rather than direct damage.
- Vitriol offers immediate Weak but adds a Sadness, making it attractive even before a complete Sadness engine exists if the defense matters.
One successful lower-Ascension run began taking Sadness pieces early, found the conversion tools, added Ecstasy, and gradually reduced the weak cards surrounding the engine. By the later fights, Anguish had become a substantial damage outlet and Joy helped keep turns moving.

The player also made ordering errors and took damage that the finished engine could afford at that difficulty. At the end, they explicitly judged that the same sequence of plays would likely have failed at A10. The win establishes that the combination can function, not that it is a dependable high-Ascension default.

An early Dread-focused card evaluation was skeptical of the Sadness route for a different reason: too many pieces had to appear in the right order, and several had weak immediate value before the payoff arrived. Those two experiences are not contradictory. They describe the floor and ceiling of the same strategy.

A sensible transition point is when a Sadness generator, a conversion or removal method, and a meaningful payoff are already visible. Taking another Sadness generator before you have an outlet is often the wrong direction. If the deck only has Vitriol, it can remain a useful Weak card without becoming a dedicated Sadness build.
<h2 id="clone-vs-upgrade" className="scroll-mt-24">
  If you can Clone Wah Symphony, should you upgrade it first?
</h2>
A particularly strong run offered a decision that cannot be answered by a normal card ranking: Clone an already-scaling Wah Symphony, or upgrade the original first?

Wah Symphony deals area damage based on how many cards containing Wah, Cho, or Ki are in your deck. Upgrading it raises the bonus per qualifying card. Cloning creates another copy, and the extra copy also contributes to the scaling of the other one.

In the recorded run, the deck already contained several qualifying cards. Upgrading first would have made the original card's immediate hit larger. Cloning first gave two attacks and made both stronger because each copy counted toward the other's scaling. The player compared those outcomes before choosing the cloning route.

The run then exposed a second constraint: two expensive copies are only better if you can actually play them. Additional Energy or cost reduction became valuable after the duplication. The final damage became enormous, but the decisive work had happened earlier, when the deck had enough qualifying Melody cards and a plan to pay for the copies.

Clone is not a fourth core Fool archetype you should force. It is an unusually favorable reward interaction with a card that already scales from the deck you built. Without that scaling or the Energy to play the copies, duplicating a card can simply add another expensive draw.
<h2 id="defense-bosses-and-dread" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How does The Fool survive before its Arrow or Melody payoff arrives?
</h2>
The Fool can have enough damage to finish a boss and still lose while waiting for the right Shot, retrieving an Impaled Arrow, or finding the first Melody payoff. Adding another expensive Shot does not help if the turn spent setting it up cannot survive the enemy's attack.

In a recorded Fool Dread clear, Cho cards, Stretch, Weak, and Song Path kept the deck alive while its Arrow damage became available. The useful character-specific lesson is to make the defensive half of the turn function even when the main Shot is not in hand; the broader A1–A10 difficulty changes are covered in the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/ascension-guide">Ascension Guide</Link>.

Check whether you can produce Block before drawing the full Melody engine, whether you have enough low-cost attacks to Dislodge the Arrow, and whether you can still spend Energy on defense after playing your draw Power. Those are separate problems, and one more damage card will not solve all three.

At a Rest Site, upgrading Rapid Shot may be worth more when the lower cost lets you fire it while defending. If the main Shot is already playable but your Melody engine starts too slowly, the Motif cost reduction may be more useful. For general Rest-versus-Smith decisions, use the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/beginner-guide">Beginner Guide</Link>.
