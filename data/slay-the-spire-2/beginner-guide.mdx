<h2 id="first-win-plan" className="scroll-mt-24">
  My first-win plan starts with the map, not with a build
</h2>

The most useful change I made as a new player was to stop deciding what kind of deck I wanted before the run had given me enough information.

The first decision is already on the map.

I scan from the bottom all the way to the boss and look for branches that preserve choices. One route may contain more Rest Sites. Another may lead through an Elite but still give me a way to turn toward a Merchant or a safer fight if the opening rooms cost more HP than expected.

On one early map, I could trace a branch with **three Rest Sites** while another gave me two. More importantly, one fork let me approach an Elite without committing to it from the first floor.

That is the kind of route I prefer for a first win.

If the first card rewards are strong and my HP stays high, I can take the aggressive branch.

If the run opens badly, I still have somewhere to go.

A route with more theoretical rewards is not better when it removes every chance to change the plan.

For the full map and pathing layer, including the broader value of different nodes, I use the <Link href="https://spiregenius.com/map">SpireGenius Map & Pathing Guide</Link>. For a beginner run, the simpler rule is enough: **leave yourself an exit before you need one.**

<h2 id="solve-the-next-fight" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Early card rewards should make the next few fights easier
</h2>

Act 1 does not need a finished archetype.

It needs cards that keep the run moving.

If the starter deck takes too long to kill ordinary enemies, I want damage.

If one bad draw leaves me unable to cover a heavy attack, I want dependable Block.

If I already have enough of both, draw, energy, or a synergy piece starts becoming more attractive.

This is where a lot of first runs become weaker without looking weaker.

Three individually interesting cards can enter the deck, yet none of them helps with the problem that is already costing HP.

I try to give every early pick a sentence:

**“I am taking this because…”**

If the sentence ends with:

> “…it might become amazing if I find several other cards later,”

I usually need a better reason.

The run may eventually develop a clear engine. Until that happens, immediate usefulness matters more than naming the build.

<h2 id="check-lethal" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Before I spend Energy on Block, I check whether the enemy can die
</h2>

New-player caution can cost HP too.

During an early combat, an enemy had scaled to an attack of **3 × 4**, or 12 total damage.

The defensive answer looked obvious.

Then I checked the enemy's remaining HP.

The attacks in hand could simply end the fight.

<Image
  src="/images/slay-the-spire-2/slay-the-spire-2-beginner-lethal-before-block.webp"
  alt="Slay the Spire 2 combat where checking lethal is better than spending Energy on Block"
  width={1600}
  height={900}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

Block only protects me from an attack that still happens.

That sounds trivial, but it changes a surprising number of turns. I now check combat in this order:

1. Can I kill the dangerous enemy?
2. If not, can I reduce its damage through Weak, stun, or another effect?
3. How much Block do I actually need after that?
4. What can the remaining Energy do for the next turn?

Sometimes full Block is right.

Sometimes taking 2 or 3 HP now ends the fight a turn sooner and prevents a much larger attack later.

And sometimes the safest defensive card in my hand is the attack that removes the enemy from combat.

<h2 id="skip-card-rewards" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Skip is part of deck building, not a reward I failed to take
</h2>

After a fight, seeing three cards can make the reward feel like a choice between A, B, and C.

There is a fourth button that matters just as much:

**Skip.**

<Image
  src="/images/slay-the-spire-2/slay-the-spire-2-beginner-card-reward-skip.webp"
  alt="Slay the Spire 2 card reward screen where Skip is available instead of adding a card"
  width={1600}
  height={900}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

Every added card changes what I can draw later.

If my best block card is already hard to find, another situational attack may make that problem worse.

If the deck has enough damage, taking another average damage card because it is technically useful can delay the cards that provide draw, defense, or scaling.

I use three questions before clicking a reward:

- Does this fix something the deck currently struggles with?
- Does it make an existing strong interaction materially better?
- Would I be happy to draw this during a bad turn?

A card does not need three yeses.

It does need a job.

This is also why I do not chase a tiny deck for its own sake. A larger deck with enough good answers can work perfectly well. The dangerous cards are the ones that enter without improving a real turn.

<h2 id="when-to-commit" className="scroll-mt-24">
  I commit to synergy after the run gives me a reason
</h2>

Synergy matters. Forcing it too early does not.

If several rewards are already supporting the same mechanic, leaning farther into that package can make the deck much stronger. The important word is **already**.

A payoff card with no setup is often weaker than a plain attack or block card that keeps Act 1 stable.

Once the engine exists, the calculation changes. Now a card that improves that engine may be worth more than another generic option.

This distinction keeps me from making two opposite mistakes:

- filling the deck with unrelated cards because each one looked individually strong;
- refusing a useful off-theme card because I decided too early what the deck was “supposed” to be.

I want the deck to become specialized through evidence from the run, not through a plan I brought into floor one.

<h2 id="shops" className="scroll-mt-24">
  I route into a shop when my Gold can realistically change the run
</h2>

Merchants are valuable, but arriving broke wastes much of that value.

In one early run, I reached a shop with **118 Gold**. The relics I actually wanted were outside that budget, so the exciting part of the shop was effectively unavailable.

The useful purchase was much simpler: card removal.

A Strike had become one of the weaker draws for the direction that run was taking, so removing it improved future hands without requiring another synergy piece.

<Image
  src="/images/slay-the-spire-2/slay-the-spire-2-beginner-shop-remove-strike.webp"
  alt="Slay the Spire 2 Merchant visited with 118 Gold before removing a weak Strike"
  width={1600}
  height={900}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

That does not mean I remove a Strike every time I enter a shop.

Before spending Gold, I compare removal against everything else the run still needs.

A potion may save the next Elite.

A strong card may fix the exact gap in the deck.

A relic can sometimes change the run much more than deleting one starter card.

And if another shop is already visible farther up the route, saving Gold can be stronger than buying something mediocre simply because I am standing in front of a Merchant now.

The question I use is:

**What does this purchase make possible before my next good chance to spend Gold?**

If I cannot answer that, I am usually happier leaving with the money.

<h2 id="rest-or-smith" className="scroll-mt-24">
  At a Rest Site, I spend the fire on the failure state closest to me
</h2>

Rest and Smith are not an HP-percentage rule.

One early Rest Site offered **24 HP of healing**, but the run was already healthy enough that the immediate danger was low.

Smithing had more value, so I upgraded instead.

<Image
  src="/images/slay-the-spire-2/slay-the-spire-2-beginner-rest-or-smith.webp"
  alt="Slay the Spire 2 Rest Site where a healthy run chooses Smith instead of 24 HP of healing"
  width={1600}
  height={900}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

The same HP total can produce a different answer on another map.

If an Elite is next and one bad opening hand can put me into lethal range, healing may protect the entire run.

If there are several ordinary rooms before the next dangerous fight, upgrading an important attack or block card may save more HP across those rooms than Rest would restore now.

I look forward rather than at the health bar alone.

The fire is there to solve the next dangerous stretch.

Sometimes that solution is more HP.

Sometimes it is making the card I expect to play repeatedly better.

<h2 id="elite-readiness" className="scroll-mt-24">
  I take an Elite when the deck can pay the entry cost
</h2>

Elites matter because their rewards can make the rest of the run much stronger.

That does not make them mandatory.

Before taking one, I check four things:

**HP:** How much damage can I afford before the route becomes unsafe?

**Immediate damage:** Can the deck pressure an enemy before its dangerous mechanic takes over?

**Defense:** What happens if the opening hand is poor?

**Potions:** Do I have one that turns a borderline fight into a reasonable one?

The branch after the Elite matters too.

An Elite followed by a Rest Site is a very different commitment from an Elite followed by two forced encounters and no recovery.

This is where HP starts making more sense as a resource.

Finishing every fight at full health is not the objective. Spending some HP to earn a relic can be worthwhile if that relic makes the next Act safer.

But HP does not become free because I call it a resource.

I have had situations where very low HP was still enough for an Elite because the deck matched the fight well. I have also backed away at high HP because the deck had no good answer to the encounter.

The deck decides how safe the health bar really is.

<h2 id="enemy-priority" className="scroll-mt-24">
  I choose targets by what they are about to become
</h2>

Enemy intent is more useful than simply attacking the target with the lowest HP.

I separate threats into three rough groups.

An **immediate threat** is already presenting a dangerous attack. I either kill it, weaken it, stun it, or spend enough Block to survive.

A **short-term threat** may be buffing or setting up this turn. If I can remove it before the next action, I can prevent the dangerous turn rather than defend against it.

A **long-term threat** gets worse every cycle, fills the deck with junk, or scales until my current defense stops working.

The correct target depends on what my deck does well.

If my Block is excellent, I may be able to ignore the enemy dealing steady damage and kill the one scaling out of control.

If my damage is much stronger than my defense, deleting the immediate attacker can prevent more HP loss than trying to block everything.

That is also why learning enemy behavior pays off faster than memorizing tier lists.

A card only becomes good or bad relative to what the next fights ask it to do.

<h2 id="potions" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A potion has value when it changes a fight, not when it survives to the boss
</h2>

I used to save potions for an imagined perfect moment.

That often meant carrying them through the fight where they would have prevented the most damage.

Now I spend them when I can identify the return.

A potion that turns an Elite from a likely 25-HP loss into a manageable fight may be buying:

- the Elite reward;
- the HP needed to Smith at the next fire;
- and the freedom to keep the aggressive branch afterward.

That is much more valuable than saying I reached the boss with a full belt.

The opposite is also true. Burning a strong potion to save 2 HP in a harmless hallway fight may leave me without an answer for something that actually threatens the run.

The decision becomes easier when I ask what the potion is buying rather than whether using it feels wasteful.

<h2 id="act-one-check" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Before the Act 1 boss, I want the deck to prove three things
</h2>

I do not expect a finished build by the end of Act 1.

I want evidence that the run can continue.

First, ordinary enemies should no longer take so long that every fight drains HP.

Second, I need at least one way to survive an ugly defensive turn. That can come from Block, Weak, another defensive mechanic, or enough control to prevent the dangerous attack.

Third, the deck needs some reason to improve instead of merely adding more basic damage cards forever.

That may be draw.

It may be a power.

It may be a resource engine.

It may be a synergy that several existing cards already support.

If one of those three pieces is missing, I use the last floors of the Act to fix it rather than trying to polish something the deck already does well.

<h2 id="act-two-reset" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Entering Act 2 is a new route decision, not permission to repeat Act 1
</h2>

Beating the first boss can make a run feel solved.

I treat the next map as a reset.

The new Ancient choice, new route, new enemies, and current HP total can all change what the deck needs.

A deck that had enough single-target damage in Act 1 may now need a better answer for multiple enemies.

A defensive deck that could slowly handle early fights may discover that one Act 2 encounter scales too quickly for that pace.

A powerful relic reward may open a line that was not worth considering earlier.

So I scan the full map again before moving.

I check shops against current Gold, locate Rest Sites, identify where the Elite branches stop being reversible, and decide how much risk the deck can actually buy.

The previous Act tells me what the deck has become.

It does not choose the next route for me.

<h2 id="after-first-win" className="scroll-mt-24">
  After the first clear, I keep the same habits and raise the pressure
</h2>

A first win is less about discovering one unbeatable build than about making enough good decisions in sequence.

I planned a route with exits.

I took cards that had jobs.

I skipped cards that did not.

I checked lethal before blocking.

I spent Gold where it changed the run.

I used Rest Sites according to what could actually kill me next.

Those habits keep working after the first clear.

What changes is how much room the game gives me to make mistakes. If I want to continue climbing, the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/ascension-guide">Slay the Spire 2 Ascension Guide</Link> covers how the pressure changes from A1 through A10 and which decisions become more expensive at each stage.

The first win taught me the more important lesson first:

**I do not need to know the final deck on floor one. I need to know what the next problem is.**
