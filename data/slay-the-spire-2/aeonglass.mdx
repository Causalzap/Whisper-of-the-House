<section id="quick-answer" className="scroll-mt-24">
  <GuideQuickAnswer title="Count Your Cards, Control Wither, and End the Fight">

  Aeonglass adds a Wither to your hand after every six cards you play. Each Wither left in your hand at the end of the turn deals damage, and the boss makes those cards stronger as the fight continues.

  Before playing another card, check whether it will trigger Withering Presence. Discard or Exhaust Withers when possible, and prepare for Ebb, Eye Lasers, and the non-attacking Increasing Intensity turn. Aeonglass also starts with three Artifact, so remove those charges before depending on important debuffs.

  </GuideQuickAnswer>
</section>

Aeonglass is an Act 3 boss found in Glory in *Slay the Spire 2*. Its danger comes from two threats working together: a repeating attack pattern and **Wither Status cards that punish long turns**.

A deck can have enough damage to beat ordinary enemies and still lose here. Playing additional cards may create more Withers, while spending too many turns defending allows Aeonglass to gain Strength and make those Withers more dangerous.

The fight is not about playing as few cards as possible. It is about making each extra card play worthwhile before the next escalation.

<h2 id="aeonglass-pattern" className="scroll-mt-24">
  What Is Aeonglass's Attack Pattern?
</h2>

Aeonglass has **512 HP**, increasing to **535 HP at Ascension 8 and above**. It also begins with three Artifact and Withering Presence.

Its attacks follow a repeating three-turn sequence.

/>

### Turn 1: Ebb

Ebb deals a substantial opening hit while giving Aeonglass 33 Block.

This makes the first turn awkward for decks that depend on dealing damage before establishing defense. Even if your opening hand contains an excellent attack, you may need to spend Energy surviving rather than trying to break through all 33 Block.

The correct choice depends on what the deck can establish immediately. A defensive Power that will protect several later turns may be worth playing. A high-damage attack that barely removes the boss's Block may not be.

Do not spend all your resources trying to force early damage when the same resources are needed to survive Eye Lasers on the next turn.

### Turn 2: Eye Lasers

Eye Lasers attacks twice.

At higher Ascension, the two hits deal 12 damage each before later Strength gains. Block is consumed across both hits, so the total incoming damage matters more than how intimidating either individual hit looks.

This is a dangerous turn for decks that spent their previous Energy on slow setup and have not found their defensive cards.

If the next draw contains Status cards, another speculative draw effect may not produce the Block you need.

### Turn 3: Increasing Intensity

Increasing Intensity does not directly attack.

Instead, Aeonglass adds upgraded Withers to your discard pile, gains Strength, upgrades existing Withers, and strengthens the Withers generated by its passive effect.

At A9 and above, the move adds two Withers rather than one and increases its Strength gain.

This turn is your opportunity to make progress without facing an ordinary attack. It is also where the fight becomes more dangerous if you do not capitalize on that opportunity.

You can use the opening to establish a Power, play a powerful attack, clear Status cards, or prepare a stronger next turn.

However, playing unnecessary cards is still costly. Withering Presence remains active even when Aeonglass is not attacking.

If the turn ends with several Withers in your hand, the absence of an attack does not guarantee safety.

<h2 id="wither-mechanics" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How Does Wither Work Against Aeonglass?
</h2>

The boss starts with **Withering Presence**, which adds one Wither to your hand after every six cards you play.

<Image
  src="/images/slay-the-spire-2/slay-the-spire-2-aeonglass-wither-card-trigger.webp"
  alt="Aeonglass's Withering Presence creates a Wither after every six cards played, while a Wither+3 shows 12 end-turn damage"
  width={1736}
  height={636}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

The Wither card is unplayable. If it remains in your hand at the end of the turn, it deals damage to you.

An ordinary Wither deals 3 damage. As Increasing Intensity upgrades Withers, their damage becomes more severe.

The card-play counter is the part of this mechanic that most often changes a decision.

Suppose you have already played five cards since the last Wither was created. The next card you play will generate another Wither after its effect resolves.

That sixth card might be worth playing if it kills Aeonglass, provides enough Block to protect you, or removes a larger amount of danger than it creates.

It might not be worth playing if it provides a small amount of damage while leaving you with no Energy or other way to handle the new Wither.

### Why should you count cards before ending a turn?

Imagine your hand contains a zero-cost attack, one defensive card, and a way to discard unwanted cards.

You are one play away from another Wither.

If you use the discard effect first and then play the attack, the attack may generate a Wither after your discard option is gone.

If the defensive card already covers the incoming damage, the extra attack may be unnecessary.

A better sequence may be to use the attack first, trigger the Wither, and then discard it before ending the turn.

The precise choice depends on the cards in your hand, but the order can decide whether the same hand costs HP or survives comfortably.

### Why can Exhaust fail to remove the newest Wither?

Withering Presence creates its Wither **after the triggering card has finished resolving**.

That means an Exhaust effect cannot remove a Wither that has not been generated yet.

For example, if Scavenge is your sixth played card, it resolves before the Wither appears. The new Wither will enter your hand afterward.

If you need Scavenge to clear the final Wither of the turn, trigger Withering Presence with another useful card first, then play Scavenge.

This also creates a reason to stop playing cards when the next play is unnecessary. A zero-cost card is not automatically free of consequences in this encounter.

<h2 id="discard-exhaust-wither" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Is Discard or Exhaust Better for Removing Wither?
</h2>

Both can solve the immediate problem, but they do not solve it for the same length of time.

**Discard** removes a Wither from your current hand. That prevents it from damaging you at the end of this turn, provided the card is not drawn again before then.

The Wither is still in your discard pile. When the deck reshuffles, it can return.

**Exhaust** removes the Wither for the rest of the combat.

That distinction matters more as the fight continues.

A fast deck that expects to finish Aeonglass next turn may only need one discard effect to survive. A deck that expects two more full attack cycles has a stronger reason to remove Withers permanently.

Cards with targeted discard or Exhaust are particularly useful because they let you preserve important attacks and defensive cards.

The best option also performs another useful job. A removal effect that clears Withers while generating damage, Block, or other resources is easier to fit into a functioning deck than a card that does nothing until the Status problem appears.

### What if you have no Wither removal?

You can still win without a dedicated removal card.

The question becomes how quickly the deck can deal enough damage and how often it can afford to trigger Withering Presence.

A high-damage turn that creates one Wither may be preferable to several low-damage turns that create multiple Withers and allow Increasing Intensity to repeat.

Conversely, refusing to play needed defensive cards merely to avoid one Wither can lose the fight immediately.

Do not treat the six-card trigger as a rule against playing cards. Treat it as an additional cost that must be included in your damage and defense calculations.

<h2 id="artifact-debuffs" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Does Aeonglass Ignore Weak, Vulnerable, Poison, or Doom?
</h2>

Aeonglass starts with **three Artifact**.

Those charges negate incoming debuffs. This can make a deck appear to lose its primary damage plan during the first few turns.

A Poison or Doom deck that expects its first application to begin scaling will instead have to remove Artifact before that plan can function.

The same problem affects Vulnerable and Weak.

If you need Weak to survive the opening attack, you cannot assume that the first Weak application will affect Aeonglass while Artifact remains.

The most useful preparation happens before the fight.

Look at which cards your deck depends on, how many debuff applications it can produce, and whether it can remove Artifact directly.

### Silent: when should you remove Aeonglass's Artifact?

For Silent, removing Artifact is especially useful when Poison or Vulnerable is central to the damage plan. Weak also needs to get through that protection before it can reduce Aeonglass's incoming damage.

In one successful Silent fight, Expose+ removed Aeonglass's Artifact and Block, then applied Vulnerable. The player could follow with attacks that benefited from the cleared protection instead of wasting additional debuff applications.

<h2 id="fool-aeonglass" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Can The Fool's Sadness or Melody Build Survive Aeonglass?
</h2>

The Fool can generate long turns through Melody-based draw and Sadness-to-Joy conversions. Against Aeonglass, however, every six cards played create another Wither. An engine that produces many cards is only useful if those cards provide enough damage, Block, or Status removal to justify the extra pressure.

A lower-Ascension Sadness/Joy run successfully defeated Aeonglass after developing enough damage and resources to finish the encounter. A different Fool attempt generated substantial Block but relied on long card sequences without a reliable way to clear the Withers they created. That run ended in defeat.

Before extending a Melody or Sadness turn, check whether the next cards will produce meaningful damage or defense. If the engine only draws more setup cards while creating additional Withers, stop treating card draw as progress.

For the full Arrow, Melody, Sadness, and Joy interactions, see the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/the-fool">The Fool Guide</Link>.

In a successful Defect Blitz fight, Refract+ and a second use enabled by Liquid Memories supplied enough damage to defeat Aeonglass.

The important calculation is whether the available attacks can overcome the boss's Block and remaining HP before another Wither trigger makes the turn unsafe. Extra card draw helps only when the cards it finds can produce enough damage or protection to justify the additional plays.

<h2 id="late-fight-failure" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Do Strong Decks Still Lose After Several Aeonglass Turns?
</h2>

Aeonglass becomes increasingly difficult to finish because each Increasing Intensity makes later turns more dangerous.

The damage pressure does not come from one source.

Its regular attacks become stronger, Withers become more costly, and additional Status cards interfere with reaching the cards that would protect you.

A particularly close Defect Dread attempt reached Aeonglass after a difficult Act.

The deck had powerful offensive cards, defensive options, and ways to manage unwanted cards. It was capable of producing significant damage.

But the final encounter required extremely precise sequencing.

At one point, the player had to decide which card to play first because a different order would create another Wither before the available removal effect could deal with it.

Later, the player calculated that the Withers alone would deal more damage than the available protection could cover.

The deck survived several additional turns, but drawing another Wither or failing to find the next defensive card could immediately end the run.

Eventually, that narrow margin disappeared.

The loss reveals something important about Aeonglass: **a deck can have enough total damage in its card pool without being able to produce that damage in the turns available**.

When the fight reaches that state, adding another powerful attack would not necessarily have solved the original problem.

The deck needed either faster access to its existing damage, fewer Withers interfering with the draw pile, or enough immediate defense to survive the time required to finish.

<h2 id="dread-aeonglass" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Is Aeonglass's Dread Dominion the Same as Withering Presence?
</h2>

No. Aeonglass's Dread Dominion adds a Wither to the top of your Draw Pile at the start of each turn during the Act's affected encounters. The boss's own Withering Presence instead adds a Wither directly to your hand after every six cards played.

When fighting Aeonglass itself, manage the boss's card-play trigger and attack cycle. For the Dominion's effect on earlier encounters, additional Ascender's Banes, and Dread-specific progression, see the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/dread">Dread Mode Guide</Link>.

<h2 id="aeonglass-troubleshooting" className="scroll-mt-24">
  What Should You Change If Aeonglass Keeps Killing Your Runs?
</h2>

A failed Aeonglass fight usually reveals one of several different weaknesses. The answer depends on whether you lost to immediate damage, Status accumulation, Artifact protection, or the increasingly dangerous attack cycle.

<GuideTable
  caption="Aeonglass: common failure states and what each one requires."
  columns={aeonglassTroubleColumns}
  rows={aeonglassTroubleRows}
/>

If you die on the opening attacks, first check whether your deck can produce useful Block without needing several Powers already in play.

If you survive the opening but lose once Withers accumulate, count how many cards you are playing before each trigger. Look for discard or Exhaust effects that can remove the Status cards after they appear.

If the boss takes almost no damage, check whether its Artifact is preventing your important debuffs or whether Ebb's Block is consuming your offensive turns.

If you survive several cycles but never get close to killing it, the deck may lack enough sustained damage to beat Increasing Intensity.

Before entering an Aeonglass fight, check whether your deck can handle Withers, remove Artifact when necessary, and deal enough damage before Increasing Intensity makes another cycle too dangerous.

You do not need a perfect counter card. You need a deck that can survive the opening, avoid excessive Wither damage, and finish the fight before another cycle becomes too expensive.
