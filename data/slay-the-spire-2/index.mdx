<h2 id="where-to-start" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Pick the Slay the Spire 2 guide that matches where the run is breaking
</h2>

A Slay the Spire 2 run can fail for completely different reasons while producing the same result.

Sometimes I reach Act 1 with a deck full of cards but no reliable damage. Another run dies because I committed to an Elite route before I knew whether the deck could survive it. Later, the cards may be strong enough for a normal clear but too inconsistent once Ascension removes more of the room for error.

I get more useful answers by identifying **where the run first became unstable**.

If I am still learning when to take a card, when to Skip, how much HP an Elite is worth, when to Rest instead of Smith, or whether a Merchant is worth entering with my current Gold, I use the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/beginner-guide">Slay the Spire 2 Beginner Guide</Link>.

If those decisions already make sense and higher difficulty is the problem, the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/ascension-guide">Ascension Guide</Link> covers how the pressure changes from A1 through A10.

Character trouble is different. Regent and Necrobinder can both produce extremely strong runs, but they punish different mistakes, so I treat them separately rather than forcing one generic deck-building rule across every character.

A run can ask for a real trade before the first floor. One Ancient offered
me +11 Max HP, another option gave a card reward and a random potion, while
Cursed Pearl offered 333 Gold at the cost of adding Greed.

<h2 id="first-win" className="scroll-mt-24">
  If the first clear still feels inconsistent, fix the run before chasing a build
</h2>

The first-win problem is rarely that I have not found the strongest archetype yet.

It is usually one decision earlier.

Maybe I entered the wrong branch.

Maybe I added a card that did not solve anything.

Maybe a potion stayed unused while HP disappeared.

Maybe I spent Gold because I reached a Merchant rather than because the purchase changed the next fight.

The <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/beginner-guide">Beginner Guide</Link> owns those decisions from the opening route through the first clear.

I keep the Hub at that level because the useful answer depends on the actual state of the run. The detailed card-reward, shop, Rest Site, Elite, and combat examples belong there rather than being repeated here.

<h2 id="ascension" className="scroll-mt-24">
  If A0 feels comfortable but the climb keeps stalling, treat Ascension as stacked pressure
</h2>

Ascension exposes habits that a normal run can let me get away with.

The early levels change how aggressively I can route and spend HP. The middle of the ladder reduces the resources available to repair a weak run. Near the top, ordinary bad turns become much more expensive and the final fights demand a deck that works repeatedly rather than only when the draw lines up.

The <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/ascension-guide">Slay the Spire 2 Ascension Guide</Link> is the Owner for the complete A1–A10 ladder and the decisions I change while climbing.

I do not duplicate the individual modifiers here. What matters at Hub level is the diagnosis:

**if the same kind of run starts dying only after the difficulty rises, look for the decision that the new pressure stopped forgiving.**

<h2 id="regent" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Regent runs improve when resources solve a real problem
</h2>

Regent can generate enough options that a run looks powerful before it is stable.

The trap is mistaking more setup for more strength.

When I am losing Regent runs that seem to have plenty of Stars, energy, or interesting interactions, I check whether the deck actually covers the next bad defensive turn and whether new cards are improving an existing engine or merely expanding it.

The <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/regent-guide">Regent Guide</Link> owns that full decision chain: Act 1 priorities, Stars, shops, removals, Rest versus Smith, route greed, and the point where a finished deck should start refusing unnecessary additions.

<h2 id="necrobinder" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Necrobinder usually feels weak before the payoff because the deck reaches its good cards too slowly
</h2>

Necrobinder can finish with enormous damage, but that ceiling is not what gets the run through its vulnerable turns.

When she feels inconsistent, I first look at draw quality, weak starter cards, what Osty is actually accomplishing, and whether Doom or another payoff has enough time and support to matter.

The <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/necrobinder-guide">Necrobinder Guide</Link> owns the deeper decisions around removal, Osty, Doom, energy storage, finishers, aggressive Elite routes, and when shrinking the deck stops being free value.

Regent and Necrobinder therefore stay separate even when both pages eventually arrive at the same broad goal: a deck that reaches its important cards reliably.

The route to that goal is different enough that combining them would make both answers worse.

<h2 id="multiplayer" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Co-op starts making sense when four decks stop solving the same problem twice
</h2>

Multiplayer is not just a solo run with extra players.

A team-wide defensive card can release several other hands to attack. One debuff can improve damage from multiple decks. A reward may be much stronger on one teammate than another, and a card that puts copies into everyone's discard pile can hurt three decks that never wanted it.

<h2 id="exact-data" className="scroll-mt-24">
  For exact cards, relics, monsters, and route data, I use SpireGenius
</h2>

Some questions are better answered as data than as another long guide.

If I need the current details of a card, relic, potion, enemy, boss, or map interaction, I would rather check the exact entity than make a strategy article repeat a database.

That is what I use <Link href="https://spiregenius.com">SpireGenius</Link> for.

Whisper stays focused on the part that usually sends me searching in the middle of a run:

**What should I do with this information now?**

That separation matters most when the game changes. Exact values can live with the structured game data, while these guides can stay centered on the decisions those values create.

<h2 id="what-to-play-next" className="scroll-mt-24">
  The next guide depends on what changed after the last run
</h2>

If I have not cleared the game reliably yet, I go back to the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/beginner-guide">Beginner Guide</Link> and look for the first decision that started draining HP, Gold, or deck consistency.

If normal clears are already comfortable, the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/ascension-guide">Ascension Guide</Link> becomes the more useful framework.

If the difficulty is character-specific, I switch to the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/regent-guide">Regent Guide</Link> or <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/necrobinder-guide">Necrobinder Guide</Link> instead of applying generic rules to a character whose resources work differently.

And if the same deck feels completely different with friends, that is no longer a solo strategy problem. The <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/multiplayer-guide">Multiplayer Guide</Link> is where the team's target priority, defense, rewards, and progression belong.

A good run gives me a stronger deck.

A useful loss tells me which decision needs a better answer next time.
