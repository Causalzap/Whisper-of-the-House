<h2 id="how-ascension-gets-harder" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Ascension gets hard because every earlier problem stays with me
</h2>

The first few levels can make Ascension look like a normal difficulty slider.

A1 adds more Elites. A2 reduces Ancient healing. A3 cuts Gold.

The important part is what happens next: **the modifiers stack**.

By A8, I am not simply fighting enemies with more HP. I am doing that while the map contains the earlier Elite pressure, Ancient recovery is weaker, Gold is tighter, I have lost a potion slot, Ascender's Bane is taking a draw, Rest Sites are less common, and strong card rewards appear less often.

That changes the cost of a mistake.

At low difficulty, taking one ugly hallway fight may only cost some HP. Higher up the ladder, the same loss can force a Rest at the only useful fire, leave me short of Gold at the next shop, and consume the potion I wanted for an Elite.

I started climbing more consistently once I stopped asking:

**“What is the strongest deck I can build?”**

and started asking:

**“Which part of this run is about to become too expensive?”**

<h2 id="unlock-ascension" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How to unlock Ascension and reach A10
</h2>

Ascension unlocks after clearing all three Acts with a character.

That first clear unlocks **Ascension 1 for that character**.

From there the progression is sequential:

A1 must be cleared to unlock A2, A2 must be cleared to unlock A3, and the same pattern continues until A10. I cannot skip a level.

Solo progression is character-specific as well. If I have already climbed several levels with Regent, a fresh Necrobinder climb does not inherit those unlocks.

Co-op uses a different progression structure, so I keep those rules in the <Link href="/slay-the-spire-2/multiplayer-guide">Slay the Spire 2 multiplayer guide</Link> rather than mixing two systems together here.

<h2 id="a1-a3" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A1–A3 turn route, HP, and Gold into planning problems
</h2>

A1 is unusual because its drawback can also look like a reward.

More Elites mean more dangerous fights, but successful Elite fights also mean more chances to collect relics. That makes aggressive routes tempting before the deck has earned them.

I have had maps where several Elite lines were available and the greediest route looked excellent if I counted only the rewards.

Then I looked at the deck.

  alt="Slay the Spire 2 A10 map with several Elite routes available"
  width={1600}
  height={900}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"

The route existed, but the current cards, block, HP, and potion coverage did not justify taking the most aggressive line.

That distinction becomes one of the most useful habits in Ascension:

**a reachable Elite is not automatically a profitable Elite.**

Before committing, I check what happens immediately before and after it.

A route becomes much safer when a branch gives me a choice between another Elite and a Rest Site, shop, or ordinary encounter. If the first half of the Act goes badly, that branch lets me convert the original plan into something survivable.

For the complete route and node layer, I use the <Link href="https://spiregenius.com/map">SpireGenius Map & Pathing Guide</Link>. Here the Ascension question is simpler: **how much flexibility am I buying before the run starts going wrong?**

<h3 id="a2-health" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A2 makes HP lost early in the Act harder to erase later
</h3>

Weary Traveler reduces Ancient recovery to **80% of missing HP**.

The difference sounds small when I am healthy.

It matters much more after an expensive Elite or a bad hallway sequence.

I can no longer price a risky path on the assumption that the next Ancient completely resets the damage. HP lost now stays relevant farther into the Act.

That changes potion use too.

If a potion prevents a large amount of damage before an important branch, I am more willing to spend it. Saving every potion for a boss can leave me entering that boss weaker because I paid too much HP getting there.

The useful question is not whether the potion can produce more value later.

It is whether I can safely reach later without using it.

<h3 id="a3-gold" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A3 makes every unnecessary purchase visible at the next shop
</h3>

Poverty cuts Gold from enemies and treasure chests by **25%**.

The pain usually arrives several rooms after the modifier did its work.

A mediocre card purchase early can become the reason I cannot afford a better relic later.

An automatic removal can consume the Gold I needed for a potion before an Elite.

A shop with several useful options stops being a place where I buy everything I like and becomes a place where I have to identify the one purchase that changes the next part of the run.

At A3 and above, I want Gold attached to a job.

If the deck already kills normal enemies comfortably, another attack has to justify its price.

If the next Elite exposes weak defense, block or a defensive potion can be worth more than a speculative synergy piece.

That is the first point in the climb where economy starts changing card evaluation rather than merely limiting how much I can buy.

<h2 id="a4-a7" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A4–A7 remove the safety nets that used to repair weak decisions
</h2>

The middle of the Ascension ladder does something more uncomfortable than simply increasing enemy stats.

It removes my margin for error.

A4 reduces potion capacity.

A5 puts Ascender's Bane into the deck.

A6 removes one Rest Site from every Act.

A7 lowers the frequency of upgraded and Rare cards.

None of those changes alone needs to kill the run.

Together they make it much harder to carry a half-finished deck while waiting for the game to rescue it.

<h3 id="a4-potions" className="scroll-mt-24">
  One fewer potion slot makes every stored potion compete with the next one
</h3>

At A4, potion decisions start happening before combat.

A full potion belt means the next drop can force a choice immediately. That makes weak potions more expensive to carry and strong ones more valuable to protect for a known problem.

I try to give each potion a likely job.

One may be my insurance for the first Elite.

Another may cover a weak opening hand.

If neither has a clear use and the current fight is costing real HP, holding both indefinitely is not automatically disciplined play.

Potion slots are resources too.

A slot occupied by something I refuse to spend can become the reason I cannot take a better answer later.

<h3 id="a5-consistency" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A5 exposes cards that were already making bad hands worse
</h3>

Ascender's Bane is unplayable, Ethereal, and Eternal, so it repeatedly enters combat as a draw that does not advance the turn.

One bad card does not destroy a functional deck.

What it does is make existing inconsistency easier to see.

If I already have several speculative powers, weak starter cards, and situational payoffs, Ascender's Bane increases the chance that an ordinary hand contains too little block or damage to solve the turn in front of me.

That pushes me toward better draw quality rather than a magic deck-size target.

Removal becomes valuable when a card has genuinely stopped helping.

Skipping a reward becomes valuable when adding it makes the next shuffle worse.

Flexible cards become more attractive because they reduce the number of hands where I have plenty of cards but no useful action.

A small deck full of missing answers can still lose.

The goal is not thinness.

The goal is fewer turns where the draw itself creates the problem.

<h3 id="a6-rest-sites" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A6 makes Rest versus Smith a decision I start several rooms early
</h3>

With one fewer Rest Site per Act, arriving at a fire with low HP costs more than the healing itself.

It may also cost the upgrade I needed.

That makes previous fights part of the campfire decision.

If I leak 15 unnecessary HP two rooms before the only useful Rest Site, the real price may be:

**15 HP + one missed Smith.**

On one A10 run, Regal Pillow made resting attractive for safety. Then the available Glitterstream upgrade changed the math.

The card showed:

**13 Block now + 7 Block next turn**

and the upgrade changed that to:

**15 Block now + 9 Block next turn.**

That is **20 total printed Block across two turns becoming 24**.

The extra four points were not exciting in isolation. The important part was that the upgrade improved the exact defensive tool the deck expected to use repeatedly.

That can be worth more than healing when the current HP total already survives the next check.

Other fires go the opposite way.

If one bad draw puts the next Elite into lethal range, a powerful upgrade still does nothing from the death screen.

The campfire question I use is:

**Which choice removes the most realistic way this run dies before the next fire?**

<h3 id="a7-scarcity" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A7 makes waiting for the perfect reward a losing plan
</h3>

Scarcity reduces how often upgraded and Rare cards appear.

By this point, I cannot draft as though the card that completes my imagined build is guaranteed to arrive.

If the deck needs block now, a solid common block card can matter more than preserving a slot for an ideal Rare that may never appear.

If Act 2 demands area damage, I want that problem solved before the dangerous multi-enemy fight, not after another three rewards.

This is also where forcing an archetype becomes especially expensive.

A reward that technically belongs to the theme can still be wrong when it does not help with the current combat checks.

The farther I climb, the more I value cards that are already useful before the dream version of the deck exists.

<h2 id="a8-a10" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A8–A10 punish the turn where my deck fails to function
</h2>

A8 gives enemies more HP.

A9 increases enemy damage.

Those are simple modifiers with complicated consequences because every earlier restriction is still active.

A fight lasting one extra turn means one more enemy action.

That attack lands while Ancient healing is weaker, potion capacity is smaller, Rest Sites are less common, and the deck is already carrying Ascender's Bane.

At this point, I stop judging a deck by its best turn.

I look at the ugly one.

What happens when my main block card is near the bottom?

Can I draw toward it?

Can another card cover the turn?

Do I have enough damage to end the fight instead?

Does my energy engine actually have something productive to spend energy on?

A high ceiling helps.

A missing floor kills runs.

<h3 id="bad-card-rewards" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Some A10 runs never hand me a clean build direction
</h3>

A difficult high-Ascension run does not always fail because I chose the wrong archetype.

Sometimes the rewards simply refuse to offer one.

One A10 sequence kept producing awkward choices while the deck still needed straightforward combat strength. A reward with **Hotfix, Claw, and Tesla Coil** is a good picture of the problem: three real options are on the screen, but that does not mean one of them cleanly fixes what the deck currently lacks.

The run around that stretch had already struggled to find straightforward attacks and block.

A resource that would normally be saved for the first Elite had to be spent earlier. Draws were awkward as well. Even after surviving the first Elite, the deck was still searching for basic pieces.

That is where “build identity” becomes the wrong priority.

I lower the bar to the next concrete problem:

Can I kill the next hallway enemy?

Can I block its dangerous turn?

Do I need draw more than another payoff?

Can I afford the Elite I originally planned?

A plain solution is better than an elegant card that becomes useful two Acts from now.

High Ascension does not guarantee that the deck will become recognizable early.

Sometimes adaptation is the strategy for most of Act 1.

<h3 id="a10-double-boss" className="scroll-mt-24">
  A10 changes Act 3 because one solved boss matchup is no longer enough
</h3>

A10 ends Act 3 with **two boss fights instead of one**.

The extra requirement changes what “ready for the boss” means.

A deck that spends every potion and all of its flexibility solving one matchup may still be too narrow for the second fight.

By late Act 3, I care about four things more than adding another clever interaction.

**The first cycle has to function.**  
If the deck needs several lucky draws before block, draw, or scaling appears, two consecutive bosses give that weakness more opportunities to matter.

**Defense has to survive beyond one big turn.**  
A single explosive block card is useful. A repeatable defensive engine is safer.

**The damage plan has to keep working after resources are spent.**  
The first fight may consume potions or temporary answers that are gone for the next one.

**A bad draw needs a recovery line.**  
Draw, retain, recursion, energy storage, or another consistency tool can stop one weak hand from deciding the entire climb.

I leave exact boss HP, moves, phases, and encounter mechanics with the boss data on SpireGenius. The Ascension-specific problem is the endurance test: the deck must keep functioning after the first boss has already taken something from it.

<h2 id="elite-risk-changes" className="scroll-mt-24">
  The value of another Elite falls once I can already beat what is ahead
</h2>

A1 encourages me to think about Elites as opportunities.

That lesson needs an endpoint.

Early in a run, a relic may solve the weakness that makes Act 2 possible. An Elite can absolutely be worth spending HP or a potion to get it.

Late in a mature run, the calculation changes.

If the remaining bosses are already covered, another Elite needs to improve something specific enough to justify the risk.

This became obvious in an A10 Regent run where Act 3 still offered optional Elite pressure after the deck had already demonstrated enough power for the bosses.

At that point, the reward had less leverage.

Extra HP loss could still hurt.

A consumed potion could still hurt.

The unknown relic was no longer guaranteed to solve anything important.

That is when the safer branch becomes progression rather than avoidance.

I ask one question before the optional Elite:

**What weakness am I expecting its reward to fix?**

If I cannot name one, keeping the run intact can be worth more than adding one more relic.

<h2 id="why-high-ascension-runs-fail" className="scroll-mt-24">
  When a strong-looking run collapses, the cause is usually visible earlier
</h2>

High Ascension makes late failures look sudden.

Often the setup started much earlier.

A Rest forced in Act 2 may trace back to HP lost on an unnecessary Elite.

A weak shop may trace back to Gold spent before A3's tighter economy became visible.

A boss hand full of setup cards may trace back to rewards that were individually attractive but never improved first-cycle consistency.

A9 enemy damage may appear to be the reason a run died, while the deeper problem is that the deck had no second defensive answer when its best block card bottom-decked.

That is why I review losses from the point where resources started disappearing, not only from the final fight.

A rough A10 run made this especially clear. Card rewards were poor, the draws were poor, and the run entered the Act 1 boss under heavy pressure. It eventually died, but the useful lesson was not the final lethal turn.

The run had been paying for weak options and awkward draws for most of the Act.

That kind of loss gives me something actionable for the next climb:

take a safer branch;

spend the potion earlier;

accept the ordinary block card;

or stop waiting for a payoff that the deck has not earned yet.

<h2 id="when-to-climb" className="scroll-mt-24">
  I move to the next Ascension when my decisions feel repeatable
</h2>

I do not need to win every run before increasing the level.

Variance makes that a poor standard.

What I want is enough control over the current difficulty that most losses have an explanation beyond “I got unlucky.”

Before climbing, I want to be able to identify why I took an Elite, what the next Rest Site was supposed to do, which weakness a shop purchase fixed, and why a card reward entered the deck at all.

The same applies when I lose.

If I can point to the branch that consumed too much HP, the speculative cards that weakened the first shuffle, or the missing defensive job that the next Act exposed, the run gave me a useful answer.

When every loss still feels mysterious, another Ascension modifier usually makes the mystery more expensive.

A1–A3 teach me to plan the run.

A4–A7 teach me to stop relying on spare resources to repair it.

A8–A10 ask whether the deck can perform those decisions consistently when the numbers stop forgiving weak turn
