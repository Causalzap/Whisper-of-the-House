This page covers the Big Walk puzzles that can block the Red Tower and Green
Tower routes: the green communication room, timed maze, basketball challenge,
projectile relay, five-pose platforms, green path-order field, counting room,
and blindfold panel building.

The exact number of active controls can change with the Host's two-player,
three-player, or four-plus-player world version. The core mechanic remains the
same, so I focus on the role split, communication method, reset rule, and mistake
that usually wastes the most time.

<h2 id="green-communication-room">
  Big Walk green communication room puzzle
</h2>

<AdUnit placement="article_after_first_h2" />

One player enters the enclosed green room while the second player remains at the
outside controls. The door can remove normal communication at the moment the
inside player needs help, so I agree on visual signals before closing it.

My setup is:

1. the inside player stands where the outside player can still see them;
2. the outside player identifies every available control before pressing one;
3. both players agree that one jump means “yes” and repeated jumping means “change it”;
4. the outside player presses one control at a time;
5. both wait for a clear success response before changing another control.

I do not repeatedly open the door to ask what happened. Reopening the room can
discard the useful state and force the group to reconstruct which control was
correct.

The clean role split is simple: the inside player reports position and success;
the outside player owns the controls and changes only one input at a time.

<h2 id="timed-maze">
  Big Walk timed maze puzzle
</h2>

The active object expires when it spends too long in the maze. The biggest waste
is activating it before the receiving player has reached the wall opening.

I solve it as a handoff:

1. place one player beside the activation point;
2. send the second player through the maze to the receiving side of the wall gap;
3. confirm that both players can see the handoff point;
4. activate the object;
5. carry it directly to the opening;
6. pass it through the gap;
7. let the receiving player complete the shorter final route to the socket.

When the object expires, I leave both players in position and restart. Walking
both players back to the beginning turns one failed attempt into another full
setup lap.

<h2 id="basketball-puzzle">
  Big Walk basketball puzzle
</h2>

In the two-player layout, this challenge completes after roughly **five successful
scores**.

The ball travels forward and then drops sharply. Repeated maximum-force throws
are less useful than keeping one release position and correcting the height.

I use this approach:

1. place one player in the shooting position;
2. let the second player lift or support the shooter when the angle is too low;
3. make one controlled throw;
4. correct the release point rather than changing force, height, and angle together;
5. keep the same setup after the first successful score.

Once the Head appears, I move it away from the ball area before taking another
practice shot. The progression reward is easier to lose when it remains mixed
with the challenge objects.

<h2 id="projectile-relay">
  Big Walk timed projectile relay puzzle
</h2>

This launcher sends an active object across the Train Station route. The receiving
player should already be standing at the destination before the starting player
presses the control.

My role split is:

- the starting player owns the launcher and activation;
- the receiving player owns the destination socket;
- both confirm the socket before launch;
- the starting player does not chase the projectile;
- the receiving player catches, redirects, or carries it into the socket before
  the timer ends.

When the destination is unclear, I reset before launching again. Improvising
after the object is already moving leaves both players chasing it without anyone
protecting the socket.

<h2 id="five-pose">
  Big Walk five-pose platform puzzle
</h2>

One player stays with the pose panel while the field player visits five distant
viewing positions. I wait for daylight because trees, slopes, and distance make
the field player difficult to track at night.

The five poses in the two-player layout were:

1. sitting with the right hand raised;
2. standing with both arms down;
3. standing with the right arm extended;
4. both hands raised;
5. left hand raised with the right arm extended.

The field player reports the poses in viewing-station order. The panel player
places the matching pieces in that same order.

I describe the body position rather than inventing nicknames. Calls such as
“right arm extended” and “both hands raised” remain clear after several pieces
have already been placed.

Before confirming the final sequence, I repeat all five calls from the beginning.
This catches a swapped third or fourth pose without sending the field player back
across the entire area.

<h2 id="green-path-order">
  Big Walk green path-order puzzle
</h2>

This field is an **order puzzle**, not a collection of permanently correct and
incorrect nodes.

A node can turn green when reached at the correct position in the sequence and
red when reached too early. That means a failed node is not automatically removed
from every future attempt.

I solve the route by preserving a confirmed prefix:

1. one player owns the walking route;
2. the second player watches the latest confidently correct node;
3. place one loose object beside that checkpoint;
4. after a reset, replay the confirmed route without changing it;
5. after the checkpoint, try only one new neighboring node;
6. when it fails, reject it for that position only;
7. when it succeeds, move the checkpoint object forward.

I do not scatter objects beside every failed node. Too many markers make it
impossible to tell whether a node failed because it was wrong or because it was
attempted at the wrong step.

The two-player solve took about **39 minutes**. The biggest improvement was
marking the last confirmed late-stage node rather than trying to memorize or
label the entire field at once.

When both players begin disagreeing about an early step, I stop and replay the
known prefix slowly. Continuing from a corrupted sequence wastes more time than a
short reset.

<h2 id="counting-symbols">
  Big Walk counting and symbol puzzle
</h2>

In the two-player layout, the inside player starts the puzzle by **closing the
lower door**. Heads then appear in groups and must be counted until the complete
sequence stops.

Opening the door resets the attempt, and the enclosed room blocks the
Walkie-talkie. The players therefore need a number language before the door
closes.

The system I use is:

- star = `10`;
- triangle = `5`;
- slash = `1`;
- a slash placed on the right is added;
- a slash placed on the left is subtracted.

For `27`, I use two stars, one triangle, and two slashes on the right.

For `29`, subtraction can reduce the number of required pieces: place one slash
to the left of a ten, then combine that value with the remaining tens.

The inside player should not stop counting after the first pause. More Heads can
appear after an early cluster. I wait until the entire sequence has ended, repeat
the total once, and only then build the symbol message.

The outside player reads the message from the agreed direction and enters the
result. When the count is correct but the input fails, I check the arithmetic and
piece orientation before changing the communication system.

The complete learning process took about **12 minutes** in the two-player route.

<h2 id="blindfold-puzzle">
  Big Walk blindfold panel puzzle
</h2>

Placing the large face on a player removes that player's vision. It can also knock
off the Backpack and carried equipment, so I stage the current Head and valuable
gear outside before anyone wears it.

The blindfolded player handles the movable panels. The sighted player remains
outside and gives movement instructions.

The two-player layout used three required panels:

1. a P-shaped panel;
2. a slash panel;
3. a swirl shaped like a `6`.

The slow approach is carrying one unknown panel upstairs, discovering that it is
wrong, and returning to the shelf. I sort the pieces first:

1. guide the blindfolded player to the shelf near the entrance;
2. pull several panels down and spread them across the floor;
3. let the sighted player identify the required shape;
4. keep only that panel in hand;
5. guide the player up the ramps to the matching socket;
6. return to the same floor pile for the next panel.

I use clock-face directions only while the player is stationary. During movement,
I use short corrections such as “slightly right,” “straight,” and “stop.”
Repeated 90-degree instructions during jumps or falls make the player lose their
orientation.

The P panel went first, the slash second, and the swirl last in the two-player
layout. The complete solve took about **23 minutes**.

<h2 id="stuck">
  Big Walk puzzle fixes
</h2>

### A completed puzzle did not increase tower progress

Find the physical Head reward and carry it to the correct receiver. Completing a
challenge and submitting its Head are separate actions.

### We cannot hear each other after a door closes

Treat the silence as part of the interaction. Use the visual signals, removable
pieces, panels, and outside controls that both players can still observe.

### A timed object expires before the handoff

Place the receiving player at the final side before activation. Do not start the
timer while both players are still moving into position.

### The same green-path node changes between attempts

Its result depends on sequence position. Replay the confirmed prefix, then change
only the first unconfirmed step.

### A distance puzzle is unreadable at night

Leave the challenge equipment at the structure and wait for daylight. Do not
carry every piece back to the tower unless the group also needs to deposit Heads.

### The Head appeared, but we lost it while continuing the puzzle

Move the reward to a named landmark immediately. Do not leave it mixed with balls,
panels, or timed objects after the challenge has already completed.

<h2 id="faq">
  Big Walk puzzle FAQ
</h2>

### Are the puzzle layouts identical for every group size?

No. Two-player, three-player, and four-plus worlds can assign different numbers
of controls or roles. The central mechanic remains recognizable, but the group
must divide the additional interactions among the players present.

### Do I need every nearby Head to complete a tower?

No. The Red and Green receivers each need five Heads, while additional challenges
remain available. I choose the five that fit the current route and equipment.

### Is the 4166 / 1899 coordinate challenge required for the Map Room?

No. The Red Tower can be completed with five other Head challenges. I return to
the coordinate equipment after the Map Room is open and the group has better
navigation context.

### Can Challenge Skipping replace a missing player?

It can bypass an interaction when the group needs it, but it does not change a
world created for a different player count. I still choose the correct version
before starting.
