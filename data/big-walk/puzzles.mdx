Big Walk has plenty of puzzles where finding the structure is easy and understanding **what the game expects from each player** is the real problem.

I use this guide for the ordinary challenge mechanics that repeatedly stopped our group after we had already reached the correct location:

- green communication room;
- timed maze;
- basketball challenge;
- timed projectile relay;
- five-pose telescope puzzle;
- green path-order field;
- counting and symbols room;
- blindfold panel building.

The biggest time saver across all of them is the same: **set the roles before touching the thing that starts the puzzle**.

<h2 id="green-communication-room">How to solve the green communication room</h2>

<AdUnit placement="article_after_first_h2" />

The problem in the green communication room is not finding the controls. It is that normal communication can disappear **after one player is already enclosed**.

I solve that before closing the door.

My two-player setup is:

1. the inside player stands somewhere the outside player can still see;
2. the outside player checks every available control before touching one;
3. we agree on one simple visual signal for “correct” and another for “change it”;
4. only the outside player changes controls;
5. only one control changes at a time;
6. both players wait for an obvious response before moving to the next input.

We used jumping because it is visible through the room:

- **one jump** = yes / keep it;
- **repeated jumps** = change it.

The exact signal does not matter. Having one before the door closes does.

### Do not keep reopening the room

My first instinct was to reopen the door whenever we became unsure.

That makes the puzzle slower.

Once we have a usable communication system, I preserve the current state and change only one outside control at a time.

If both players start changing things, we lose the ability to tell which input produced the useful response.

I keep the role split fixed:

**inside player observes → outside player operates.**

<h2 id="timed-maze">How to solve the timed maze</h2>

The active object has a limited useful window once it is started.

The mistake is activating it while both players are still figuring out where to stand.

I prepare the entire handoff first.

My route is:

1. leave one player beside the activation point;
2. send the second player through the maze;
3. place that player on the far side of the wall opening;
4. make sure both players know exactly where the handoff happens;
5. only then activate the object;
6. carry it directly to the wall gap;
7. pass it through;
8. let the receiving player finish the shorter final route.

### What I do after a failed attempt

If the object expires after the handoff, I **leave the receiving player where they are**.

Only the starting player resets.

Walking both players back to the beginning turns a short retry into another full maze traversal.

If the failure happened before the handoff point, then I reset both roles because the original setup itself was wrong.

<h2 id="basketball-puzzle">How to solve the basketball puzzle</h2>

In my two-player world, this challenge completed after roughly **five successful scores**.

The difficult part was not generating enough power. The ball already travels forward well; it then drops sharply.

That means repeated full-force throws are less useful than finding one repeatable release.

I use:

1. one fixed shooting position;
2. one player as the shooter;
3. the second player as support when extra release height is needed;
4. one controlled throw;
5. one small correction after each miss.

Once I make the first clean score, I stop experimenting.

I repeat the same body position, force, and general angle.

### Change one variable at a time

This puzzle became much worse whenever I changed:

- force;
- height;
- body position;
- release angle;

all after the same miss.

I keep three of those stable and correct only the obvious problem.

If the shot consistently lands short, I adjust the release or support height rather than rebuilding the whole throw.

Once the Head appears, I move it away from the shooting area before anyone takes another shot.

Loose puzzle objects and the actual progression reward look much less distinct once everyone starts kicking things around.

<h2 id="projectile-relay">How to solve the timed projectile relay</h2>

This launcher puzzle is another case where **starting early is the main mistake**.

One player controls the launch.

Another player needs to already be standing at the destination.

I split the roles as:

- **launcher player:** activation and launch only;
- **receiver:** destination socket and final object handling.

The launcher player does not chase the projectile.

The receiver does not leave the destination after launch.

Before activating anything, both players identify the final socket.

If we cannot both point to the same destination, I do not launch yet.

### Reset before another blind launch

When the first attempt fails because nobody knew where the object was going, I stop.

I do not immediately fire another one just because the launcher is ready.

We locate the socket first, reposition the receiver, and then make the next launch meaningful.

<h2 id="five-pose">How to solve the five-pose telescope puzzle</h2>

This is the **pose / telescope challenge**, not the colored Pegboard puzzle.

One player stays near the answer area while the field player visits **five distant viewing positions** and reports what each figure is doing.

In my two-player layout, the five poses were:

1. sitting with the right hand raised;
2. standing with both arms down;
3. standing with the right arm extended;
4. both hands raised;
5. left hand raised with the right arm extended.

I report them in **viewing-station order**.

I never switch halfway to “the tall one,” “the funny one,” or another nickname that depends on memory.

Calls such as:

- “right arm extended”;
- “both hands raised”;
- “sitting, right hand up”;

remain understandable even several minutes later.

### Wait for daylight if the silhouettes are unclear

This is one puzzle where I stop forcing progress at night.

Trees, terrain, and distance can make a raised arm difficult to distinguish from the background.

If we are arguing about whether an arm is horizontal or down, I leave the pieces at the puzzle and wait for better visibility.

Before confirming the final answer, I read all five poses back from the beginning.

That catches a swapped middle entry without sending the field player around the entire route again.

<h2 id="green-path-order">How to solve the green path-order puzzle</h2>

The green field is an **order puzzle**.

A node turning red does not necessarily mean:

> never step on this node.

It can mean:

> this node is wrong **at this point in the sequence**.

That distinction is the entire puzzle.

I solve it by maintaining a confirmed prefix.

My method is:

1. one player owns the walking route;
2. the second player watches the last confidently correct node;
3. place one loose object beside that latest checkpoint;
4. after a reset, replay the confirmed prefix exactly;
5. after the checkpoint, try only one new neighboring node;
6. if it fails, reject it for that sequence position;
7. if it succeeds, move the marker forward.

### Do not mark every failed node

We tried using too many loose objects as notes.

That becomes worse than using none.

A marker beside a red node does not tell me **why** it failed:

- wrong node entirely;
- correct node at the wrong sequence position;
- or a route mistake happened before reaching it.

I therefore mark only the end of the confirmed prefix.

When the two players start disagreeing about an early step, I stop extending the route and replay the known sequence slowly.

Continuing from a corrupted prefix wastes much more time.

<h2 id="counting-symbols">How to solve the counting and symbols puzzle</h2>

In my two-player layout, the inside player begins this challenge by **closing the lower door**.

After that, groups of Heads appear and need to be counted.

The room blocks normal communication, so I establish the number language **before closing the door**.

The values we used were:

- star = `10`;
- triangle = `5`;
- slash = `1`;
- slash on the right = add;
- slash on the left = subtract.

For example, I can build `27` as:

**10 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1**

using two stars, one triangle, and two slashes on the additive side.

For some totals, subtraction lets me communicate the value with fewer pieces.

### Do not stop counting at the first pause

This was the more dangerous mistake.

A group can appear, stop, and make it feel like the sequence is over.

Then more can arrive.

I wait until I am confident the full sequence has finished.

Only then do I:

1. repeat the total once;
2. construct the number message;
3. let the outside player interpret it;
4. commit the answer.

If the result fails but the count still feels correct, I inspect:

- arithmetic;
- left/right slash placement;
- reading direction;

before inventing a completely new signaling system.

<h2 id="blindfold-puzzle">How to solve the blindfold panel puzzle</h2>

The large face in this building removes the wearer's vision.

More importantly, putting it on can interfere with the player's **Backpack and carried gear**.

Before anyone wears it, I move:

- the current Head;
- Backpack;
- useful tools;
- anything I do not want scattered;

outside the working route.

The role split is:

- **blindfolded player:** carries and places panels;
- **sighted player:** identifies shapes and guides movement.

In my two-player layout, the three required shapes were:

1. a P-shaped panel;
2. a slash panel;
3. a swirl shaped like a `6`.

The slow method is carrying one unknown panel upstairs, learning that it is wrong, then walking back down blind.

I sort them first.

My sequence is:

1. guide the blindfolded player to the panel shelf;
2. pull several panels down;
3. spread them across the floor near the entrance;
4. have the sighted player identify the required shape;
5. let the blindfolded player hold only that piece;
6. guide them up the route;
7. place it;
8. return to the same organized floor pile.

### Use short movement calls

Clock-face directions are useful while the blindfolded player is standing still.

During actual movement, I switch to short commands:

- slightly left;
- slightly right;
- straight;
- stop;
- jump.

Repeated “turn 90 degrees” calls while somebody is falling, climbing, or recovering from a collision quickly destroy their orientation.

In my two-player layout, the **P went first, slash second, and swirl last**.

I treat that as the sequence from that layout, not a guarantee that every player-count version presents identical roles.

<h2 id="stuck">Big Walk puzzle fixes</h2>

### The puzzle started before everyone was ready

Reset before trying to improvise around bad positioning.

For timed puzzles, I put the receiver at the destination first.

For communication puzzles, I agree on the signal system first.

For observation puzzles, I agree on the reading order first.

### We cannot hear each other after a door closes

I assume the silence is part of the mechanic before assuming something is broken.

I look for:

- windows;
- lights;
- removable pieces;
- outside buttons;
- glass that can be tapped;
- visible body movements.

The challenge usually leaves another information channel available.

### A timed object expires before the handoff

I keep the player nearest the destination in position.

Only the starting side resets unless the whole setup was wrong.

### A sequence changes after a reset

I preserve only the **confirmed prefix**.

I do not assume a node or object that failed at step four is permanently incorrect. It may belong at step six.

### A distant observation puzzle is unreadable

I wait for daylight rather than guessing.

If the important objects can stay at the puzzle safely, I leave them there instead of carrying everything back across the island.

### A completed challenge produced a Head and then we lost it

I stop solving.

First I search:

1. the exact reward point;
2. the nearest loose-object pile;
3. the current carrier;
4. Backpacks;
5. the named landmark where we normally stage completed rewards.

I do not replay the puzzle until I have checked whether the physical reward already exists.

### The puzzle looks different from a solution for another group

I check the world version.

Big Walk can change the number of active roles or controls depending on player count, while keeping the same central mechanic.

I copy the **logic**, not blindly the number of buttons or roles from another setup.

<h2 id="faq">Big Walk puzzle FAQ</h2>

### Is the colored Peg Puzzle the same as the five-pose telescope puzzle?

No.

The telescope challenge asks one player to observe five distant poses and communicate their order. The [Colored Peg Puzzle](/big-walk/peg-puzzle) asks the group to collect colored pieces and reproduce the surrounding landscape on a Pegboard.

### Should I activate a timed puzzle as soon as I find it?

Usually no.

I first locate the destination and place every required player in position. Then I activate the timer.

### Is it worth waiting for daylight?

Yes when the challenge depends on reading distant poses, small objects, terrain, or color differences.

I do not wait for daylight on a puzzle where all relevant controls are already clearly visible indoors.

### Should both players experiment with the controls?

Usually no.

For communication and sequence puzzles, I prefer one operator and one observer. When both players change the puzzle state simultaneously, troubleshooting becomes much harder.

### Are puzzle layouts identical in every player-count world?

No.

The core mechanic can stay the same while additional players receive extra roles, controls, or interactions. I use the current world state as the final authority.

### Where should I go if the puzzle I need is not covered here?

Use the [Big Walk Hub](/big-walk) if the problem is progression or deciding where to go next.

Use the [Colored Peg Puzzle guide](/big-walk/peg-puzzle), [4166 / 1899 guide](/big-walk/4166-1899-puzzle), or [Purple Puzzles guide](/big-walk/purple-puzzles) when the challenge belongs to one of those dedicated routes.
