It covers the green communication room, timed maze, basketball challenge, projectile relay, five-pose platforms, green path-order field, counting and symbols room, and blindfold panel building.

I use this page for **role assignment, communication rules, reset conditions, and exact puzzle logic**. I use the tower walkthroughs for Head collection, return routes, key cutters, and transport activation.

<h2 id="green-communication-room">How to solve the green communication room</h2>

<AdUnit placement="article_after_first_h2" />

One player enters the enclosed green room while another remains at the outside controls. The door can remove normal communication at the exact moment the inside player needs help, so I agree on visual signals before closing it.

My setup is:

1. the inside player stands where the outside player can still see them;
2. the outside player identifies every available control before pressing one;
3. both players agree that one jump means “yes” and repeated jumping means “change it”;
4. the outside player changes only one control at a time;
5. both wait for a clear success response before changing anything else.

I do not repeatedly reopen the door to ask what happened. Reopening the room can discard the useful state and force the group to reconstruct which control was correct.

**Common mistake:** both players try to own the controls. I keep the role split fixed: the inside player reports position and success, while the outside player changes inputs.

<h2 id="timed-maze">How to solve the timed maze</h2>

The active object expires when it spends too long in the maze. The most common failure is activating it before the receiving player has reached the wall opening.

I solve it as a prepared handoff:

1. keep one player beside the activation point;
2. send the second player through the maze to the receiving side of the wall gap;
3. confirm that both players can see the handoff point;
4. activate the object;
5. carry it directly to the opening;
6. pass it through the gap;
7. let the receiving player complete the shorter route to the socket.

When the object expires, I leave the receiving player in position and restart from the activation side. Walking both players back to the beginning turns one failed attempt into another full setup lap.

<h2 id="basketball-puzzle">How to solve the basketball puzzle</h2>

In my two-player world, the challenge completed after roughly **five successful scores**.

The ball travels forward and then drops sharply. Repeated maximum-force throws are less useful than keeping one release position and correcting only the height.

I use this method:

1. place one player in the shooting position;
2. let the second player lift or support the shooter when the release angle is too low;
3. make one controlled throw;
4. correct the release point without changing force, height, and angle together;
5. keep the same setup after the first successful score.

Once the Head appears, I move it away from the ball area before taking another practice shot.

**Common mistake:** changing several variables after every miss. I keep the throw force stable and adjust one part of the release at a time.

<h2 id="projectile-relay">How to solve the timed projectile relay</h2>

This launcher sends an active object toward a distant socket. The receiving player should already be standing at the destination before the starting player presses the control.

My role split is:

- the starting player owns the launcher and activation;
- the receiving player owns the destination socket;
- both players confirm the socket before launch;
- the starting player does not chase the projectile;
- the receiving player catches, redirects, or carries it into the socket before the timer ends.

When the destination is unclear, I reset before launching again. Activating first and searching second leaves both players chasing the object without anyone protecting the socket.

<h2 id="five-pose">How to solve the five-pose platform puzzle</h2>

One player stays with the pose panel while the field player visits five distant viewing positions. I wait for daylight because trees, slopes, and distance can make the poses difficult to read.

The five poses in my two-player layout were:

1. sitting with the right hand raised;
2. standing with both arms down;
3. standing with the right arm extended;
4. both hands raised;
5. left hand raised with the right arm extended.

The field player reports the poses in viewing-station order. The panel player places the matching pieces in that same order.

I describe the body position instead of inventing nicknames. Calls such as “right arm extended” and “both hands raised” remain clear after several pieces have already been placed.

Before confirming the final sequence, I repeat all five calls from the beginning. This catches a swapped middle pose without sending the field player across the entire area again.

<h2 id="green-path-order">How to solve the green path-order puzzle</h2>

This field is an **order puzzle**, not a collection of permanently correct and incorrect nodes.

A node can turn green when reached at the correct position in the sequence and red when reached too early. A failed node is therefore not automatically wrong for every future step.

I solve the route by preserving a confirmed prefix:

1. one player owns the walking route;
2. the second player watches the latest confidently correct node;
3. place one loose object beside that checkpoint;
4. after a reset, replay the confirmed route without changing it;
5. after the checkpoint, try only one new neighboring node;
6. when it fails, reject it for that sequence position only;
7. when it succeeds, move the checkpoint object forward.

I do not scatter objects beside every failed node. Too many markers make it impossible to tell whether a node failed because it was wrong or because it was attempted at the wrong step.

When both players begin disagreeing about an early step, I stop and replay the known prefix slowly. Continuing from a corrupted sequence wastes more time than a clean reset.

<h2 id="counting-symbols">How to solve the counting and symbols puzzle</h2>

In the two-player layout, the inside player starts the puzzle by **closing the lower door**. Heads then appear in groups and must be counted until the complete sequence stops.

Opening the door resets the attempt, and the enclosed room blocks normal communication. Both players therefore need a number language before the door closes.

The system I use is:

- star = `10`;
- triangle = `5`;
- slash = `1`;
- a slash placed on the right is added;
- a slash placed on the left is subtracted.

For `27`, I use two stars, one triangle, and two slashes on the right.

For `29`, subtraction can reduce the number of pieces: place one slash to the left of a ten, then combine that value with the remaining tens.

The inside player should not stop counting after the first pause. More Heads can appear after an early cluster. I wait until the entire sequence has ended, repeat the total once, and only then build the symbol message.

The outside player reads the message from the agreed direction and enters the result. When the count seems correct but the input fails, I check the arithmetic and piece orientation before changing the communication system.

<h2 id="blindfold-puzzle">How to solve the blindfold panel puzzle</h2>

Placing the large face on a player removes that player's vision. It can also knock off the Backpack and carried equipment, so I stage the current Head and valuable gear outside before anyone wears it.

The blindfolded player handles the movable panels. The sighted player remains outside and gives movement instructions.

The two-player layout used three required panels:

1. a P-shaped panel;
2. a slash panel;
3. a swirl shaped like a `6`.

The slow approach is carrying one unknown panel upstairs, discovering it is wrong, and returning to the shelf. I sort the pieces first:

1. guide the blindfolded player to the shelf near the entrance;
2. pull several panels down and spread them across the floor;
3. let the sighted player identify the required shape;
4. keep only that panel in hand;
5. guide the player up the ramps to the matching socket;
6. return to the same floor pile for the next panel.

I use clock-face directions only while the player is stationary. During movement, I use short corrections such as “slightly right,” “straight,” and “stop.” Repeated 90-degree instructions during jumps or falls make the blindfolded player lose orientation.

The P panel went first, the slash second, and the swirl last in my two-player layout.

<h2 id="stuck">Big Walk puzzle fixes</h2>

### A completed puzzle did not increase tower progress

Find the physical Head reward and carry it to the active receiver. Completing a challenge and submitting its Head are separate actions.

### We cannot hear each other after a door closes

Treat the silence as part of the interaction. Use visual signals, removable pieces, panels, and outside controls that both players can still observe.

### A timed object expires before the handoff

Place the receiving player at the destination before activation. Do not start the timer while both players are still moving into position.

### The same green-path node changes between attempts

Its result depends on sequence position. Replay the confirmed prefix, then change only the first unconfirmed step.

### A distance puzzle is unreadable at night

Leave the challenge equipment at the structure and wait for daylight. Do not carry every piece back to a tower unless the group also needs to deposit Heads.

### The Head appeared, but we lost it while continuing the puzzle

Move the reward to a named landmark immediately. Do not leave it mixed with balls, panels, or timed objects after the challenge is complete.

### The final key is still not appearing

Clear every purple puzzle, check the full map for older unfinished challenges, and confirm that all required completion objects were delivered. The final key is a full-completion check, not a reward for the purple set alone.

<h2 id="faq">Big Walk puzzle FAQ</h2>

### Are the puzzle layouts identical for every group size?

No. Two-player, three-player, and four-plus worlds can assign different numbers of controls or roles. The central mechanic remains recognizable, but the group must divide the additional interactions among the players present.

### Do I need every nearby Head to complete a tower?

No. A receiver needs a fixed number of physical Heads while additional challenges remain available. I choose puzzles that fit the current route, player count, and equipment.

### Are Heads locked to the nearest tower color?

No. A completed physical Head can be used at the active receiver.

### Can Challenge Skipping replace a missing player?

It can bypass an interaction when the group needs it, but it does not change a world created for a different player count. I still choose the correct version before starting.

### Does completing every puzzle on this page unlock Big Game?

Not by itself. This page covers eight specific mechanics. Big Game requires complete puzzle progression across the save, including the seven post-game purple puzzles and any normal puzzle left unfinished.
