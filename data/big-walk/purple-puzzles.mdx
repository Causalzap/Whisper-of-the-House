Big Walk adds **seven Purple Puzzles** after the main ending, and I treat them very differently from the normal puzzle cleanup.

Most of them are not difficult because of a hidden answer. They are difficult because the game removes normal communication, separates the group, asks you to move awkward physical objects, or simply forces you to wait.

The biggest improvement I made was changing the order. I start the **30-minute timer first**, leave it running, and spend that waiting time clearing the other Purple Puzzles.

  </ol>

The order above is my cleanup order, not an official puzzle numbering system. The useful idea is to let the one puzzle that consumes real-world time run in the background while the group works elsewhere.

<h2 id="purple-30-minute-puzzle">Start the 30-minute Purple Puzzle first</h2>

This is the easiest puzzle mechanically and the worst one to leave until last.

Inside the room is a timer mechanism similar to the shorter timed challenges elsewhere in the game.

I press the button and let it run.

The wait is **30 real minutes**.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image

    alt="Big Walk Purple Puzzle room with the 30-minute timer button"
    width={1280}

    className="h-auto w-full rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    I start this timer before working on the other Purple Puzzles instead of spending half an hour standing in the room.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

I do **not** wait beside it.

Once the timer starts, I leave and work on another Purple Puzzle.

That turns the 30-minute requirement from dead time into background progress.

My decision rule is:

- if the timer has not started, start it now;
- if it is already running, do not reset or babysit it;
- if everything else is finished, return and check whether the 30 minutes have elapsed.

This is the first thing I would change on a second cleanup run.

<h2 id="purple-golf-heavy-object-puzzle">Move the heavy object back through the Golf Course route</h2>

This was the most time-consuming Purple Puzzle for my small group.

The Purple marker points toward the **Golf Course area**, but the object you need is not sitting beside the destination.

I follow the arrow away from the Purple endpoint until I reach the original puzzle location.

The job is then to bring the large physical object **all the way back**.

The return route can involve the **Train and Drawbridge**, so this is less about solving a code and more about moving a difficult object through the island without losing it.

With **two players**, our attempt took roughly **an hour**.

That is not a fixed completion time, but it is a useful warning: this can take much longer than the other Purple challenges when only two people are pushing and catching the object.

With a larger group, I would assign roles rather than having everyone crowd the object:

1. one or two players push;
2. one moves ahead and checks the next obstacle;
3. one waits on the dangerous side to stop the object from falling;
4. one manages transport or doors when needed.

### Do not rush the object near drops

The worst mistake here is gaining speed just because the next section looks open.

If the object falls off the useful route, the recovery costs much more time than moving slowly.

I stop before:

- narrow approaches;
- transport transitions;
- slopes;
- bridge edges;
- places where the object can roll out of reach.

This is the Purple Puzzle where patience saves the most time.

<h2 id="purple-light-puzzle">Solve the Purple light room with numbered positions</h2>

The light puzzle became much easier once we stopped trying to describe the wall visually.

The room separates information between players. One person can see the relevant light state while the other needs to operate the matching position.

Normal free-form instructions such as:

“the one a little above the left one”

became confusing almost immediately.

Instead, we created a **shared numbering system**.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/big-walk/big-walk-purple-light-number-system.webp"
    alt="Big Walk Purple light puzzle wall used with a numbered communication system"
    width={1280}
    height={720}
    className="h-auto w-full rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Before committing to the room, I give the wall positions a fixed order so every light can be communicated as a number.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

I number the positions in one predictable direction and make sure both players understand the same orientation **before** the puzzle begins.

Then the communication becomes:

**position number → confirm → next position.**

If the room blocks normal speech, I use clicks or flashes to transmit the number.

The exact numbering pattern matters less than consistency.

I do not copy somebody else's arbitrary numbering if my teammate is looking at the wall from another orientation. We stand together first, agree what “1” means, then separate.

### Confirm every number before pressing

I add a simple acknowledgement system.

For example:

- repeated flashes / clicks = number;
- a short confirmation signal = understood;
- then the operator commits.

This adds a few seconds per input but prevents restarting a much longer sequence because one player counted from the opposite side.

<h2 id="purple-music-box-puzzle">Use object numbers for the Purple music puzzle</h2>

The music room creates a different communication problem.

One player hears information that is difficult to describe precisely to the player handling the objects.

I do not try to explain the sound itself.

Instead, before starting, I arrange the available choices in a fixed physical order.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/big-walk/big-walk-purple-music-box-numbered-items.webp"
    alt="Big Walk Purple music box puzzle with movable choices arranged into numbered positions"
    width={1280}
    height={720}
    className="h-auto w-full rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    I spread the choices into fixed positions before entering so the group can refer to them by number instead of trying to describe the sounds.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

My process is:

1. place the choices where they are easy to distinguish;
2. assign them simple numbers;
3. memorize the arrangement before the players are separated;
4. listen to the required sound;
5. tap the glass the matching number of times;
6. have the outside player take that numbered choice;
7. place it into the next required slot.

If I need item 3, I tap **three times**.

That is much more reliable than trying to invent names for sounds while communication is restricted.

Our method took a couple of attempts before the rhythm was clean, but once both players trusted the numbering system it worked.

<h2 id="purple-nine-object-matching-puzzle">Line up all nine objects before the matching puzzle</h2>

Another Purple room uses **nine movable objects**.

Again, the trap is trying to communicate their appearance after the puzzle has already separated the players.

I avoid that by preparing the room first.

I take all **nine** choices down and line them up in a stable order.

**fixed object position → number → taps → placement.**

The important decision is to finish arranging the nine objects **before** committing to the puzzle.

If someone moves the lineup halfway through, the numbering system becomes useless.

I tell everyone not to touch unused pieces unless they are the current answer.

<h2 id="purple-fixed-number-symbol-puzzle">Inspect the fixed number and symbol room together first</h2>

This room initially looked like the one that would need the most complicated callouts.

It became easier when we realized an important detail:

**the relevant symbols / choices do not keep changing.**

That means I can inspect them before relying on restricted communication.

  <Image

    alt="Big Walk Purple number and symbol puzzle with fixed matching choices"
    width={1280}

  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Because the choices stay fixed, I inspect the matching set with my teammate before committing to separate roles.
  </figcaption>

My setup is:

1. both players inspect the available set;
2. identify the pieces that correspond to the target side;
3. remember or isolate the useful ones;
4. move irrelevant pieces out of the working area;
5. only then begin the restricted phase.

This reduces the number of possibilities I need to communicate later.

That is much easier than standing on opposite sides and trying to describe a full room of symbols from scratch.

### Remove information instead of communicating more of it

This is one of the most useful lessons from the Purple set.

When communication becomes difficult, my first instinct is no longer “how can I describe this better?”

I ask:

**what can I eliminate before we separate?**

If I can reduce nine or ten visible options to only the few that matter, the actual communication problem becomes much smaller.

<h2 id="purple-flagpole-puzzle">Remove your Backpack for the Purple Flagpole puzzle</h2>

The Purple Flagpole challenge is mechanically straightforward, but player equipment can make it far more annoying.

The goal is to keep moving the object farther up the obstacle route until the gate opens.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/big-walk/big-walk-purple-flagpole-puzzle.webp"
    alt="Big Walk Purple Flagpole puzzle and obstacle route"
    width={1280}
    height={720}
    className="h-auto w-full rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    I remove my Backpack before pushing through the tighter Flagpole section because the extra collision makes getting stuck much more likely.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Before starting, I take off any **Backpack** I am wearing.

That sounds minor, but the extra bulk can catch on the obstacle geometry and turn a simple push into repeated resets.

My approach is:

1. remove the Backpack;
2. push / kick the object farther up the route;
3. stop whenever it begins drifting toward an edge;
4. reposition behind it;
5. continue until the gate opens;
6. finish the easier final portion after the gate.

I do not spam kicks from the side.

A slower straight push keeps the object moving toward the intended route instead of introducing sideways momentum that I then have to undo.

<h2 id="purple-puzzles-communication-strategy">The communication system I reuse across the Purple Puzzles</h2>

Three of the seven challenges became much easier once I stopped trying to describe objects naturally.

I use the same preparation pattern for the:

- light room;
- music room;
- nine-object matching room.

Before communication becomes restricted, I create a **shared index**.

That can mean:

- number positions left to right;
- arrange objects into slots 1 through 9;
- agree on which side is the starting side;
- use taps, flashes, or button presses to transmit a count.

Then the conversation becomes:

**“4”**

instead of:

**“the yellow-looking thing under the other curved thing on your left.”**

This is one of the few cases where taking an extra minute before starting genuinely saves repeated failed attempts.

<h2 id="purple-puzzles-stuck">What I check when a Purple Puzzle seems stuck</h2>

### The 30-minute puzzle still has not completed

I first check whether I actually started the timer.

If it is running, I leave it alone and continue another challenge rather than repeatedly interacting with the room.

### The heavy object keeps falling

I stop trying to push faster.

With a small group, I use one player ahead as a catcher / spotter and only move the object once the next section is clear.

### Our light calls keep producing the wrong button

I reset the numbering system with both players standing on the same side.

The usual problem is mirrored orientation, not bad counting.

### The music or matching calls suddenly stopped making sense

I check whether somebody moved the numbered objects.

If “3” no longer refers to the object that was originally in position 3, I rebuild the lineup before continuing.

### The Flagpole object keeps snagging on me

I remove the Backpack before trying again.

That is the first thing I change before blaming the obstacle itself.

<h2 id="after-seven-purple-puzzles">What I do after all seven Purple Puzzles are finished</h2>

I stop using the Purple Puzzle route once all seven challenges are complete.

I do **not** assume that seven Purple completions alone automatically means the entire game is at 100%.

Older normal puzzles can still matter to full completion.

At that point I return to the [Big Walk Ending Guide](/big-walk/ending-guide), check the overall completion state, and continue the Big Game route from there.

That keeps the ownership clean:

**this page solves the seven Purple Puzzles; the Ending Guide owns what those completions unlock.**

<h2 id="purple-puzzles-takeaway">Start the timer, simplify communication, and do not rush the physical puzzles</h2>

My Purple Puzzle cleanup became much faster once I stopped treating all seven challenges as ordinary standalone riddles.

I use three different strategies:

- **time management:** start the 30-minute timer first;
- **communication reduction:** number positions and objects before players are separated;
- **physical control:** move the heavy object and Flagpole object slowly instead of trying to recover from bad pushes.

The most demanding challenge in my small-group run was the Golf Course object route, which took roughly an hour with two players.

The most avoidable time loss came from communication puzzles where we had not agreed on a reference system beforehand.

And the easiest free optimization is still the first one: **press the 30-minute timer button before doing anything else.**
