The **Peg Puzzle in Big Walk** looks like a color-sorting puzzle at first, but that approach sent me in circles. The colored pieces are not a code you decode from the board itself. The board is asking you to **rebuild the landscape around the puzzle**.

Once I stopped staring at the empty holes and started looking at the aqueduct, trees, arches, bridge, and surrounding terrain, the puzzle made much more sense.

<h2 id="what-colored-pegs-mean">What the colored pegs actually mean</h2>

My first assumption was that the colors represented a sequence or a hidden numerical code. That was the wrong way to read the puzzle.

The clue is the environment.

Once I looked away from the board, I could see that the surrounding area has several obvious shapes:

- an aqueduct-like outer structure;
- groups of trees;
- arches;
- rocks;
- a bridge and raised sections;
- smaller terrain features inside the larger ring.

The breakthrough was realizing that the board is a simplified top-down reconstruction of those landmarks.

The clearest relationship for me was the **blue outer section**. The aqueduct wraps around the play area, so the blue pieces belong around the outside of the board rather than forming a random shape through the middle.

That observation gives the rest of the puzzle structure. The trees and other landmarks then fill the interior according to where they sit in the real area.

I do not try to memorize the exact board first. I walk a short loop around the site and build a mental map of what is outside, what is inside, and which landmarks sit next to each other.

<h2 id="collect-all-colored-pegs-first">Collect the loose colored pieces before solving the layout</h2>

The search phase is where I spent more time than the actual arrangement.

There are loose pieces scattered:

- around the main structure;
- near trees;
- around and above the arches;
- close to the bridge;
- on raised ledges;
- near rocks;
- around the lower edges of the area.

Some pieces require one player to help the other reach a higher position.

The mistake I would avoid is carrying several pieces away from the board while continuing to search. These objects can roll downhill or disappear into grass and rocks surprisingly easily.

My safer loop is:

1. find one or two nearby pieces;
2. bring them back to the pegboard;
3. place or park them there;
4. search the next section;
5. repeat until the area is exhausted.

That sounds slower, but it prevents the much worse situation where I know I found a piece earlier and then have no idea where I dropped it.

During our search, the remaining count dropped from roughly **10 missing pieces to six, then four, and finally one**. That last piece took disproportionately longer than the earlier batch, so I would expect the final search to be the real bottleneck rather than the board arrangement itself.

### Do not over-search the tunnel first

There is a small tunnel or bunker-like passage in the area.

I checked it when we were down to fewer pieces, but it was more useful as a shortcut than as the main hiding place. I would clear the open terrain, bridge, rocks, trees, and raised structures before repeatedly sweeping the tunnel.

### Search during daylight when only one piece remains

Early in the puzzle I am happy to grab obvious pieces at night with a light.

Once only one or two are missing, I stop wasting time in darkness.

The last piece is much easier to distinguish when daylight returns, especially around rocks and changes in elevation. If the group has already checked the same ground twice at night, I wait rather than doing a third identical lap.

<h2 id="how-to-solve-big-walk-pegboard">How I arrange the Peg Puzzle board</h2>

I do not recommend copying colors one by one without understanding the layout. The reliable method is to reconstruct the nearby terrain from the outside inward.

### Start with the blue outer shape

The strongest anchor is the aqueduct surrounding the area.

I place the **blue pieces around the outside** of the board to reproduce that larger boundary.

That gives me a frame. Once the frame makes sense, it becomes much easier to judge where the interior colors belong.

### Place tree-related pieces where the real trees sit

Next I stand near the board and look outward at the groups of trees.

I compare:

- left versus right;
- closer to the aqueduct versus closer to the center;
- whether a tree sits between two other landmarks;
- whether it lines up with a bridge, rock, or arch.

I then place the corresponding interior pieces in those relative positions.

I am not trying to reproduce real-world distance perfectly. The board compresses the scene. What matters is the **relationship between landmarks**.

### Use the arches as another fixed reference

The arches are easier to recognize than isolated rocks, so I use them as another anchor before filling ambiguous positions.

If the outer blue layout looks right but the center still feels wrong, I check the arches before I start moving every piece.

This keeps troubleshooting local instead of resetting the whole board.

<h2 id="last-missing-peg">Where to look for the last missing peg</h2>

The final missing piece was the most annoying part of my run.

By that stage, we had already searched the obvious structures and thought the board might simply be arranged incorrectly. It was not. We were still missing a physical piece.

I found it only after checking the **lower side of the rocks**, while another search also covered the upper side.

If I am stuck on one remaining piece, my search order is:

1. underside and backside of the rocks;
2. tops of the arches and raised structures;
3. bridge edges;
4. bases of trees;
5. outer side of the aqueduct;
6. only then the tunnel and already-cleared central ground.

Two players should split **high ground and low ground** rather than walking directly behind each other. That doubles the useful search area and reduces the chance that both players miss the same object.

<h2 id="peg-puzzle-not-working">Why the Peg Puzzle is not working</h2>

If the board does not validate, I check these problems in this order.

### You are still missing a loose piece

This is the first thing I suspect.

The last piece can be far enough from the board that the layout looks complete even when it is not. Search the rock edges, bridge, arches, trees, and raised ledges again before rebuilding everything.

### The outside boundary is wrong

The blue pieces gave me the clearest orientation.

If they are not recreating the outer aqueduct shape, the rest of the board can look almost believable while still being rotated or shifted.

I fix that boundary first.

### An interior landmark is mirrored

The board is easier to understand if I stand in one consistent viewing position.

Walking to the opposite side and then placing pieces from memory can reverse left and right. I return to the same side of the board before comparing the real trees and arches with the miniature layout.

### A loose piece rolled away after you found it

This happened often enough that I stopped trusting “we already picked that one up.”

If a piece is not physically at the board, I treat it as missing.

I retrace only the section where it was found instead of doing a full-map search.

<h2 id="big-walk-peg-puzzle-faq">Big Walk Peg Puzzle FAQ</h2>

### Do I need Walkie-Talkies for the Peg Puzzle?

Not really. Both players can stay close enough to communicate normally. I prioritize a light source instead, especially if the search runs into nighttime.

### Can I solve the Peg Puzzle at night?

Yes, but I only collect obvious pieces at night. When one or two pieces remain, daylight is much more efficient because the small colored objects can disappear against rocks and vegetation.

### Is the colored Peg Puzzle the same as the pose-and-telescope puzzle?

No. They are separate challenges. The colored Peg Puzzle is the landscape-reconstruction puzzle covered here. The pose and telescope challenge belongs with the other puzzle mechanics in the [Big Walk Puzzle Solutions guide](/big-walk/puzzles).

<h2 id="peg-puzzle-takeaway">Treat the pegboard like a map, not a color code</h2>

The Peg Puzzle became much easier once I stopped asking, “What order do these colors go in?” and started asking, “What part of the real landscape does this piece represent?”

My reliable route is:

1. inspect the surrounding landmarks;
2. collect every loose piece;
3. use the aqueduct to establish the blue outer boundary;
4. place the trees, arches, rocks, and other interior landmarks relative to that boundary;
5. wait for daylight if the final piece is hiding;
6. press the validation button only when the physical search is complete.

The arrangement is not the hardest part. **Finding every piece without losing one is.** Once the board has the full set and the surrounding landscape has been reconstructed correctly, the puzzle resolves cleanly.
