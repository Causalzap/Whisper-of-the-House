<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
A rocket standing at the **Launch Site** is not ready until its required propellants have been delivered and Mission Control can operate the pad. Early R7 rockets need **Kerosene and Liquid Oxygen**. Select the occupied launch site, choose an orbit the vehicle can reach, and review its **Expected Failure Rate** before authorizing launch. Afterward, judge the result by the mission objective: liftoff alone does not confirm a successful satellite deployment.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

A completed rocket sitting on its launch pad is not necessarily ready to fly. The pad may still need fuel, Mission Control must be able to operate, and the chosen mission has to match the rocket's capabilities.

Even after everything is ready, launching carries a risk. A rocket may explode almost immediately, fail during ascent, or reach space without completing the intended mission.

The most useful approach is to check each launch condition before pressing the button, then use the actual flight result to decide what needs changing.

## How Do You Prepare a Rocket Launch Site?

Start by choosing a location for the **Rocket Launch Site** that is separated from residential districts and important industrial facilities.

Failed launches can damage nearby buildings. One R7 launch ended with the rocket crashing into the launch facility and starting a fire. In another attempt, falling rocket debris landed near surrounding infrastructure.

Do not treat an empty patch of ground beside a busy city as an ideal launch location merely because the building fits.

### What does the Launch Site need?

The launch facility must be accessible to the infrastructure supporting your rocket program.

Plan for:

- Railway access from the Rocket Assembly Hall.
- Deliveries of the required rocket propellants.
- A working Mission Control Center.
- Enough space around the launch facility to reduce the consequences of a failed launch.

The rocket itself is assembled elsewhere. The Launch Site is where the finished vehicle is positioned, fueled, and launched.

If the completed rocket is still at the Assembly Hall, [check the rocket transport and assembly process](/workers-and-resources-space-program/rocket-assembly) before attempting to launch.

### Standard Rocket Launch Site vs Proton Launch Site

The game includes separate launch facilities for standard rockets and larger **Proton** rockets.

Check the rocket type supported by the intended launch facility before building an additional site.

An ordinary R7 launch does not require you to build the larger Proton facility first. Conversely, do not assume a larger rocket can use the same infrastructure as an early test vehicle.

For your first R7 mission, focus on completing one functioning standard launch setup before expanding into another rocket family.

## How Do You Fuel a Rocket With Kerosene and Liquid Oxygen?

For early R7 missions, the rocket needs **Kerosene** and **Liquid Oxygen** before launch.

These are separate resources. Supplying one does not replace the other, and a completed rocket cannot satisfy the fueling requirement merely by standing at the pad.

The Baikonur Campaign provides a useful example because its starting infrastructure includes prepared fuel supplies and delivery vehicles.

### Step 1: Check the rocket's fuel requirements

Select the occupied Launch Site and inspect the current fueling quantities.

One R7 test flight required approximately:

- **85 tons of Kerosene**
- **169 tons of Liquid Oxygen**

Those quantities were shown for that particular rocket. A later crewed vehicle required different amounts, so do not treat them as a fixed recipe for every R7 launch.

Always use the requirements displayed for the rocket currently on your pad.

### Step 2: Configure the fuel deliveries

In the Baikonur Campaign, a nearby Distribution Office already has access to the required fuels and tanker vehicles.

The remaining task is to set the Launch Site as a destination and configure the deliveries correctly.

A working setup follows this process:

1. Open the Distribution Office responsible for rocket fueling.
2. Check which available source contains Kerosene and which contains Liquid Oxygen.
3. Confirm that the assigned vehicles can carry their respective cargo.
4. Add the Launch Site as the destination.
5. Configure the loading and unloading tasks for the appropriate resources.
6. Start the deliveries and watch the quantities at the Launch Site.

The initial setup may already contain correctly configured vehicles. Check what is present before purchasing more.

In one launch preparation, the delivery vehicles remained idle until the Distribution Office's unloading orders were corrected. Once both resources were assigned to the destination, the trucks began moving.

### Step 3: Wait for both fuel quantities to be satisfied

Large rockets may require several tanker deliveries.

A delivery truck arriving at the pad does not prove that fueling is complete. Check the remaining quantities in the Launch Site interface.

If the pad still needs Kerosene or Liquid Oxygen, allow the remaining deliveries to finish before requesting launch.

### Why should Liquid Oxygen be delivered shortly before launch?

**Liquid Oxygen cannot remain available on the rocket indefinitely.**

The Baikonur fueling instructions specifically warn against delaying the launch unnecessarily once fueling begins.

This makes timing important.

Complete rocket assembly, transport, Mission Control preparation, and your intended mission selection before relying on the final Liquid Oxygen delivery.

If you leave a fueled rocket waiting for too long, check its remaining propellant rather than assuming it still has everything originally delivered.

### What if you are playing in a normal republic?

In a sandbox republic, you may need to produce or import the required propellants before arranging deliveries to the Launch Site.

Unlike the Baikonur Campaign, your save may not have prepared fuel storage or tanker vehicles. Check the rocket's required fuel types and quantities, then confirm that the correct resources can reach the launch facility.

Advanced rockets may require different propellants, so inspect each vehicle's requirements before configuring deliveries.

## How Do You Use the Mission Control Center?

**Mission Control is where you choose the launch site, set the mission parameters, and authorize the launch.**

Build a Mission Control Center and make sure it is staffed. Its interface displays the current **Dispatchers** count, so check the workers actually present instead of assuming the building is operating simply because construction has finished.

If staffing is low, check whether workers can reach the center through your road, walking, or passenger transport network.

### Step 1: Open Mission Control

Select the Mission Control Center after your rocket has arrived at its Launch Site.

Find the launch controls and choose the occupied launch site.

If you have more than one launch pad, pay attention to which facility is selected. Different pads may contain different rocket types or have different preparation states.

### Step 2: Choose the mission and orbit

The available mission options depend on the rocket and its capabilities.

The launch interface can display orbital choices such as:

- **Circular Orbit**
- **Elliptical Orbit**
- **Geosynchronous Orbit**

You can also encounter controls for the intended orbital inclination or orientation.

Not every rocket can perform every orbital mission. In particular, do not assume an early rocket can reach a demanding high-altitude target just because the option appears elsewhere in the system.

### Which orbit should you choose?

Choose the orbit required by your current objective rather than selecting the most ambitious option.

**Circular Orbit** is a straightforward target when you want the spacecraft to follow a relatively consistent orbital path.

**Elliptical Orbit** gives the spacecraft a path with changing distance from Earth.

**Geosynchronous Orbit** is a substantially different, higher-altitude target and may be beyond the capabilities of the selected rocket.

The practical decision is whether the current vehicle supports the intended target. If the game indicates that it cannot, change the mission or prepare a suitable rocket.

For the official Baikonur sequence, follow the requested mission objective rather than inventing a different orbit requirement.

### Step 3: Check the displayed failure risk

Before launching, inspect the rocket's **Expected Failure Rate**.

This is the final displayed risk for the assembled vehicle. It is distinct from an individual booster's test result.

If the risk is higher than you are willing to accept, consider preparing another rocket with tested components instead of assuming that repeated launches will eventually be inexpensive.

The [Rocket Assembly guide](/workers-and-resources-space-program/rocket-assembly) explains how compatible rocket components are tested before assembly.

### Step 4: Authorize the launch

When the launch pad is fueled and ready, select its launch command through Mission Control.

The countdown begins and the camera follows the rocket through the flight.

At this point, the result can still be a success or failure. Completing the preparations does not guarantee that the launch will succeed.

## What Does Expected Failure Rate Mean?

**Expected Failure Rate is the risk displayed for the complete rocket before launch. It does not tell you that a particular launch will definitely fail.**

  <Image
    src="/images/workers-and-resources-space-program/space-program-rocket-expected-failure-rate.webp"
    alt="Complete R7 rocket on the Launch Site displaying an Expected Failure Rate of 24.0 percent"
    width={1280}
    height={780}
    className="h-auto w-full rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The complete rocket displays a 24.0% Expected Failure Rate before launch. A lower risk does not guarantee success.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Several launches demonstrate why this matters:

- A rocket with approximately **98–99% expected failure** was launched without the intended improvements and failed.
- A rocket with **24% expected failure** exploded almost immediately during a later attempt.
- A rocket with **19% expected failure** successfully carried a satellite into a stable orbit.
- A Baikonur Sputnik launch succeeded despite displaying **42% expected failure** before launch.

These results do not establish a guaranteed success threshold.

A 24% displayed risk is not a promise that the rocket will survive. Similarly, a successful launch at 42% does not mean that level of risk is safe or recommended.

### How do you reduce the risk before launching?

If the displayed failure risk is too high, consider testing compatible engines and other components before assembling a replacement rocket. Follow the [Rocket Engine Testing instructions](/workers-and-resources-space-program/rocket-assembly#test-rocket-engines) to check individual parts before assembly.

Do not calculate the complete rocket's failure rate by simply adding or averaging individual component percentages. Use the Expected Failure Rate displayed for the assembled vehicle.

### Should you launch with a high failure rate?

That depends on what you are risking.

A cheap experimental flight may be worth attempting even when the chance of failure is substantial.

A more expensive rocket carrying a satellite or crewed spacecraft represents a larger investment of manufacturing time, industrial resources, transport capacity, and fuel.

The displayed probability should inform the decision, not replace it.

If another failed launch would leave your republic short of money, materials, or production capacity, improving reliability before trying again is usually the more useful investment.

## How Can You Tell Whether a Rocket Launch Succeeded?

There are several different results to distinguish.

A rocket leaving the pad, a rocket reaching space, and a satellite entering a stable orbit are not necessarily the same achievement.

### Successful test flight

An R7 Testing Rocket can perform a test flight without leaving a permanent satellite in orbit.

During one test flight, the rocket reached space, separated its stages, and released an object that later re-entered the atmosphere and burned up.

That sequence alone does not confirm whether the mission succeeded or failed. Check the actual objective result. A test flight and a satellite deployment have different completion requirements.

### Successful satellite launch

A satellite mission has a more specific goal.

During a successful Sputnik flight, the rocket ascended, separated its stages, deployed its payload, and the game confirmed that the satellite had entered Earth orbit.

Watch for these milestones:

1. Liftoff from the launch pad.
2. Successful ascent.
3. Stage separation.
4. Payload or fairing deployment where applicable.
5. Orbital insertion and the mission completion result.

The fifth step is the important confirmation for a satellite mission.

In another successful launch, Mission Control showed the spacecraft in a **stable orbit** after the payload separated. That is stronger evidence of orbital success than simply watching the rocket climb above the atmosphere.

### How do you find a spacecraft after launch?

Return to the Mission Control Center and inspect the objects or spacecraft currently listed in orbit.

This is useful when the camera has returned to your republic but you still need to confirm the spacecraft's state.

An object that reached space briefly is not necessarily still in a stable orbit.

For the official Sputnik, Vostok, and Soyuz mission order, follow the [Baikonur Campaign walkthrough](/workers-and-resources-space-program/baikonur-campaign).

## Why Did Your Rocket Launch Fail?

A failed launch is not always caused by the same problem.

The rocket might explode on the pad, ascend before failing, crash near the launch area, or complete part of its flight without meeting the objective.

Start with the actual result instead of treating all failures as missing fuel or a broken launch button.

### Failure 1: The rocket explodes immediately

An assembled rocket can fail just after launch authorization.

One attempt with **24% expected failure** exploded almost as soon as the launch began.

The rocket had been positioned and the launch command had worked. The problem was not that the player failed to press the button correctly.

For this type of failure:

1. Check the final failure rate recorded for that rocket.
2. Inspect the Launch Site for damage or fire.
3. Restore any damaged infrastructure.
4. Prepare another rocket.
5. Consider testing its components before the next attempt.

Repeatedly selecting the same orbit does not repair a destroyed rocket.

### Failure 2: The rocket climbs and then crashes

Other failures occur after a rocket has already left the pad.

A rocket may begin its ascent, lose control later in flight, and crash back toward the republic.

During one early launch, debris landed on the launch facility and started a fire. Another attempt failed during flight but left the pad itself intact.

This is why launch-site location matters even after the building has been successfully constructed.

Inspect the area for damaged buildings, fires, and interrupted infrastructure before preparing the next launch.

### Failure 3: Guidance Failure

A rocket can survive the initial ascent and still fail to complete the planned mission.

A **Guidance Failure** was recorded in an early launch series. The vehicle did not accomplish its intended flight, even though the result differed from an immediate explosion on the pad.

Treat the displayed failure result as an unsuccessful mission. Check the rocket's recorded risk and prepare a replacement rather than assuming that reaching the upper atmosphere guarantees success.

Do not confuse a guidance failure during flight with a transport-route problem before the rocket reaches the launch site. They occur at different stages and require different checks.

### Failure 4: The rocket cannot reach the selected orbit

A rocket's performance limits can prevent it from reaching the chosen target.

If the interface reports:

**"This rocket cannot reach this orbit."**

Check the current vehicle and the selected orbit.

Try a target supported by that rocket or prepare a vehicle capable of the required mission.

Do not interpret the warning as a random launch failure. It is a target compatibility problem that should be addressed before attempting the mission.

### Failure 5: The rocket reaches space but the objective is not completed

Reaching space does not guarantee that a satellite mission has succeeded. A rocket may complete part of its ascent without placing its payload into the required orbit.

Check whether stage separation and payload deployment occurred, then inspect Mission Control for the spacecraft's final orbital status. An experimental test flight does not need to leave a permanent satellite in orbit, but a Sputnik mission does.

Crewed campaign missions have additional return requirements. If you have already reached orbit with Vostok or Soyuz, follow the [remaining Baikonur mission steps](/workers-and-resources-space-program/baikonur-campaign) rather than treating the unfinished return as a launch failure.

## What Should You Do After Your First Successful Launch?

After a successful test flight, record the rocket's fuel requirements and final failure risk before preparing the next vehicle. A satellite mission adds a payload and may require different components, so inspect the new Assembly Hall blueprint rather than reusing the experimental rocket unchanged.

For the official sequence, [continue with Sputnik and the later Baikonur missions](/workers-and-resources-space-program/baikonur-campaign). If you are developing your own republic, [choose the next research and infrastructure goal](/workers-and-resources-space-program) based on the spacecraft you intend to launch.
