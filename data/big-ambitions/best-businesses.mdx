<h2 id="best-business-now">
  What is the best business to open in Big Ambitions?
</h2>

There is no business I would call the permanent best choice across an entire
save.

Market Insider changes that answer too often.

During one Garment District check, **Bag of Carrots was at 88% demand, Bottle
of Wine at 85%, and Burger at 84%**. In another market state, several cheap
Clothing products were clustered around **79–80%** instead.

Those numbers describe opportunities in that district at that moment. They are
not a tier list.

The useful comparison has four parts:

- what the market currently wants;
- what it costs to get the doors open;
- how complicated the business is to staff and supply;
- whether the available building fits the equipment and customer throughput.

That is why a Gym can be my favorite first business while Electronics can become
far more interesting later. They are solving different problems at different
stages of the same company.

<h2 id="gym-first-business">
  Is a Gym a good first business?
</h2>

A Gym is one of the easiest openings for me when I want to keep inventory out of
the way while learning the rest of the game.

The basic setup needs equipment such as a **Workout Machine, Gym Locker, Public
Shower, Fitness Planning Board and Drinks Fridge**.

Most of that spending happens once.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/big-ambitions/big-ambitions-gym-first-business-requirements.webp"
    alt="Gym requirements showing workout equipment locker shower planning board and drinks fridge in Big Ambitions"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    A Gym puts more of the first investment into reusable equipment and less
    into a broad merchandise catalog, which keeps the opening easier to manage.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

One early 1.0 setup used roughly **$8,000** during the main equipment shopping
run. I treat that as a real example rather than a universal startup price:
property choice, equipment and layout can all move the final amount.

The more revealing number came after opening.

That Gym sat in a property limited to **15 customers per hour**, but the first
combination of locker and shower capacity only supported roughly **10 per
hour**. After adding enough facilities to bring the business toward the
building's 15-customer limit, another round of equipment no longer made sense.

At that point the building was the bottleneck.

This is where a small Gym can actually save money. I only need enough equipment
to reach the throughput the property can use. A larger machine count does not
turn a 15-customer building into a 30-customer one.

The supply side is also forgiving. Drinks still need stock, but the store does
not collapse merely because I forgot twelve separate food or retail products.
That makes a Gym especially comfortable while I am still learning scheduling,
pricing and expansion.

The tradeoff is ceiling.

If local Gym demand is poor or the available properties are bad, its operational
simplicity is not enough to rescue the business. And once I can afford a more
staff-intensive or higher-value opportunity, the reason I chose the Gym in the
first place matters less.

<h2 id="hairdresser">
  Hairdresser: low inventory, heavier staffing
</h2>

A Hairdresser moves the difficulty away from merchandise and into the schedule.

Most of the revenue comes through services such as cutting, coloring, washing
and styling rather than a large shelf of products. The retail side is relatively
small; **Hair Care Product** is the main item I need to keep stocked alongside
the bags used for sales.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image

    alt="Hairdresser service stations and Hair Care Product in Big Ambitions"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The Hairdresser avoids the wide inventory burden of retail, but every useful
    service station still needs employee coverage.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

That distinction matters once I start adding chairs and wash stations.

An empty chair is not productive capacity. It is furniture I paid for until an
employee is available to operate it.

So I size the first Hairdresser around the workforce I can actually cover for a
full week. If customer demand later starts pushing against that capacity, I can
add stations and staff together.

Demand can vary dramatically between districts. I have seen Hairdresser demand
around **86%** in a favorable market and only somewhere in the **50s** in a
weaker one.

That gap is large enough to change the whole decision.

A Hairdresser at 86% demand with a workable property can be much more appealing
than a Gym simply because the market is giving me a stronger service
opportunity. The same business in a weak district may not be worth the payroll.

<h2 id="fast-food">
  When does Fast Food become worth opening?
</h2>

Fast Food becomes much easier to judge once I stop looking only at product
demand and start looking at the equipment behind each product.

The machines have their own customer-capacity breakpoints.

A production line around a Grill can sit around **20 customer capacity** for
products such as Burgers and Chicken Kebab Skewers. Other menu lines can use
equipment closer to **30**, while some categories remain around 20.

  <Image

    alt="Fast Food equipment showing customer capacity values in Big Ambitions"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The equipment is part of the property decision. A line capped at 20
    customers does not fully use a 30-customer building unless I add more
    capacity.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

That creates real purchasing tradeoffs.

Put a 20-capacity line into a **30-customer** property and a second machine takes
the business to 40, leaving part of the equipment capacity unused.

Put a 30-capacity machine into a **15-customer** property and half of its
potential is already unnecessary.

Neither case automatically makes the restaurant bad. It changes the building I
am willing to rent.

Menu width is the second cost.

Fast Food can support many products, and every extra category can mean another
machine, another stock item and another thing that can run out. I get better
results opening around a sensible core menu than trying to install every
possible station before the first customer arrives.

There is also an opportunity cost to using a premium property.

If I need one of the city's better high-capacity retail spaces simply to make a
modest Fast Food setup feel efficient, I compare that against what the same
building might support later.

A smaller restaurant with slightly imperfect machine utilization can still be
the better business decision.

<h2 id="supermarket">
  Why I delay a Supermarket when cash is still tight
</h2>

A Supermarket can look excellent in Market Insider because so many individual
products can have strong demand at the same time.

The startup bill is hidden in the number of categories I decide to support.

Each category needs the right fixture, enough customer capacity and enough stock
to make that fixture useful.

The Drinks Fridge shows the problem clearly.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/big-ambitions/big-ambitions-drinks-fridge-customer-capacity.webp"
    alt="Standard and large Drinks Fridges showing price and customer capacity in Big Ambitions"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The standard Drinks Fridge costs $875 and supports 20 customers, while the
    Large version costs $2,200 and supports 50. The larger fixture only helps
    when the property and demand can use that extra capacity.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

That is a concrete example of why I calculate the store from the inside out.

A standard Drinks Fridge gives me **20 customer capacity for $875**.

The Large version jumps to **50 for $2,200**.

If the property itself only handles 15 or 20 customers, paying for 50 solves
nothing. If I am planning a genuinely high-volume Supermarket, buying around the
smaller fixture can create its own bottleneck.

Other product categories add different breakpoints. Fresh Food and freezer
equipment can land around the 20–30 range, while produce fixtures can support
larger chunks such as **40**.

That gets awkward in something like a **75-customer** property.

Several pieces of equipment may overshoot the building limit in different ways,
so trying to support every possible product from day one can turn the first
shopping trip into a wall of refrigerators and displays.

I have had better results opening with a narrower product mix.

In one large-store setup, I left fruit out initially because the produce
capacity did not fit the rest of the build cleanly. That removed several
products from the first inventory order and let the store open sooner.

Nothing prevented me from adding them after the business was earning.

The point where a Supermarket becomes much more attractive is when inventory is
already an automated problem. A broad catalog is far easier to exploit once I
am no longer personally responsible for every restock.

<h2 id="high-value-businesses">
  When Clothing, Electronics and Jewelry start making sense
</h2>

These are the businesses most likely to make me confuse strong demand with a
business I should open immediately.

Clothing is a good example.

I have seen its products sit near the top of current market demand while the
business selector still showed later progression requirements involving
**Headquarters and Factory**.

That is not a bad opportunity.

It is an opportunity for a later company.

Electronics follows the same broader pattern. Some products can be sourced
through Headquarters importers while others sit on the manufacturing side of
the business. Jewelry also belongs much closer to a company with substantial
capital and an established supply network than to the first-business stage.

Once Headquarters, warehousing and manufacturing are already part of normal
operations, the calculation changes.

Now I can use high-value retail as a way to scale systems the company already
owns. Better properties become easier to justify because higher-value products
and a larger supply network can actually use them.

I still start from retail demand rather than building a Factory first and then
looking for something to sell.

<h2 id="business-and-location">
  How I match a business to the right building
</h2>

Business choice and real estate are one decision for me.

A great business in an awkward property can waste more money than a slightly
weaker business in a building that fits.

The two numbers I read together are **Traffic Index** and **customer capacity**.

I have compared properties around the **40s in Traffic Index** with capacities
ranging from **15 to 30 and 75 customers**. A larger capacity is useful only
when the business inside has enough demand, staffing and equipment to serve it.

For a Gym, that can mean avoiding rent for 30 or 75 customers when the current
equipment plan only needs 15.

For Fast Food, the machine breakpoints matter.

For a Supermarket, fixture and stock costs grow with the property.

For a Hairdresser, every extra service station also implies another staffing
decision.

Then I look at local competition.

A demand percentage is easier to exploit when the neighborhood is not already
crowded with providers. If a business has several established competitors or a
Special Rival is concentrated in that market, I need a stronger reason to enter
than the same demand number would require somewhere cleaner.

I do not use this page to plan the rivalry itself. When competition changes from
normal market pressure into a deliberate fight, that belongs in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/rivals-guide">
  {" "}Rivals guide
</Link>

<h2 id="best-business-by-stage">
  The businesses I favor at each stage
</h2>

My choices tend to change in a predictable direction even though the exact
business still depends on the save.

**Early game:** I prefer something operationally simple. A Gym is attractive
when I want low inventory pressure. A Hairdresser works when service demand is
strong and I am comfortable paying for more staff.

**Early expansion:** Fast Food becomes more interesting when I can afford the
equipment without gutting working capital. The property needs to fit the
machine capacities rather than merely satisfy the objective.

**Established supply chain:** Supermarkets become much less annoying once a
wide catalog no longer means a wide list of personal errands.

**Headquarters and manufacturing stage:** Clothing, Electronics and Jewelry can
finally be evaluated on their real upside because their supporting systems are
already part of the company.

That sequence is not a fixed progression requirement.

If Market Insider gives me an exceptional opportunity earlier, I still evaluate
it. What changes by stage is how many hidden costs I can absorb without turning
the new business into the thing that breaks everything else.

<h2 id="when-to-pass">
  When I walk away from an otherwise good business
</h2>

The expensive mistakes usually begin with a number I like too much.

An 80% demand product catches my attention, but the business needs a system I
have not unlocked.

A Fast Food property looks ideal until the machinery fits the customer limit
poorly.

A huge Supermarket location is available, but the fixtures and opening inventory
would consume the cash I need for payroll and supply.

A large Gym property looks impressive, yet the smaller one already handles the
throughput my equipment can provide.

Those are easy businesses to revisit later.

I would rather leave a good opportunity on the map than open it in a form that
needs three additional problems solved before it becomes profitable.

Before I sign the lease, I want the demand, property, staffing and supply chain
to point in the same direction.

When they do, the business usually feels straightforward.

When they do not, the Market Insider screen will still be there after the
company is better prepared.
