<h2 id="what-to-do-next">
  What to do next in Big Ambitions 1.0
</h2>

The campaign gives me objectives, but those objectives do not always tell me
whether my company is financially ready for the thing they just unlocked.

That distinction becomes important almost immediately.

A new business can appear before I have enough cash to staff it comfortably.
Headquarters can become available while another profitable store would help me
more. Product Manufacturing can finish long before my retail network is large
enough to justify a Factory.

So I use the objectives as the route through the game's systems and my existing
businesses as the test for when I actually invest in them.

The early game is mostly about proving that I can make one store work. The next
stage tests whether I can repeat it. Headquarters starts removing administrative
work, the warehouse removes routine stock runs, and manufacturing eventually
moves part of the supply chain inside my own company.

By late game, progression stops being about finding the next feature. Most of
the features are already there. The decision becomes where I can repeat a
profitable system without creating a new staffing, supply, or competition
problem.

<h2 id="first-business">
  First business: open something the current company can support
</h2>

The normal opening takes me through Uncle Fred's objectives and eventually to
the bank for a **$15,000 loan**, followed by an **A1 or A2 retail building**.

The important 1.0 change is that I have more freedom over the business itself.
I am not building around the old assumption that every fresh save must follow
one fixed Gift Shop into Fast Food sequence.

For this first store, I care more about getting the entire loop working than
squeezing out the theoretically highest profit.

I need the required equipment, enough employee coverage to keep the doors open,
a product supply I can maintain, and enough money left after setup that one bad
day does not put the loan payment or payroll under pressure.

should be affordable enough to survive while I learn everything surrounding it.

<h2 id="stabilize-first-business">
  When is the first store ready for expansion?
</h2>

I usually give it at least enough time to reveal the problems that were invisible
on opening day.

A business is ready to leave alone when its required stations have real
coverage, essential stock is not repeatedly reaching zero, cleaning is handled,
and customers are willing to pay the current prices.

None of those numbers has to be perfect.

An Interior Score below 100 is not automatically a reason to delay the second
business. Neither is an employee who could still use more training. If the store
opens every day, serves the customers who arrive, keeps its essential products
available and leaves cash behind, I would rather use the next chunk of capital
where it creates more earning power.

The schedule deserves one last look before I move on.

A store can appear fully staffed while I am standing there and still have gaps
later in the week. Full-time and part-time employees, days off, sick calls and
role coverage matter more than the number of names in the employee list.

When schedules themselves start becoming the work, I move that problem into my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/employees-hr-guide">
  {" "}Employees & HR guide
</Link>
 rather than turning this page into a second staffing manual.

<h2 id="second-business">
  The second-business objective is a filter, not a shopping list
</h2>

The next Business School stretch gives me much more useful numbers.

I am pushed toward a retail location with at least **40% Traffic Index** and a
business whose primary product has at least **80% demand**.

Those thresholds narrow the search. They do not guarantee that the first
matching result is affordable or even available to my company.

I usually check the sequence in this order:

**current demand → business availability → suitable property → full startup
cost.**

That prevents an easy progression mistake: finding a great demand number,
renting around it, and only afterward discovering that the business depends on
a supply system I have not reached yet.

It also stops me from overspending on the building.

A larger property only helps when I can use the extra customer capacity. Early
on, the difference in rent and equipment can matter more than capacity that
will sit unused for the next several weeks.

The second business is where I want proof that the first store was not a lucky
one-off. If I can establish another profitable location without the original
store collapsing while I am away, the company is ready to become more than a
single-business operation.

<h2 id="installation-firm">
  When retail interior installation starts saving more than it costs
</h2>

The installation firm changes the value of my time, but I rarely use it as soon
as it appears.

Building the first few stores manually teaches me which counters, stations,
shelves and equipment are actually required. That knowledge is useful later,
because it makes an expensive premade setup much easier to evaluate.

The convenience becomes more attractive after I have several profitable
businesses.

At that point, spending another day driving between furniture stores may cost me
more in lost expansion time than paying somebody else to finish the interior.
The game itself introduces installation as an expensive option, so smaller
properties remain perfectly sensible while cash is still tight.

I tend to pay for installation when I already understand the business layout and
want another copy running quickly.

If paying the installer would leave payroll or inventory uncomfortable, I am
still too early.

<h2 id="when-to-open-hq">
  When should you open Headquarters?
</h2>

Headquarters becomes useful when the company has enough repetitive work to give
the office a real job.

That can happen through several different routes.

Maybe I am repeatedly recruiting and rebuilding schedules. Maybe product
sourcing is becoming awkward. Maybe I have enough locations that checking prices
one store at a time is starting to eat the day.

An empty Headquarters fixes none of that by itself.

This is also a stage where I watch cash more carefully in 1.0. Loan payments
leave less room for casually stacking office rent, equipment and management
salaries onto a company that still has weak operating income.

Calendar timing can matter as well.

If I reach the Headquarters objective late on Friday, I am comfortable preparing
the office and letting the existing stores earn through the weekend rather than
draining cash merely so I can say the office exists. Management roles still need
scheduled employees before the system starts doing useful work.

<h2 id="warehouse-stage">
  When should you rent the first warehouse?
</h2>

The first warehouse earns its place when product movement is taking time away
from growth.

One small store can tolerate manual restocking. Even two simple businesses may
be manageable if their product lists are short.

The calculation changes when I keep revisiting the same supply problem: another
order, another pickup, another store running low, another drive that has to
happen before I can work on the next location.

That is when warehouse progression starts solving something real.

I start with the warehouse the current company needs.

Product variety is usually the bigger warning than raw floor space. A new retail
business may add another importer, new stock targets and another delivery
destination even when the physical boxes themselves would easily fit.

If the warehouse stage is reached but stores are still not receiving goods
reliably, I stop progression there and repair the chain before adding another
inventory-heavy business.

<h2 id="management-automation">
  The middle game gets easier when repeated decisions stop coming back to me
</h2>

After Headquarters and the first warehouse are working, progression becomes less
visible.

There is no single dramatic purchase that suddenly makes five businesses easy
to operate. Instead, small recurring jobs gradually disappear.

An employee calls in sick and the week no longer needs to be rebuilt by hand.
A replacement candidate can arrive without another recruitment request. Store
prices can be reviewed centrally. Routine stock is purchased and moved without
a personal trip across Manhattan.

This is the stage where another business becomes much cheaper in terms of my
attention.

I still watch the outputs.

Automation that keeps ordering unwanted stock, schedules the wrong workforce or
protects an unprofitable price is not useful simply because it runs without me.
The goal is to remove repetition while keeping the important decisions visible.

Once that works, the company is finally ready for systems whose payoff depends
on scale.

<h2 id="product-manufacturing">
  Product Manufacturing comes before the Factory is actually worth building
</h2>

Business School eventually introduces **Product Manufacturing**.

I complete the course when progression reaches it, even if I have no intention
of renting a Factory that same day. Unlocking the manufacturing layer early
means it is ready when the retail side finally creates a reason to use it.

A working manufacturing chain sits on top of things I have already built:
Headquarters staff, raw-material purchasing, employees, storage, transportation,
and retail demand.

If one of those layers is still unreliable, adding machinery makes the weak
part more expensive.

The first Factory becomes attractive when I can point to products that several
existing or planned stores are ready to consume. Manufacturing then replaces
part of a real purchasing requirement instead of creating inventory simply
because the machinery can run.

<h2 id="late-game-expansion">
  Late-game progression is about where the proven business system goes next
</h2>

By this stage I have usually stopped asking what the game unlocks next.

The useful questions are geographic and operational.

Which neighborhood still has demand?

Does the available property fit the customer capacity of the business?

How crowded is the market?

Can the current warehouse network take another destination?

Will the new location need another management plan or additional staff capacity?

And is a Special Rival already making the area more expensive to enter?

This is where Market Insider becomes more valuable than a linear objective list.
A company that works in Garment District does not automatically belong in
Midtown at the same price, with the same products and the same building size.

Rivals also become a deliberate strategic choice rather than an early warning I
have to answer immediately.

If a cleaner district lets me add profitable scale with less friction, I am
happy to take that expansion first. Direct pressure on a Special Rival makes
more sense after the rest of the company can continue earning while I devote
money and attention to the fight.

The actual competition, takeover and Rival-owned property decisions are covered
in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/rivals-guide">
  {" "}Rivals guide
</Link>

<h2 id="stalled-progression">
  If progression feels stalled, I look for the part of the company that still needs babysitting
</h2>

The useful signal is rarely the largest number on the screen.

I have had stores where another decoration purchase would improve the Interior
Score but the real problem was an uncovered station. I have reached warehouse
progression while still creating stock problems through bad routes. I have
finished Product Manufacturing with no retail network large enough to justify
more output.

Those situations all feel different, but they point to the same diagnosis:
something in the existing company still needs too much intervention.

When that happens, I spend a normal business day watching where my time goes.

If I keep returning to the employee schedule, staffing is not finished.

If I am chasing stock, supply is not finished.

If customers arrive but sales are weak, I check the business itself before
expanding the infrastructure behind it.

If warehouses and stores are already holding more stock than they sell, more
production is premature.

Once those interruptions disappear, the next stage usually becomes obvious
without needing to force it.
