<h2 id="when-factory-makes-sense">
  When does a Factory actually make sense?
</h2>

Finishing the **Product Manufacturing** course gives me access to the system.
It does not automatically give me a reason to spend heavily on it.

The reason comes from the stores.

If several locations are already selling products that I can manufacture, the
Factory has an immediate job. If the company still has one small store and a
warehouse route I am constantly repairing, manufacturing mainly adds another
layer of employees, raw materials, machinery and inventory to something that
was already unstable.

So before I commit, I want three things to be true.

My ordinary stock network works without frequent rescue trips.

I know which finished product has somewhere to go.

And the company has enough cash that buying machinery does not make payroll,
raw materials or normal retail expansion uncomfortable.

The warehouse side needs to be boring first. Purchasing contracts, Smart
Delivery, store minimums and Logistics Manager routes stay in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/warehouse-logistics-guide">
  {" "}Warehouse & Logistics guide
</Link>
.

Once those systems can move goods without me, manufacturing becomes much easier
to judge on its own merits.

<h2 id="choose-factory-building">
  Which Factory building should you rent?
</h2>

The exterior tells me surprisingly little.

What I care about is the usable floor area, how open that space is, and whether
the loading side leaves enough room for storage and machinery without turning
movement around the building into a nuisance.

The example below makes that difference obvious.

A first production site only needs enough room for the product lines I can
actually keep busy plus some sensible expansion space. Paying for a huge floor
while half the machines sit idle is just another form of unused capacity.

The larger properties become much more attractive after I already know that one
Factory will be supplying several stores or several production lines.

<h2 id="factory-layout">
  Factory layouts are much cleaner after the production-system rework
</h2>

The modern Factory floor is far easier to organize than the older
conveyor-heavy version of the system.

I still plan around the relationships between machines and production, but the
floor is no longer dominated by building an elaborate belt maze between every
piece of equipment. That gives me more freedom to group related machinery and
leave the center of the building usable.

Preset or installed interiors can still save setup time, especially once money

Early on, I prefer knowing exactly what I am paying for.

If a layout gives me machinery for products I have no intention of making, that
equipment consumes both cash and floor area. Removing the irrelevant pieces and
building around the product I actually sell usually gives me a clearer first
Factory.

<h2 id="choose-products">
  Start with the product, then build the Factory backward
</h2>

My production plan begins in the retail network.

I look at what the stores are already selling, which products are expensive or
awkward to source, and how many locations could use the same Factory output.

Only then do I decide what machinery and raw materials belong inside the
building.

Electronics makes the relationship easy to see.

That split is useful because a Factory does not have to replace every imported
item on day one.

I can start with the manufactured products that have a clear retail destination,
let that chain settle, and widen production after I know how quickly the stores
consume it.

A narrow first product list also makes problems much easier to diagnose.

If five unrelated product families all stop at once, finding the missing raw
material becomes annoying. With one or two focused lines, I can usually tell
what went wrong immediately.

<h2 id="raw-materials">
  Raw-material shortages can make a good Factory look underbuilt
</h2>

A slow production line is not automatically a machine-capacity problem.

The first place I look is the input side.

One missing material can stop the product even while the Factory appears full
of everything else. That becomes especially easy to miss once several products
share part of the same raw-material pool.

For the first production cycle, I prefer a reasonable buffer rather than several
weeks of ingredients.

Too little creates emergency purchasing and idle workers.

Too much locks cash into materials that may sit untouched because the downstream
stores are selling more slowly than I expected.

The useful buffer is the one that lasts comfortably until the next normal supply
cycle.

After I have watched the line run for a while, I increase targets using actual
consumption rather than guessing from the size of the Factory.

<h2 id="factory-storage">
  Where I keep raw materials and finished goods
</h2>

I find the Factory easier to read when the production floor has a clear
upstream and downstream side.

Raw materials need enough nearby storage that the machines can keep running.

Finished goods need somewhere to leave.

In current 1.0, **Storage Shelves can be used in factories as well as
warehouses**, so the building has more flexibility than a setup that forces
everything into one exact storage arrangement.

For a small operation, holding some finished inventory inside the Factory is
perfectly workable.

Once several retail locations depend on the output, I prefer the finished goods
to move into the wider warehouse network. That keeps retail distribution
separate from manufacturing and gives me a cleaner signal when something goes
wrong.

If raw materials are empty, the problem is upstream.

If finished goods are stacking up, the problem is downstream.

That distinction saves a lot of unnecessary machine purchases.

<h2 id="factory-workers">
  How much does Factory Worker skill matter?
</h2>

Worker quality matters enough that I check it before copying another production
line.

A weak workforce can make the same machinery look slower than it really is.

The post-rework Factory system gives Factory Worker skill a meaningful effect on
production performance, so comparing two layouts only by machine count misses
part of the picture.

If I already have the required materials and the line still feels
underproductive, I inspect the workers next.

Training and workforce automation themselves belong in the
<Link href="/big-ambitions/employees-hr-guide">
  {" "}Employees & HR guide
</Link>
. From the Factory side, the important point is that another expensive machine
is not always the cheapest way to increase useful output.

There is also a supply consequence.

More productive workers and more machines both consume materials faster. If the
raw-material flow was only barely keeping up before the upgrade, improving
production can expose that weakness immediately.

<h2 id="add-more-machines">
  When should you add another production line?
</h2>

I watch finished-goods movement rather than empty floor space.

If the warehouse keeps draining the product and the stores are repeatedly close
to running short, extra production has a job waiting for it.

If finished goods are already accumulating, another line gives me more of
something I cannot move.

That difference is especially important in a large building because unused floor
creates a strong temptation to keep buying machinery.

The floor is available, so adding another line feels like progress.

But an unused square meter costs far less than a machine, employee and raw
materials producing permanent surplus.

I expand the production side after the retail side demonstrates that the current
output is insufficient.

<h2 id="scale-across-stores">
  One Factory becomes much more valuable when several stores use the same output
</h2>

Manufacturing economics change dramatically with scale.

A Factory that exists for one mediocre retail location has to carry its
building, machinery, labor and raw-material overhead against one store's demand.

The same production infrastructure makes far more sense when several profitable
locations are pulling from it.

An electronics operation can scale across a surprisingly large retail network;
I have used a single production base as the supply source for **12 Electronics
Stores** spread across several districts.

The useful lesson is not “build 12 stores.”

It is that the fixed production layer becomes easier to justify as more good
retail locations share it.

That also explains why I choose the business opportunity first.

If I cannot find enough demand or suitable locations for the finished product,
manufacturing more of it will not create customers.

The retail side of that decision stays in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/best-businesses">
  {" "}Best Businesses guide
</Link>
.

<h2 id="overproduction">
  What changed about Factory overproduction in 1.0?
</h2>

This is the part of older Factory strategy I would be most careful carrying into
1.0.

Producing a giant surplus and exporting everything that retail cannot absorb is
no longer a consequence-free fallback.

Exports now affect the product's **import/export index**. Heavy exporting can
push the value downward for the rest of the week, which means dumping more
surplus can gradually worsen the price I receive for that same product.

The index also gives useful context to imports.

An index of **1.0** represents the normal price level.

At **1.3**, the product is roughly **30% above normal**. That is favorable if I
am selling into the export side, but expensive if I am trying to import the same
product or related supply at that moment.

For a Factory, I mainly use this information as an overproduction warning.

If finished goods keep rising, the first fix is usually not “export faster.”

I check whether the production line is larger than the retail network needs,
whether another store can genuinely use the product, or whether output should
simply stop growing until demand catches up.

Export remains useful.

It just has a market consequence now.

<h2 id="factory-not-producing">
  Why is my Factory not producing?
</h2>

The fastest way I have found to fix a stopped line is to identify exactly where
the product stopped moving.

### Machines are idle

I check every required raw material first, then the Factory Workers assigned to
the line.

A full shelf of the wrong ingredients is still a shortage.

### Raw materials are available but output stops

Storage can be the next bottleneck.

Current 1.0 fixed an issue where production could fail with limited shelf space
despite some capacity still being available, but I still check finished-goods
storage whenever the line behaves as though it has nowhere to place output.

### The Factory is producing but retail stores are empty

Production is no longer the problem.

The finished product has made it out of the manufacturing stage, so I move to
the warehouse and store-distribution chain rather than rebuilding the Factory.

### Factory and warehouse stock keep increasing

That is the clearest signal of all.

The company has more production than retail demand.

Buying another machine would make the imbalance worse.

### One product works while another keeps stopping

I compare their inputs separately.

Products that share some materials can create the illusion that the Factory is
well stocked even though one unique ingredient is missing for the failing line.

Following the product through those stages is much cheaper than responding to
every slowdown with more equipment.

<h2 id="factory-expansion">
  How I know the Factory is ready for the next product
</h2>

A stable first product gives me a baseline.

The raw materials arrive without emergency intervention. Workers keep the line
moving. Finished goods leave storage at a reasonable pace, and the retail
network sells enough of them that production still has a purpose.

Only then do I add another product family or substantially widen the existing
one.

The main reason is clarity.

If I add three new products, another group of workers and several raw materials
at the same time, the next shortage tells me almost nothing about which part of
the expansion was badly sized.

Adding one meaningful layer at a time lets me see its effect on materials,
labor and finished stock.

That makes scaling slower for a few in-game days and much easier to manage over
the rest of the save.

<h2 id="when-factory-pays-off">
  What a successful Factory looks like
</h2>

I judge the Factory by movement, not by the largest revenue screenshot I can
produce from it.

The machines stay supplied.

The workers have enough skill to keep useful output moving.

Finished products leave the building instead of taking over every shelf.

Several stores benefit from the same production infrastructure.

And outside purchasing for those manufactured goods becomes a smaller part of
the retail network.

That is a much more reliable sign than a fixed break-even number.

Factory profitability changes with product choice, labor, raw-material prices,
import/export conditions, the number of retail outlets and how aggressively the
production floor has been built.

A setup that looks extraordinary while feeding a large electronics network can
be terrible when copied into a save with one weak store.

When production is matched to actual retail demand, the Factory becomes a
scaling tool.

When production is built ahead of demand, it becomes an expensive warehouse
filling machine.
