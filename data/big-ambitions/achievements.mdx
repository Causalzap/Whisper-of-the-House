<h2 id="achievement-roadmap">
  The route I use for all 126 achievements
</h2>

Trying to progress every achievement from the opening hour makes a normal save
worse.

The better route is to separate the list into three kinds of objectives.

Some happen because the company is growing anyway: businesses, employees,
income, wealth, and Uncle Fred objectives.

Some need to start early because the final number is large: taxi rides, gasoline,
parking tickets, private-driver trips, Factory production, and specialist
workforces.

The last group is ideal cleanup material: expensive homes, huge fleets, boats,
casino visits, hospitalizations, sports, and the hidden one-off actions.

That gives me a much cleaner save.

I can build the company normally while making sure the counters most likely to
be painful later are already moving.

If a family eventually wants 250 taxi rides, 100 employees, or 1,000,000

<h2 id="start-early">
  Start these counters while normal play can still advance them
</h2>

Several achievements become disproportionately annoying when left until the rest
of the game is finished.

### The Passenger

The final target is **250 taxi rides**.

Taxis are genuinely useful early, when I am traveling between Business School,
furniture stores, new properties, and distant districts without a mature vehicle
setup.

Those ordinary trips are free achievement progress.

Once a private driver becomes my default transportation, discovering that I
still need hundreds of taxis turns a normal travel counter into deliberate
grinding.

### Expensive Drops

Gold requires **$10,000 spent on gasoline**.

Company vehicles and normal personal driving contribute over a long save, so I
let the number build before replacing every trip with another form of transport.

### Crime Rider

The Gold requirement is **250 parking tickets**.

This one is never especially elegant, but ordinary bad parking can remove a
surprising amount of the eventual cleanup.

I would rather enter late game needing another 70 or 80 tickets than arrive with
almost no progress.

### They See Me Rollin'

The private-driver family progresses through:

- Bronze — **10 trips**
- Silver — **25 trips**
- Gold — **100 trips**

Once private driving is convenient, I use it for real travel instead of saving
all 100 trips for the final hour of the achievement run.

<h2 id="natural-progression">
  Let the company unlock the ordinary milestones
</h2>

A large part of the list measures how far the business has grown.

**Product Pusher** eventually requires **30 successful retail businesses**.

**Suit Castles** ends at **10 successful office businesses**.

**Boss Man** reaches **100 employees**.

**Money Maker** asks a single business to reach **$250,000 weekly income**.

**Big Stacks** ends at a **$10,000,000 bank balance**.

**Blue Cheese** pushes total personal wealth to **$100,000,000**.

These are much easier to treat as milestones than as isolated tasks.

If the bank balance is already close to $10 million, delaying an unnecessary
luxury purchase for a short time makes sense.

Building the whole save around sitting on cash from the moment I reach $2
million does not.

Uncle Fred's objective family works the same way.

**Your Best Friend** progresses at **30, 60, and 90 completed objectives**.

Those objectives are already moving the company through its main systems, so I
follow the normal route and let the achievement ride alongside it. The actual
sequence of what to unlock next is covered in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/progression-guide">
  {" "}Big Ambitions progression guide
</Link>
.

<h2 id="customer-income">
  Build the high-customer and high-income tiers around the right location
</h2>

A few achievements look like ordinary progression until the Gold requirement
exposes how specialized the final business needs to be.

**Dirty Floors** requires:

- 100 customers per day
- 250 customers per day
- **800 customers per day**

A small early Gym is the wrong place to force that final number.

If its building only supports 15 customers per hour, adding more equipment
cannot turn the property into an 800-customer-per-day location.

Gold belongs on a business with the demand, operating hours, equipment capacity,
and property size to support serious throughput.

**Money Maker** has a similar final-tier trap.

Its Gold requirement is **$250,000 weekly income from one business**.

Thirty average stores do not combine to satisfy that condition.

I keep at least one high-capacity, high-demand business capable of becoming the
large single-location earner rather than assuming total company income will
cover it.

The business and building choices behind that are handled in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/best-businesses">
  {" "}Best Businesses guide
</Link>
.

<h2 id="specialist-employees">
  Save the 100-person specialist teams for automated recruitment
</h2>

The profession achievements are straightforward on paper and tedious if started
at the wrong stage.

Several Gold tiers require **100 hired and scheduled employees of one
profession**, including:

- Lawyers
- Programmers
- Graphic Designers
- Travel Agents
- Event Planners

The important word is **scheduled**.

Hiring 100 people and leaving them unused in the employee list does not solve the
requirement.

That is why I wait until the company can support genuine large offices and the
recruitment process no longer depends on repeatedly handling every candidate by
hand.

Two related families end at smaller numbers:

- **Human Resources:** **50 HR Managers**
- **Freightshakers:** **20 Delivery Drivers**

By this stage, the challenge is mostly scale.

Recruitment, Headhunters, employee requirements, schedules, HR training, and
replacement automation are all covered in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/employees-hr-guide">
  {" "}Employees & HR guide
</Link>
.

For achievement planning, the rule is simpler: do not dismantle the businesses
that can schedule those professions until their counters are finished.

<h2 id="property-achievements">
  Property achievements are cheaper after cash stops being fragile
</h2>

Several late-game families ask me to own or rent far more property than normal
early progression requires.

**Storage King** ends at **10 rented warehouses**.

**Bricks and Mortar** requires **30 owned buildings**.

**Home Sweet Home** reaches **20 rented apartments**.

**Private Mansion** progresses through homes of at least:

- **90 m²**
- **200 m²**
- **500 m²**

I leave most of this until operating income can absorb the leases and purchases
without competing with useful expansion.

Ten warehouses are a good example.

My first warehouse should solve a real supply problem. Renting another nine
before the company needs them simply turns an achievement counter into recurring
overhead.

Late in the save, that cost matters far less.

### Bling Cribs

This family measures the value of the furniture inside the home rather than just
the property.

Gold requires at least **$2,000,000 worth of furniture in one apartment**.

At that point I deliberately furnish for the condition. There is no need to
pretend two million dollars of furniture is a normal apartment setup.

### The Designer

Gold requires **$1,000,000 spent on interior designing**.

This one can accumulate more naturally as the company reaches the stage where I
pay for installations instead of manually carrying every desk, chair, and
fixture into every new business.

<h2 id="vehicles-luxury">
  Delay the pure luxury purchases until they no longer compete with expansion
</h2>

The vehicle achievements mix useful company assets with obvious endgame spending.

**Carpark** ends at **40 owned vehicles**.

Delivery vehicles and anything else the company genuinely uses can contribute
before I start buying extras purely for the counter.

**Mad Whip** requires a single car worth at least:

- $15,000
- $90,000
- **$120,000**

That $120,000 car is much easier to justify after the same money has stopped
being the difference between opening another profitable location and waiting.

**Mechanic Paradise** is cumulative instead.

Gold requires **$250,000 spent repairing vehicles**.

Normal accidents and vehicle use can push that number for a long time. I check
the remaining amount near the end before deciding whether deliberate repair
spending is necessary.

Boats are even more clearly late-game.

**The Sailer** finishes at **10 boats**.

**Sea Bling** Gold requires owning a boat worth at least **$90,000,000**.

That is cleanup money.

I want the operating company finished before ninety million dollars disappears
into a boat.

<h2 id="factory-achievements">
  Keep Executive Producer running beside useful manufacturing
</h2>

The **Executive Producer** family is large enough to deserve early planning:

- Bronze — produce **10,000 Factory items**
- Silver — **100,000**
- Gold — **1,000,000**

One million units can take a long time.

The easiest route is to get a real production network working and let the
counter grow while I continue playing the rest of the save.

I do not need a special million-item Factory built only for the achievement.

If stores are already consuming the output, every useful production cycle moves
the achievement forward without creating an enormous pile of unwanted goods.

If production repeatedly stops, I fix the manufacturing chain rather than
treating the achievement counter as the problem.

Raw materials, Factory Workers, production scaling, storage, and 1.0
overproduction behavior are covered in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/factory-guide">
  {" "}Factory guide
</Link>
.

There is also a good reason not to manufacture millions of surplus units purely
for Gold.

In 1.0, heavy exports affect the import/export index. Useful production is a
much cleaner achievement route than creating a giant surplus and trying to dump
it afterward.

<h2 id="rival-achievements">
  Arch-nemesis only counts after a Special Rival is defeated
</h2>

The achievement family is explicit:

- **Arch-nemesis - Bronze:** defeat **1 Special Rival**
- **Arch-nemesis - Silver:** defeat **2**
- **Arch-nemesis - Gold:** defeat **all 4 Special Rivals**

Several earlier Rival states are easy to mistake for progress farther along the
chain.

Receiving the first warning is not defeat.

Entering **Active Rivalry** is not defeat.

Reducing one of the Rival's businesses is not defeat.

Having more total income than that Rival is not enough evidence by itself either.

I leave the checklist item open until the game recognizes the opponent as a
defeated Rival.

The direct competition, takeover, property, and pressure strategy stays in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/rivals-guide">
  {" "}Special Rivals guide
</Link>
.

That distinction also matters for one of the hidden achievements later, because
its requirement specifically refers to a **former rival**.

<h2 id="long-save-achievements">
  Some achievements mostly reward keeping the same save alive
</h2>

A few goals become easier simply because a successful company keeps operating.

### Society's Friend

Gold ends at **$20,000,000 paid in taxes**.

A large profitable company eventually generates enormous tax payments without
needing me to make the business deliberately inefficient.

### Lucky Potato

Casino winnings are cumulative:

- $50,000
- $250,000
- **$1,000,000**

### Ludomani

This is a separate casino counter:

- 2 visits
- 5 visits
- **10 visits**

Because the two families measure different things, I make sure the visit count
is also finished rather than assuming one long gambling session covers both.

### Another One

The age milestones are:

- celebrate the 70th birthday
- celebrate the 75th birthday
- celebrate the **100th birthday**

There is no profitable business trick that makes the character turn 100
instantly.

A genuine 126/126 run therefore needs a save that survives long after the
company has already become extremely wealthy.

<h2 id="hoarder-insomnia">
  Hoarder and Insomnia belong after the company can survive the disruption
</h2>

**Hoarder** escalates sharply:

- Bronze — stockpile **100,000** units of one product
- Silver — **2,000,000**
- Gold — **10,000,000**

Ten million units is not a sensible early warehouse buffer.

I wait until tying up that much money and storage capacity no longer threatens
the rest of the company.

The achievement only needs the stockpile.

It does not need me to pretend ten million units is good day-to-day inventory
management.

**Insomnia** asks for hospitalization:

- 5 times
- 10 times
- **20 times**

This becomes much less disruptive once businesses can operate for stretches
without the player being physically available.

Early hospitalization costs time that I still need for setup and emergency
fixes.

Late hospitalization is mostly a cleanup counter.

<h2 id="golf-tennis">
  Put golf and tennis into one late-game activity session
</h2>

The sports families have almost no reason to interrupt business progression.

**A Game of Love** requires:

- win 1 tennis match
- win 2
- win **3**

**Just Tap It In** requires a single-game golf score of:

- 250
- 500
- **750**

I save both for a quiet period when the company can run while I spend time away
from normal business tasks.

<h2 id="hidden-achievements">
  All three hidden achievements
</h2>

These are the three standalone hidden achievements outside the 41
Bronze/Silver/Gold families.

### Bullseye

**Hit the golf cart while playing golf.**

I combine this with the 250 / 500 / 750 golf-score attempts rather than making a
separate trip.

### Hostile Takeover

**Own the Hamptons home of a former rival.**

The key wording is **former rival**.

Owning an expensive Hamptons house by itself does not satisfy the condition. I
leave this until the Rival side of the save has progressed far enough for the
relevant owner to qualify as a former Rival.

### Pound Sand

**Destroy a sandcastle on the beach.**

This has almost no connection to normal business progression, which makes it
easy to overlook for an entire save.

I handle it during a deliberate late-game beach trip instead of waiting for
normal objectives to send me there.

<h2 id="final-cleanup">
  The final cleanup order before I sell or dismantle anything
</h2>

The dangerous point in a 100% run is when the company feels finished before the
achievement list actually is.

Before selling buildings, emptying warehouses, shrinking offices, or removing
production, I check every remaining requirement that still depends on the empire
I built.

**Factory still needed?**  
Keep production running until Executive Producer reaches the required tier.

**Hoarder unfinished?**  
Do not empty the warehouse holding the stockpile.

**Specialist employee counters incomplete?**  
Keep the businesses that can schedule those professions.

**Freightshakers incomplete?**  
Keep enough logistics infrastructure to employ the Delivery Drivers.

**Property or fleet counters unfinished?**  
Check Bricks and Mortar, Storage King, Home Sweet Home, and Carpark before
liquidating assets.

Only after those infrastructure-dependent goals are secure do I focus entirely
on the remaining repetition:

- taxi rides;
- parking tickets;
- gasoline spending;
- private-driver trips;
- casino winnings and visits;
- hospitalizations;
- birthdays;
- golf and tennis;
- hidden actions;
- luxury property and vehicle purchases.

That order prevents the worst kind of achievement cleanup: selling a mature
company and then discovering that the missing achievement requires rebuilding
half of it.

The profitable empire is useful until the checklist says otherwise.
