<h2 id="set-prices">
  How I set prices in Big Ambitions 1.0
</h2>

Pricing is one of the cheapest things to change and one of the easiest things to
blame for the wrong problem.

Early in a save, I handle it manually.

I open the business in BizMan, look at the individual products or services, and
compare what I am charging with the local market rather than carrying a number
over from another district or an older save.

A Gym gave me a good early example.

Its cover charge had been sitting around **$32**, while the lowest competing
price visible in that market was about **$25.68**. I opened at **$24** instead
of trying to defend the old number.

I would not use $24 as a universal Gym price. What mattered was the gap between
my price and the market I had actually entered.

That same approach works for retail products: start with the neighborhood in
front of me, let the business run, then adjust from what customers and the
business metrics are showing.

<h2 id="is-price-the-problem">
  Before changing the price, check whether pricing is actually the problem
</h2>

A weak day does not automatically mean something is overpriced.

The **Insight** screen is where I separate pricing from the rest of the store.

That 1.0 snapshot is useful because several numbers point in different
directions at once:

- **Pricing: 100%**
- **Interior: 100%**
- **Cleanliness: 96%**
- **Satisfaction: 85%**
- **Customer Service: 47%**
- **Promotion: 43%**
- **Marketing: 27%**
- **Traffic Index: 19%**

If I looked only at revenue and started discounting, I would be fixing a
100%-rated part of the business while leaving much weaker constraints untouched.

A low Traffic Index is especially important. Price changes cannot create
customers who never reach the location.

Poor Customer Service creates a different problem. More people may arrive, but
the store still fails to turn that traffic into the experience I expected.

I use price as the fix when the evidence points at price.

Otherwise I leave it alone.

<h2 id="price-by-neighborhood">
  The same business can need different prices in different neighborhoods
</h2>

Opening a second copy of a profitable store does not give me a second copy of
its market.

A Gym in Garment District and a Gym in Lower Manhattan may face different
competitors and different local pricing. The equipment and operating model can
be almost identical while the price that makes sense is not.

That becomes even more obvious with multi-product businesses.

Fast Food might sell Burgers, Pizza, Hot Dogs, Soda and several other items from
the same property. One product can be comfortably priced while another is the
one drawing complaints.

I treat those prices separately.

If Soda is the problem, I adjust Soda.

Changing every menu item because one line is wrong makes the next day's results
harder to interpret and can destroy margin on products that customers were
already happy to buy.

The same idea applies when I expand across Manhattan: I carry the business model
into the new district, then rebuild its prices around the market that exists
there.

<h2 id="manual-pricing">
  How aggressive I am with manual pricing
</h2>

A newly opened business already has several unknowns.

I am still learning how much traffic the building really attracts, whether the
weekly schedule holds together, how quickly inventory moves, and whether the
equipment capacity matches the customer flow.

During that period, I prefer a price that lets the store produce clean
information.

Starting near the local market gives me that.

If the business is busy, capacity is being used and Pricing remains healthy,
there may be room to move upward later.

When price satisfaction falls, I can bring the affected item back toward the
market without changing four other parts of the business at the same time.

Service businesses deserve a little extra discipline here.

A Gym cover charge or Hairdresser service does not show me an obvious box cost
every time a customer pays, so it can feel as though every extra dollar is free
margin. That stops being useful if customers stop accepting the price.

I care about the result after demand reacts, not the theoretical margin printed
on one transaction.

<h2 id="service-and-product-prices">
  Service prices and add-on products need separate attention
</h2>

A Gym can have a perfectly reasonable cover charge and an overpriced Energy
Drink.

A Hairdresser can have good service pricing while Hair Care Product needs an
adjustment.

Those smaller items are easy to overlook because they are not the reason I
opened the business, but the game prices them independently.

That becomes more important as the catalog widens.

I avoid treating a business as though it has one master price slider. Each
product or service is part of the local market on its own, so the useful change
is usually the smallest one that solves the visible pricing issue.

<h2 id="when-to-hire-pricing-manager">
  When is a Pricing Manager worth hiring?
</h2>

Manual pricing is quick when I own one or two businesses.

The job changes after expansion.

Checking several stores across multiple districts means reopening the same type
of comparison again and again. At that point I would rather see the pricing
problem centrally than spend part of every in-game week cycling through BizMan
pages.

That is where the Headquarters **Pricing Manager** earns a desk.

I assign the manager to a neighborhood, open the pricing plan, and get a
side-by-side comparison for the businesses and products under that area.

The broader decision about when Headquarters belongs in the company stays in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/progression-guide">
  {" "}progression guide
</Link>
. Once I already have it, Pricing Manager is the role I add when manual price
checks have become recurring administration.

<h2 id="current-suggested-market">
  Current Price vs Suggested Price vs Market Price
</h2>

The 1.0 Pricing Manager screen gives me three numbers that answer different
questions.

**Current Price** is what the store is charging now.

**Suggested Price** is the manager's proposed adjustment.

**Market Price** gives me the surrounding benchmark that makes the suggestion
easier to judge.

The same screen showed:

- **Energy Drink:** $3.90 Current → $4.90 Suggested → $4.91 Market
- **Gym Cover Charge:** $20.00 Current → $25.15 Suggested → $25.16 Market
- **Soda Can:** $3.00 Current → $3.77 Suggested → $3.77 Market

This is why I do not think of Suggested Price as a discount tool.

In that case, I was leaving money on the table.

The manager was proposing **higher** prices across all three visible lines, and
the suggestions sat almost exactly on the current market values.

That is a useful late-game correction because underpricing can be much harder to
notice than complaints about overpricing. Customers are happy, the store still
makes money, and nothing is obviously broken; I simply earn less from each sale
than the current neighborhood would support.

<h2 id="apply-suggested-prices">
  When I use Apply Suggested Prices
</h2>

Once the Current, Suggested, and Market numbers are close enough to make sense
together, applying the manager's prices is the fast option.

I still read the screen before pressing it.

The Lower Manhattan example above was easy to accept because the suggestions
were only a cent or so below the visible market benchmarks:

**$4.90 vs $4.91**, **$25.15 vs $25.16**, and **$3.77 vs $3.77**.

That is very different from blindly accepting a number when the store has just
opened, inventory is broken, or the market has been disrupted by a competitor.

For established businesses, Suggested Price gives me a strong baseline and saves
the repetitive work of rebuilding the comparison manually for every location.

After applying it, I let the business operate.

The next useful information comes from what happens afterward, not from pressing
the button several more times the same day.

<h2 id="when-not-to-apply">
  When I leave the current price alone
</h2>

There are several situations where I want more context before changing anything.

A brand-new store has not produced enough normal operating data yet.

A store running out of stock is mixing supply failure with pricing, so changing
both at once makes the result difficult to read.

A business already capped by customer throughput may have a more valuable
equipment or property decision ahead of it.

And when competition has just changed sharply, I want to understand whether the
new market price still leaves enough margin for the location to be worth
fighting over.

Pricing Manager makes the adjustment fast. It does not remove the reason for
looking at the business around that adjustment.

<h2 id="rival-pricing">
  What happens to pricing when a Rival starts competing directly?
</h2>

A price that worked for weeks can become stale even when nothing inside the
store changed.

Direct Rival competition is one reason.

Once another company starts pressing the same market, the local benchmark can
move. That is when I reopen the Pricing Manager instead of assuming the store
itself suddenly became worse.

If Suggested Price has shifted, I know the environment changed enough to warrant
another pricing decision.

There is still a limit to how far I follow that move.

Matching a Rival lower and lower only helps while the business remains worth
operating. If the fight is destroying margin, the better decision may involve
where I compete, what business I open next, or whether this is the market I want
to contest at all.

Those decisions belong in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/rivals-guide">
  {" "}Rivals guide
</Link>
. On the pricing side, the important habit is simply to recheck the market when
competition changes instead of treating yesterday's price as permanent.

<h2 id="pricing-troubleshooting">
  A weak store after a price change: what I check next
</h2>

When revenue drops after repricing, I avoid changing the same number again
immediately.

First I look at Pricing in Insight.

If that score worsened, I have a useful signal that the new price went too far.

If Pricing remains healthy, I move outward.

Traffic may have changed. Customer Service may be weak. Marketing may not be
bringing enough people in. A station may be understaffed, inventory may be
missing, or the building may already be hitting its practical capacity.

That sequence matters because several of those failures can look like the same
thing on the income graph.

Low revenue is the symptom.

Price is only one possible cause.

<h2 id="price-is-set">
  When I stop adjusting a price
</h2>

A stable price becomes something I leave in the background.

Customers accept it, Insight gives me no pricing warning, the margin fits what I
expect from the business, and the local market has not moved enough to justify
another change.

At that point there is no reward for nudging the number every morning looking
for the last few cents.

I revisit it when the environment gives me a reason: a new district, a new
business, a Rival pushing the market, or a Pricing Manager showing that my old
number has drifted well away from current conditions.

Until then, the useful work is elsewhere in the company.
