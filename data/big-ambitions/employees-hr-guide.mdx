<h2 id="employee-system">
  How I handle employees before HR automation
</h2>

The first employee is simple enough.

I need somebody on a register, behind a Hairdresser station, cleaning the floor
or operating whatever job the business requires. I hire the person, put them on
the schedule and see whether the store stays open.

That approach starts falling apart surprisingly quickly.

One Hairdresser can need several specialists plus cleaning and customer-service
coverage. A second business adds another weekly schedule. Then somebody calls
in sick, a candidate wants equipment I forgot to buy, and the employee I just
trained is working fewer hours than the empty station actually needs.

At that point the problem is no longer finding one worker.

It is maintaining a workforce.

In one 1.0 company, that shift became obvious at roughly **30 employees across
several businesses**. A sick call was no longer an unusual interruption; it was
part of normal operation. That was the point where Headquarters HR started
saving more time than another round of manual scheduling.

I still learn the manual system first, because HR automation works much better
when I already understand what a broken schedule looks like.

<h2 id="recruit-employees">
  What I check before hiring a candidate
</h2>

Skill is only one line on the candidate card.

I also look at wage, full-time or part-time availability, and the demands that
come with that employee.

Those demands can turn a cheap hire into immediate extra work.

I have hired an employee who would not be satisfied without an **office phone**.
That meant leaving Headquarters, going to an office-supply store and buying the
missing equipment before the complaint was actually solved.

Other candidates can care about things such as their work pattern, weekends,
salary or workplace equipment.

Early on, I am fairly selective because replacing one awkward candidate is
cheap. I would rather wait for another applicant than redesign a tiny first
store around a demand that makes no sense there.

That changes later.

Once I have a Headhunter producing a large candidate pool, I can filter much
more deliberately instead of accepting whoever happens to arrive from the
recruitment agency.

<h2 id="employee-schedule">
  Why copying one schedule across the whole week can cause problems
</h2>

Manual scheduling looks faster than it really is.

I can build one day that covers every required station, copy it, paste it
across the week and make the calendar look full in seconds.

The trouble appears when I check the employees rather than the colored blocks.

A copied schedule can push someone beyond the hours they are willing or allowed
to work. I ran into exactly that while filling a Hairdresser: the daily pattern
looked fine, but repeating it across every day left employees over their hour
limit.

For a small store, I usually solve this by hand.

I cover the required jobs first, then spread the remaining hours between the
employees I already have. If one station is empty for a quiet hour, that can be
cheaper than hiring another person whose only purpose is making the schedule
look perfect.

A larger office is different. When I am assigning ten Event Planners or dozens
of workers across several businesses, manually shuffling every block stops being
a useful use of my time.

That is where the HR Manager starts to matter.

<h2 id="when-to-hire-hr-manager">
  When is an HR Manager worth hiring?
</h2>

I hire one when employee maintenance starts interrupting expansion.

The exact employee count is less important than the symptoms:

the same week contains several schedules to rebuild, training is piling up,
sick employees leave holes, or I am delaying another business because I know
staffing it will mean another hour inside the calendar.

At Headquarters, the HR Manager turns those repeated jobs into a plan.

In the 1.0 setup I used, the HR Manager screen offered:

- automatic employee training up to **100%**;
- replacement of absent employees;
- employee assignment to the HR plan;
- schedule support for the employees under that manager.

One active plan also showed capacity for **50 assigned employees**.

That 50-employee example is useful for planning a growing company, but I still
check the capacity shown by my own manager rather than assuming one HR desk will
cover the company forever.

A later office expansion made the limit visible in practice: after assigning a
large group of employees, the manager showed only **17 more slots** available.
At that point I knew another wave of hiring would need additional HR capacity.

<h2 id="manual-vs-hr-training">
  Manual training is faster; HR training is easier to live with
</h2>

Before HR, training is noticeable work.

In the current 1.0 run I used, a manual training session increased skill by
**10 percentage points**. That makes it possible to push a weak employee upward
quickly, but doing the same thing across a growing workforce becomes repetitive
very fast.

HR changes the tradeoff.

The automated training is described in-game as **slower but cheaper**, and the
employees can continue working while they improve.

That is usually what I want once the business is operating normally.

If I urgently need a Headquarters specialist to reach a particular skill level,
manual training can still be worth the interruption. For ordinary retail and
office staff, I am happier letting HR handle the long climb in the background.

This distinction becomes especially useful when I hire cheap candidates with
mediocre starting skill.

I do not need every new employee to arrive at 80–100% if the company already
has enough coverage for HR to improve them safely over time.

<h2 id="schedule-autofill">
  HR schedule autofill is useful, but I still inspect the result once
</h2>

The first time I used autofill on a larger business, I expected a perfect
calendar.

What I got was closer to a very good first draft.

After adding employees and assigning them to the HR Manager, the schedule filled
itself. In an Event Planning office, the system covered almost the entire week,
but I still found a small number of unfilled hours afterward.

That was fine.

The important question was whether those empty hours were worth another salary.

In that case, a few uncovered hours were not enough to make me keep hiring
indefinitely. On another schedule I needed one more worker because the missing
coverage affected a real station.

This is why I check the finished calendar once after autofill.

I am looking for an operational gap, not visual perfection.

<h2 id="employee-sick">
  What happens when an employee calls in sick?
</h2>

Sick calls are one of the reasons I stop relying on perfectly fitted schedules.

If every station depends on exactly one person and there is no spare capacity,
one absence can remove the coverage I spent all that time building.

An HR Manager can handle **absent-employee replacement** for the employees
assigned to the plan.

That became much more valuable once the company had dozens of workers. In one
1.0 run, a sick employee called out when the company had roughly **33
employees**, and HR dealt with the absence instead of sending me back into the
schedule manually.

I still make sure there are enough employees for HR to work with.

Automation cannot invent a spare Hairdresser or Event Planner if I have hired
exactly the minimum number of people and every one of them is already busy.

<h2 id="employee-demands">
  Employee demands are cheaper to solve before the schedule depends on that person
</h2>

Some demands are trivial.

An office phone is a one-time purchase. A water cooler or other workplace item
can be easy to add if there is room.

Other demands affect how I want the business staffed.

A candidate who refuses weekends can be perfectly fine for a weekday office and
awkward for a seven-day retail business. A full-time preference can be useful
when I need long coverage but unnecessary when I am only filling a small gap.

So I read demands in the context of the job.

The employee does not need to be “easy” in the abstract. They need to fit the
schedule I am trying to build.

Once Headhunters are available, I become stricter because the recruiting system
gives me better tools for controlling the candidate pool.

<h2 id="headhunter">
  What the Headhunter changes about recruitment
</h2>

The Headhunter is the point where recruitment stops feeling like a series of
phone calls.

I create the plan from Headquarters, choose the employee type I want, set a
target wage and skill range, decide which demands I am willing to tolerate, and
let candidates continue arriving.

In one current setup, I deliberately asked for candidates around **40% skill**
because the HR Manager could train them toward 100% afterward.

That let me shift the recruiting question from:

> Who is already highly trained?

to:

> Who has acceptable pay and demands, and can my HR system finish the training?

The plan also lets me use recruitment points to remove demands I do not want to
deal with.

That becomes valuable for repeated expansion. If I am opening another Gym, I
can keep recruiting Gym Trainers rather than returning to the recruitment agency
every time the candidate list gets thin.

<h2 id="automatic-replacement">
  How automatic employee replacement works
</h2>

Continuous recruiting solves the candidate pipeline.

Automatic replacement solves what happens after somebody leaves.

In the 1.0 route I used, this Headhunter option remained unavailable until the
employee reached **75% skill**. After that, I could link the Headhunter with an
HR Manager and enable replacement for employees who:

- **resign**;
- **retire**.

The first plan also taught me that one Headhunter does not necessarily solve
every future staffing problem.

After setting up replacement, I was already preparing a second Headhunter so
one could maintain the existing workforce while another kept a broader candidate
pipeline moving.

That becomes worthwhile much later than the first HR Manager.

For a company with twenty or thirty employees, one good recruiting setup can
feel excessive. For a large office expansion or the achievement requirements
that ask for dozens of specialist employees, it stops feeling excessive very
quickly.

<h2 id="hr-and-headhunter-difference">
  HR Manager or Headhunter: which one do I add first?
</h2>

They solve different sides of the same problem.

The **HR Manager** is most useful after employees are already mine. I use HR for
training, schedule support and absence coverage.

The **Headhunter** works on the pipeline around that workforce: finding
candidates, filtering them and eventually replacing people who permanently
leave.

That is why HR comes first for me.

When I have ten people and a broken schedule, another stream of candidates is
not the main problem. I need the ten people I already employ to be trained and
covered properly.

Once opening a new business means recruiting ten people at a time, the
Headhunter starts saving more effort.

The two systems become much stronger together because I can recruit less-skilled
but acceptable candidates, hand them to HR for training, and stop personally
handling every resignation.

<h2 id="large-workforce">
  How I staff a large office without turning it into another full-time job
</h2>

Large office businesses are where the employee system finally justifies all of
this infrastructure.

A ten-desk Event Planning office gave me a good example.

Instead of recruiting one Event Planner, placing one shift, checking the
calendar and repeating that process ten times, I let the Headhunter build the
candidate pool, hired the group, assigned them under HR and used schedule
autofill.

The first pass was not mathematically perfect. Some hires were part-time and I
needed another worker or two to finish the coverage I wanted.

But the difference in workload was enormous.

That same approach matters even more for the achievement families that
eventually ask me to **hire and schedule 100 Lawyers, Programmers, Graphic
Designers, Travel Agents or Event Planners**.

I would never try to build those workforces through manual recruiting and
individual calendars from the beginning.

<h2 id="when-i-add-more-hr">
  When one HR Manager is no longer enough
</h2>

I add another manager when the existing plan is genuinely full or when splitting
the workforce makes the company easier to read.

A single HR Manager covering a manageable group is simpler than several
half-empty plans. There is no advantage in filling Headquarters with HR desks
while I still have plenty of assignment capacity.

The warning comes when new hires no longer fit comfortably under the current
manager.

At that point I prefer adding HR capacity before opening another staff-heavy
business. Otherwise the new location arrives with employees I can recruit but
cannot automate cleanly.

The same reasoning applies to Headhunters.

I add the second recruiting plan because the first one has a separate job to do,
not because Headquarters has an empty desk.

That keeps the employee side of the company proportional to the businesses it
actually supports.
