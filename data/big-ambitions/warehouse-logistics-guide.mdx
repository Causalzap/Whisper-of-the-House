I set up warehouse automation once repeated restocking is costing more time than
the businesses themselves. The working chain is **Purchasing Agent → warehouse
storage → Smart Delivery target → delivery vehicle → Logistics Manager → store
minimum stock**. If a store stays empty, I trace that chain in order instead of
adding more inventory: first confirm the product reached the warehouse, then
check storage space, the destination, the assigned product, and the store's

<h2 id="when-to-rent-warehouse">
  When should you rent your first warehouse?
</h2>

With one simple store, a warehouse can cost more than the problem it solves.

If I only need an occasional box of drinks or one small product restock, driving
that run myself is still manageable. Adding warehouse rent, pallet shelving, a
vehicle and Headquarters salaries simply moves money into infrastructure before
the business has enough volume to use it.

The calculation changes when the same supply run keeps returning.

Maybe two stores are consuming the same product. Maybe the catalog has widened
enough that one missed pickup empties several shelves. Or I am delaying another
location because I know that opening it means another set of recurring stock
trips.

That is the stage where warehousing starts buying back useful time.

The progression route that gets me to this point stays in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/progression-guide">
  {" "}progression guide
</Link>
Here I am assuming the decision has already been made: the company needs a
real supply chain.

<h2 id="first-warehouse">
  What the first warehouse actually needs
</h2>

Uncle Fred's warehouse objectives give a sensible minimum setup:

- rent a small warehouse;
- start the warehouse business;
- purchase at least one pallet shelf;
- place the pallet shelf inside.

A small warehouse is not a temporary mistake that needs replacing as soon as I
can afford something larger.

For the first supply network, it is often exactly enough.

The important question is not how much empty floor I can buy. It is how many
products and destinations the current company needs to support.

A Gym that mainly needs drinks creates a very different storage problem from a
Supermarket carrying several refrigerated and retail categories. Two simple
stores can be easier to supply than one wide-catalog business even if their
combined revenue is higher.

That is why I size the first warehouse around the **SKUs and destinations I have
now**, with a little room for the next location.

<h2 id="purchasing-agent">
  How the Purchasing Agent fits into warehouse automation
</h2>

A warehouse only stores what reaches it.

The Purchasing Agent is the upstream side of the system: the employee at
Headquarters who handles the buying relationship that keeps goods coming in.

If the shelves inside the warehouse remain empty, that is where I look before I
touch the delivery route.

I check whether the correct product is being purchased, whether the order is
going to this warehouse, and whether the target actually calls for more stock.

That distinction matters because later problems happen on the other side of the
warehouse.

A full warehouse with an empty store is a distribution problem.

An empty warehouse is usually a purchasing or storage problem.

Keeping those two directions separate makes troubleshooting much faster.

<h2 id="pallet-storage">
  How much pallet storage should you buy?
</h2>

I buy enough shelving to hold the stock buffer I am actually asking the system
to maintain.

Buying shelves first and inventing stock targets afterward usually leaves me
with either unused floor or a warehouse that fills immediately.

The product mix tells me what storage I need.

A few high-turnover products may justify large buffers even when the catalog is
small. A wide catalog can consume pallet space surprisingly quickly because each
product needs somewhere to sit even if individual sales are modest.

I leave expansion space, but I do not furnish the entire building in advance.

There is a practical reason for that beyond cost: if storage fills up later, I
want it to be obvious that the business has genuinely outgrown the current
layout rather than wonder whether I simply bought far too much stock.

<h2 id="smart-delivery">
  What does Smart Delivery actually do?
</h2>

Smart Delivery became much easier to use once I stopped reading its number as
the size of the next shipment.

It behaves as a **warehouse stock target**.

If the target is **4,000**, I am effectively telling the purchasing system that
I want the warehouse to hold around 4,000 units of that product. Once the target
has been reached, Smart Delivery stops ordering more simply because another
delivery day arrives.

That difference explains two common mistakes.

A target that is too low can make a healthy system look unreliable because the
stores consume the buffer almost as quickly as it arrives.

A target that is far too high does the opposite: the warehouse looks wonderfully
stocked while a large amount of cash sits inside boxes that the stores may not
need for weeks.

Smart Delivery works best for me when the target reflects real consumption
rather than a round number that merely feels safe.

<h2 id="stock-targets">
  How I choose warehouse and store stock targets
</h2>

There is no single target I reuse for every business.

I start by watching how quickly the stores consume the product and how long I
want the warehouse to survive without another purchase.

One 1.0 setup used routine warehouse targets around **4,000 units**.

In a larger Gym network, I held **6,000** units in the warehouse while setting
store destinations to maintain **2,000** each.

Those numbers worked for those businesses because their consumption and product
mix supported them. They are useful reference points, not universal settings.

The `Runs out in` estimate is especially useful once several destinations are
active.

If a store has enough stock for another ten or nineteen days, I do not need to
react to the raw unit count alone. If the estimate keeps dropping toward zero
between deliveries, I know the buffer or route needs attention.

That is a much better signal than simply making every number larger.

<h2 id="warehouse-to-store">
  How do products get from the warehouse to a store?
</h2>

Purchasing finishes when the goods arrive at the warehouse.

Retail restocking starts after that.

For the second half of the chain I need a delivery vehicle and a
**Logistics Manager** plan that connects the warehouse to the businesses I want
to supply.

The store must exist as a destination, and the products I expect to move need
minimum-stock settings for that destination.

This is where a warehouse can appear to be working perfectly while the retail
side still fails.

The pallets are full.

The Purchasing Agent is doing the job.

But no route is actually telling those goods to leave.

Once I separate inbound stock from outbound distribution, that situation becomes
much easier to diagnose.

<h2 id="logistics-destinations">
  How many destinations can a Logistics Manager handle?
</h2>

Destination capacity is one of the limits I watch as the company grows.

An earlier Logistics Manager setup I used could handle **3 destinations**. Later
in the same 1.0 progression, the interface showed capacity for **up to 4
destinations**.

I use the number shown by the current manager rather than assuming every plan has
unlimited reach.

That matters because a fourth or fifth store can create an apparent delivery
problem even though the warehouse is stocked and the route itself is configured
correctly.

The manager may simply have no room for another destination.

At that point I have a scaling decision: improve or expand logistics capacity,
reorganize which warehouse serves which businesses, or add another warehouse
network if the company has genuinely grown beyond the first one.

<h2 id="warehouse-not-delivering">
  Why is my warehouse not delivering?
</h2>

When a store stays empty, I trace the product from supplier to shelf.

### The product never reaches the warehouse

I start upstream.

The Purchasing Agent may not be buying the correct item, the purchase may point
somewhere else, or the Smart Delivery target may already believe enough stock is
available.

### The warehouse is full or has too little usable storage

A purchasing setup can be correct and still fail when the goods have nowhere to
go.

I check pallet space before increasing order targets again.

### The product is in the warehouse but the store is empty

Now I move downstream.

The store needs to be a Logistics Manager destination, and the relevant product
needs a minimum-stock target there.

A full warehouse proves purchasing is working. It does not prove distribution
has been configured.

### Deliveries happen, but the store still runs out

That usually sends me to the target amounts.

A minimum of 200 units can be technically valid and still be useless for a store
that sells through 200 units before the next route catches up.

I compare consumption with the `Runs out in` estimate and increase the buffer
only as much as the business actually needs.

### A new product never appears in the route

This often happens after expansion.

Adding another SKU to the store does not mean every existing logistics plan
automatically knows I want that product maintained there. I verify that the
product itself is assigned to the correct destination.

This left-to-right check solves most warehouse problems without buying anything
new.

<h2 id="new-business-logistics">
  What I check before adding another inventory-heavy business
</h2>

A new store can look affordable until I count what it adds behind the storefront.

Another business may introduce:

- products from a different supplier;
- additional warehouse stock;
- more pallet capacity;
- another delivery destination;
- higher vehicle usage;
- another Logistics Manager limit to watch.

That is why a Supermarket can change the supply network far more than another
Gym even when both fit inside the same rental budget.

The decision about which business deserves that infrastructure stays in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/best-businesses">
  {" "}Best Businesses guide
</Link>
On the warehouse side, I only care whether the existing network can support
the new catalog without becoming manual again.

If opening the next store means I will spend the following week compensating for
its supply chain personally, I prepare logistics first.

<h2 id="second-warehouse">
  When does a second warehouse make sense?
</h2>

Storage space is the obvious reason, but it is not the only one.

The first network can also become awkward because too many unrelated products
and destinations are competing for the same logistics capacity.

I keep one warehouse for as long as it remains easy to understand.

If there is still spare pallet space, destination capacity is available and the
stores are being supplied reliably, another warehouse only adds rent and another
system to configure.

The second warehouse becomes useful when expansion has produced a real split:
too much inventory for the current space, too many destinations for the current
management plan, or a product network that would be cleaner if another warehouse
served a separate group of stores.

That is a much stronger reason than owning enough cash to rent another building.

<h2 id="factory-handoff">
  What changes once Factory production enters the supply chain?
</h2>

The warehouse remains the distribution layer even after I begin manufacturing.

The difference is where some of the finished goods come from.

Instead of relying entirely on outside purchasing, the Factory can feed products
into the same broader retail network. That makes warehouse capacity and
downstream demand more important, not less.

A Factory producing faster than the stores consume can fill storage quickly, so
I want the warehouse system working cleanly before adding that extra source of
inventory.

The manufacturing side—machines, raw materials, Factory Workers, production
capacity and overproduction—is handled in my
<Link href="/big-ambitions/factory-guide">
  {" "}Factory guide
</Link>

From the warehouse perspective, I only need one thing to remain true:

finished goods should keep moving toward stores that can actually sell them.

<h2 id="healthy-logistics">
  What a healthy warehouse network looks like
</h2>

Once the setup is mature, I spend less time inside the warehouse menus.

Stock arrives before the warehouse is empty. Store buffers last through normal
sales. Delivery routes have room for the destinations I am using, and opening
another location no longer automatically adds another personal restocking run.

The system is still visible when something changes—a fast-selling product, a
new SKU, another destination, or a Factory suddenly producing more—but ordinary
days stop generating supply emergencies.

That is the point where warehousing has done its job.

The company can grow because products are moving in the background rather than
because I am still carrying them myself.
