<h2 id="what-i-keep">
  I choose materials around the next job instead of carrying everything
</h2>

The useful resource list changes as Chapter 1 moves away from the crashed
train.

During the early Outskirts, the materials I am least willing to waste are:

- **Electronics**
- **Wires**
- **Electrical Tape**
- **Aluminum**
- enough **Ice** that I still have options when I get back

Once I am working through the Depot, different shortages begin showing up.
**Light Bulbs** become worth grabbing before I need them, while Rubber, Refined
Metal and Titanium start appearing around machinery that did not matter at the
beginning.

I build each scavenging trip around the objective in front of me.

If the current repair needs Wires and Electronics, those take inventory space
before another pile of ordinary Metal. If I am one Titanium short of a Backpack,
a Titanium detour suddenly has much more value than it did an hour earlier.

That keeps resource gathering tied to progression instead of turning every
wreck into something I feel obligated to strip clean.

<h2 id="how-resources-work">
  The fastest resource searches start with the interaction prompt
</h2>

Breathedge 2 does not present every crafting material as an obvious chunk
floating in open space.

Many useful resources are still attached to the object they came from.

I find more by looking for actions such as:

**Break · Cut · Unscrew · Pull · Drill**

than by staring at the colour or shape of every piece of debris.

A washing machine can be a source of Electronics. Electrical Tape can still be
wrapped around a pipe. A lamp only matters when its fitting gives me an
interaction. Aluminum is something I drill from a deposit rather than collect
like loose scrap.

and over is usually a worse use of the trip than moving to the next
interactable object.

<h2 id="early-repair-materials">
  Electronics, Wires and Electrical Tape are the early repair materials I watch most closely
</h2>

These three components appear often enough that I can always find a reason to
spend them.

That does not mean I should.

<h3 id="electronics">
  Appliances are where I look first for Electronics
</h3>

Early train systems, communications equipment and survival repairs all compete
for Electronics.

I get them from electronic wreckage and dismantleable appliances. A washing
machine around the starting structures is one clear example.

untouched until the job is done.

<h3 id="wires">
  Wires become expensive once several repairs start consuming them together
</h3>

The first Wires near the train make the material feel common.

Later repairs change that impression quickly.

I inspect electrical panels, exposed wiring and machinery that still offers a
usable interaction. Seeing a cable is not enough; some sources need the correct
tool before I can actually take anything from them.

The shortage becomes more noticeable in the

<h3 id="electrical-tape">
  Taped pipes are much more useful than searching containers for Electrical Tape
</h3>

Electrical Tape was the early material that made me change how I looked at the
environment.

Instead of hoping it appears in storage, I watch for taped sections of pipes
and machinery that can be cut.

<h2 id="aluminum-plastic">
  Aluminum is worth taking on sight; Plastic is usually an early local search
</h2>

Ordinary **Metal** is rarely the material that keeps me stuck for long.

**Aluminum** is different.

I repeatedly ended up one or two pieces short, so once I learned what its
deposits look like I started collecting them whenever the route passed close
enough.

I find it attached to station equipment and broken components, so when an early

Neither material is a reason to abandon the current route and wander deep into
the Outskirts without a target.

<h2 id="depot-materials">
  Depot introduces materials that were easy to ignore earlier
</h2>

The colder industrial route changes what I pick up.

Light Bulbs suddenly matter for Service Warmers. Rubber becomes easier to find
once the industrial structures expose the right objects. Refined Metal starts
competing with ordinary scrap for attention.

These resources are easier to manage once I know their physical forms.

<h3 id="light-bulbs">
  Light Bulbs come from specific lamp assemblies
</h3>

Smashing random glowing objects did not solve my Light Bulb shortage.

The useful sources are interactable lamps and similar fixtures. I approach the
assembly, **unscrew the clamp or fitting**, then take the bulb.

<h3 id="refined-metal">
  Refined Metal is worth saving even when ordinary Metal is everywhere
</h3>

**Metal** and **Refined Metal** are separate resources.

I can have plenty of normal scrap while still counting individual pieces of
Refined Metal.

The resource appears around deeper industrial equipment and can also turn up in
containers. When I see an interactable Refined Metal object, I break it and
keep the result for a recipe that actually matters.

It appears as a small **spiked ball** rather than the kind of obvious mineral
deposit I had been looking for.

That is a **+75 carry-weight increase**.

**Cardboard, Foam and Fabric** are real crafting materials rather than

<h2 id="when-to-stop-scavenging">
  I end a resource run when the missing part is solved
</h2>

The most wasteful trips are usually the ones that continue after I have already
found what I came for.

If a repair was blocked by one Light Bulb and I now have the bulb, the next
random wreck is no longer automatically worth exploring.

If I needed two Electrical Tape and have them in the inventory, getting back to
the repair has more value than filling the remaining weight with ordinary
Metal.

And if the Backpack is the reason I went looking for Titanium, reaching four is
enough.

This keeps my storage from becoming a record of everything I happened to pass.

By the later Depot and jammer routes, the useful inventory is the one that
finishes the next job without forcing another scavenging trip — not the one
with the largest number of unrelated materials.
