<h3 id="favorite-flowers">
  Favorite Flowers — build complete flowers from the pieces
</h3>

During the guest-preparation sequence, I have to arrange Alexandra's favorite
flowers.

My first instinct was to sort the pieces by color.

That got me nowhere.

Each movable section is part of a **complete flower**. Some pieces contain the
top of a bloom, some contain leaves or stems, and others complete the lower
part.

I move them until the fragments combine into whole flowers instead of trying
to create a red-to-white color sequence.

<h3 id="flag-puzzle">
  Flag puzzle — sort by containment and size
</h3>

The Flag puzzle was another one where I originally interpreted the symbols too
literally.

The arrows represent relationships between things that can contain, produce, or
lead into one another.

The useful example for me was:

**tree → nest → egg → bird**

I separate the objects into their logical chains and arrange them from the
larger outside object toward what belongs inside or comes from it.

<h3 id="waking-william">
  Waking up William — 9:00, the bird, and the toe puzzle
</h3>

William is the Day 2 puzzle where I most often thought I needed to leave the
room and find another item somewhere else.

Most of the sequence is actually self-contained.

After I bring him Coffee, I start working through the objects around his room.

One early clock interaction resolves at:

**9:00**

From there I keep following the object clues rather than trying to wake William
with Coffee alone.

A sequence of room symbols leads me to **Bird Seed**.

I give the Bird Seed to the bird and receive a **Feather**.

The Feather is useful around William's feet.

The toes themselves give me the pattern for which toe to tickle next, so I do
not simply click them from left to right.

This was my biggest blocker in the room.

I had the correct item but was treating the feet as one interaction instead of
a small sequence.

Once I complete the toe pattern, I get access to a **small Key**.

The Key opens the next compartment and gives me **Eye Drops** and a **window
knob**.

I continue using those room items until William finally wakes.

At that point the task changes from "wake him up" to finishing his morning
requests.

He asks for his **hat**, then an **egg**, and then tells me to put the
**scroll on the table**.

I do not go back to the Coffee room once William is awake.

Everything I need to finish this task is part of William's sequence.

<h3 id="cleaning-floors">
  Cleaning the Floors — stop scrubbing when the dirt becomes a puzzle
</h3>

Cleaning the Floors is one of my favorite examples of Servant of the Lake
turning an ordinary chore into several small logic puzzles.

At first I kept scrubbing the stubborn spots and thought I needed stronger
cleaning fluid.

That is the wrong diagnosis.

When a dirty patch stops reacting like dirt, I look at the marks on it.

The first puzzle uses **dot counts**.

I follow the sequence from:

**1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6**

The next patch is a **directional path**.

I follow the directions through the pattern rather than counting marks again.

The last type is a **matching puzzle**.

I remove or pair the symbols that visually match until the board clears.

The important troubleshooting rule is:

**if scrubbing stops changing the stain, stop scrubbing.**

The remaining work is puzzle logic, not a missing cleaning item.

Once all of those hallway patches are cleared, Kate's dialogue advances and the
route moves toward the hunt.

<h3 id="cellar-tools">
  The Cellar tool puzzle needs more than one loadout
</h3>

This is where I came closest to thinking I had soft-locked the game.

I can only take **three tools at a time** into the cave.

The first combination I chose did not solve every obstacle.

That is intentional.

The statue lets me reset the sequence and choose another loadout.

The tools I keep in mind are:

- the **sledgehammer** for breakable obstacles;
- **Matches** when a branch needs fire or light;
- the **Magnet** when a Key is out of reach;
- the **Hook and Weight** for the related gap mechanism;
- the **Shovel** when the route requires digging.

I do not search for a single perfect three-tool combination.

When a branch reaches something my current tools cannot handle, I remember the
obstacle, reset, and return with the tool that matches it.

That turns the cave from a guessing game into several short reconnaissance
runs.

<h3 id="preparing-dinner">
  Preparing Dinner — soup, potatoes, and cake are separate jobs
</h3>

The Day 3 kitchen overwhelmed me at first because Kate asks for several things
at the same time.

I have to prepare:

- the soup;
- potatoes;
- and the cake.

I do not put everything into one cooking path.

For the normal Lake-style Shrimp Soup, the written recipe gives me this order:

**cut Onion, Tomato, and Shrimp**

Then:

**Oil → Onion Rings → Shrimp Parts → Tomato Wedges → Heat → Season**

<h3 id="serving-dinner">
  Serving Dinner — solve the three plates as constraints
</h3>

The main-course puzzle became much easier once I stopped trying to make three
balanced plates.

The family does not want balanced meals.

They each give me different rules.

The note tells me:

- **all food must be served**;
- **Aldous wants a vegetarian meal**;
- **William eats twice as much as Aldous**;
- I must account for **Aldous's allergy**;
- **Alexandra wants more meat than vegetables**;
- **William wants as much variety as possible**.

I start with **Aldous**.

His vegetarian restriction reduces the number of possible foods immediately,
and I make sure his allergy rule is satisfied before moving anything onto the
other plates.

Then I build **William's** plate.

His total needs to be twice Aldous's, and I give him as much variety as the
remaining food allows.

I leave **Alexandra** until last.

Her key condition is that she receives more meat than vegetables.

Working in that order is much easier than redistributing all three plates every
time one rule fails.
