<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
For a first Hawks build, I would start with **Gunslinger + Blaster Rifle** if I
want flexible ranged damage, or **Assault** if I expect to move aggressively
between objectives and exposed targets.

I do not restart because I dislike my opening choice. Specializations can be
changed later, while each named Operator's **locked unique talent** is the part
I build around for the long term.

I also want a **Medic somewhere in the squad**, even when Hawks is not the one
using it.

<h2 id="best-starting-specialization">
  The starting specializations I would actually pick for Hawks
</h2>

There are **eight** starting specializations:

**Assault, Gunslinger, Heavy, Medic, Soldier, Sharpshooter, Scout, and
Scoundrel.**

For a blind first campaign, I find **Gunslinger** and **Assault** the easiest
Hawks choices to live with.

Gunslinger works when I want Hawks making frequent ranged attacks without
waiting for one perfect firing position. I pair it with a **Blaster Rifle**
because the extra reach fits the way I actually move through most early
objectives better than forcing Hawks into pistol distance.

Assault is my fallback when I keep spending Hawks' turns advancing, flanking,
or attacking enemies that have lost good cover. Its mobility stays useful on
maps where sitting still is not an option.

I would not call either one universally best.

The more important lesson is that I do not have to solve Hawks' final build on
the character-creation screen.

<h2 id="specialization-not-permanent">
  I change specialization when the abilities stop matching my turns
</h2>

Once I can manage Operators through Personnel, I stop thinking of
specialization as their permanent identity.

Named Operators have their own locked talent.

That is the part I consider their real foundation.

Hawks has **Fearless Leader**. Kabb has Uppercut as a defining part of his
kit. Trick has his own recovery-focused talent.

Those stay relevant even when I reconsider the specialization sitting around
them.

My trigger for changing a class is simple:

**if several specialization abilities keep going unused across multiple
Missions, I stop paying for the idea of that build and change the role.**

That happened with Soldier Hawks.

The class looked excellent at first because it combined blaster bonuses,
grenades, melee, and Rocket Strike. In actual missions, I found myself using
Hawks mostly as a ranged leader. Melee came up rarely, a grenade might only
matter once, and other Operators could already cover explosive damage.

At that point, the versatility shown on the skill tree was no longer helping
me.

I would rather have three abilities supporting what Hawks does every turn than
six abilities covering situations I rarely create.

<h2 id="gunslinger">
  Gunslinger is my easiest ranged Hawks build
</h2>

Gunslinger is the first specialization I would return to if I wanted a simple
ranged Hawks.

Its early identity is repeated pressure rather than one slow, high-value shot.

**Keen Eye** starts by adding **6% ranged critical chance**.

**Quick Draw** gives me another way to fit an attack into a turn without
committing Hawks to a heavy two-AP firing pattern.

That works especially well with how often story missions ask me to move,
interact with an objective, or reposition around reinforcements.

The important part is that I do **not** automatically pair Gunslinger with a
Blaster Pistol just because the names look right together.

Specialization and weapon class are separate choices.

That pistol screen also shows **10 Melee Damage**.

The important correction for me is that the **10 is not Weapon Damage**. The
displayed Weapon Damage is **6**.

The pistol does have an excellent action-economy advantage: it can fire for
**1 AP**, so I can move and still fit multiple attacks into the same turn.

The problem is distance.

I tried the natural Gunslinger + Pistol idea in an early campaign and quickly
found myself having to choose between staying in good cover and moving Hawks
closer just to get the range I wanted.

One particularly ugly stretch had several displayed **50–70% shots** fail in
succession. I do not treat a run of misses as proof that a weapon is secretly
bad — probability can simply be cruel — but the situation exposed the real
problem: short range kept making Hawks work harder for acceptable shots.

That is why I prefer:

**Gunslinger + Blaster Rifle**

for a blind run.

I keep the fast, multi-shot identity while giving Hawks a more comfortable
working distance.

<h2 id="assault">
  Assault is the better pick when I keep moving toward the objective
</h2>

Assault grew on me once story missions started asking the squad to advance
instead of simply clear one defensive arena.

Its kit is built around movement, medium-to-close pressure, and punishing
enemies once they are exposed.

The starting screen gives me two especially useful numbers:

**Fleet of Foot — +1.0m First Movement**

**Strike the Flanks — +20% Critical Chance against Exposed enemies**

That combination is easy to understand in real combat.

I get farther with the first move of the turn, then gain a substantial crit
bonus when another action, piece of destroyed cover, or battlefield angle
leaves an enemy exposed.

Assault also has **Bull Rush**, which can push a target, and **Sweeping Salvo**
for a larger cone attack.

The Sweeping Salvo setup I tested cost **4 Advantage**.

Four matters because Advantage belongs to the entire squad. I can realistically
reach that cost without saving nearly every point for one character.

I prefer Assault over Gunslinger when I notice Hawks doing this repeatedly:

**move forward → create an angle → attack → reposition again next turn.**

If Hawks is already fighting that way, Assault stops asking me to change my
playstyle just to justify the skill tree.

<h2 id="soldier">
  Soldier only stays good when I use its whole toolkit
</h2>

Soldier was one of the most attractive options to me on the opening screen.

It offers a little of almost everything.

The version I inspected could increase **Blaster Damage and Grenade Damage by
2**, use a melee rush that can Daze, and work toward **Rocket Strike**.

That sounds perfect until I ask what Hawks actually does during a normal turn.

If I am:

- closing for melee;
- using grenades regularly;
- firing the blaster;
- and taking advantage of Rocket Strike;

then Soldier's flexibility is real value.

If I am standing behind a rifle for most of the Mission, it becomes a skill
tree full of bonuses I am paying for but rarely triggering.

That is exactly what happened to me.

I liked Soldier more while reading the abilities than I did after watching how
I actually spent Hawks' AP.

So I do not rank Soldier low because its abilities are weak.

I rank it below Gunslinger or Assault for my own first Hawks because it asks me
to use a broader toolkit to extract the same value.

If that hybrid playstyle is what I want, Soldier makes sense.

If not, I switch.

<h2 id="scout">
  Scout is what I use when Hawks should make everyone else better
</h2>

Scout made much more sense once I understood the value of shared Advantage and
accuracy support.

**Assess Threats** can generate Advantage from enemies inside its area.

Scout can also develop a **20% chance to gain Advantage after a kill**, while
its blaster support can **Spot** targets for the squad.

That creates a very different Hawks from Gunslinger.

With Gunslinger, I am usually asking:

> **How much useful damage can Hawks fit into this turn?**

With Scout, I am asking:

> **What can Hawks do now that improves the next two or three attacks?**

I like Scout when the rest of the squad already contains strong finishers.

If Kabb, Trick, or another damage Operator can capitalize on better target
setup, Hawks does not need to personally take every kill.

Scout becomes especially tempting when I am already generating plenty of raw
damage but keep losing efficiency to awkward hit chances and expensive
Advantage abilities.

<h2 id="scoundrel">
  Scoundrel fits a squad built around assists and one priority target
</h2>

Scoundrel is another specialization I prefer when Hawks is setting up the squad
instead of trying to lead the damage chart.

**Easy Mark** helps prepare an enemy for an ally's attack.

**Identify Weakness** can apply **Vulnerable**, which gives me a much clearer
reason to concentrate the whole squad on one dangerous target.

That makes Scoundrel more attractive in fights where one enemy matters much
more than the others.

A tough droid, specialist, or high-health threat is exactly where I want Hawks
starting the sequence and another Operator finishing it.

I like Gunslinger more when Hawks needs to spread personal damage across
several enemies.

I like Scoundrel more when I want the team to erase **one** enemy before it
gets another turn.

<h2 id="heavy">
  Heavy is the role I use when the squad needs someone to take pressure
</h2>

Heavy is not usually my Hawks specialization, but I like having the role
available somewhere in Zero Company.

On the Heavy setup I tested, moving an Operator into the specialization
immediately gave a meaningful defensive package:

- **+2 Defense**
- **+4 Health**
- basic blaster attacks that can Taunt
- **Bulwark** for area Taunt and additional Defense
- **Unstoppable** for retaliation against enemies that attack the Operator

That is not a subtle role.

Heavy is who I use when I deliberately want enemies looking at one durable
Operator instead of my more fragile damage or support characters.

Some Heavy progression can also lean into Morale. One setup I inspected could
reach **4 Morale stacks per turn**, with each stack restoring **2 HP**.

I usually keep Hawks out of this job.

Hawks is required for story progression often enough that I prefer keeping the
captain flexible rather than making my mandatory character the person I
deliberately expose to enemy fire.

Heavy makes more sense to me on somebody whose entire job is:

**take pressure → hold the position → give the rest of the squad room to work.**

<h2 id="medic">
  Medic is the specialization I most want somewhere in the squad
</h2>

Medic is not my favorite Hawks class.

It may be the class I am least willing to leave out of a difficult squad,
though.

The Medic setup I tested gets a **free Medpack that does not consume the normal
utility slot**, and I could use that healing **twice per battle**.

That is already enough to change how many consumables I need to bring on other
Operators.

**Combat Stim** costs **1 AP** and applies Stimulant for **2 turns**, increasing
the target's:

- Max Health
- Melee Damage
- Ranged Damage

That makes it useful before a teammate commits to a high-value attack sequence,
not just after the squad starts losing Health.

**First Responder** is even more important in a bad fight.

When the Medic rescues an ally using Rally, that ally comes back **Fully
Healed**.

I do not assume that means the injury system disappears — being downed has
consequences outside the immediate Health bar — but tactically, returning an
Operator at full Health is a huge improvement over barely getting them back
into the fight.

That is why my first-run rule is:

> **Hawks does not need to be the Medic, but somebody usually should be.**

Going into a difficult Tactical Mission without dependable healing has hurt me
more than running two Operators whose damage roles overlap slightly.

<h2 id="sharpshooter">
  Sharpshooter is strongest when the mission lets me stay put
</h2>

Sharpshooter has the clearest long-range identity, and the numbers can look
excellent.

One setup gives **3 stacks of Lethal**, worth:

**+15% chance to hit**

and

**+9% critical chance**.

The catch is not accuracy.

It is action economy.

The long-range weapon setup I tested could require **2 AP to fire**.

On a defensive map where I can claim good cover and keep shooting from the
same lane, that is exactly what I want.

On a mission where I need to:

- rescue somebody;
- capture an objective;
- reach extraction;
- rotate around reinforcements;
- or keep advancing through the map;

the same two-AP shot can become awkward.

A long move plus a two-AP attack may simply not fit into the turn I need.

That is why I do not call Sharpshooter the automatic best Hawks class just
because its ideal shots look great.

I use it when the map lets the Operator **establish a firing position and
profit from staying there**.

For a blind story run, I prefer something less dependent on predicting the map.

<h2 id="weapon-class">
  I choose weapon class from range and AP, not the specialization name
</h2>

The opening creator separates specialization from weapon class.

The four weapon classes I can choose from are:

**Blaster Rifle**

**Repeater**

**Blaster Pistol**

**Longarm Blaster**

That separation is one of the most useful build rules in the game.

A Gunslinger does not have to use a pistol.

A Sharpshooter does not have to carry the longest-range option.

The weapon needs to fit what I expect that Operator to do with their **three
Action Points**, not what looks thematically correct beside the class name.

My Gunslinger test is the clearest example.

The pistol costs only **1 AP to fire**, which is fantastic if I can operate at
its preferred range.

But it is also marked **Short** range.

If getting that short-range shot requires me to spend too much movement or
leave good cover, the cheap firing cost stops being as valuable.

A Longarm setup creates the opposite problem: the better reach is useful, but a
two-AP firing pattern becomes expensive whenever the mission forces me to move.

The Blaster Rifle is the middle ground I like for Hawks because it gives me
enough reach without building every turn around one static firing lane.

I pick the weapon after asking:

> **Where will this Operator normally stand, and how many AP will they have left
> when they get there?**

That question has been much more reliable than matching "Gunslinger" with
"Pistol" or "Sharpshooter" with "Longarm."

<h2 id="locked-talents">
  Locked talents matter more to me than forcing one class forever
</h2>

Once I start spending Focus Points, I stop evaluating Operators by
specialization alone.

Named Operators have talents that remain central to the way I use them.

Hawks' **Fearless Leader**, for example, can improve the rest of Zero Company
instead of forcing Hawks into a particular weapon or damage role.

Kabb's Uppercut gives me a genuine reason to preserve a melee identity around
him.

Trick's unique talent can automatically bring him back after being downed,
which changes how comfortable I am letting him take certain risks.

Those abilities give me a foundation.

The specialization builds around that foundation.

So if I expect to move Hawks from Gunslinger into Scout later, I do not panic
about having "ruined" the character.

I keep investing in strengths I know will remain useful and change the role
when the campaign gives me a reason.

The basic Focus and Bond systems are covered in my
<Link href="/star-wars-zero-company/beginner-guide">
  {" "}Zero Company beginner guide
</Link>
. Here, I only care about what they mean for the build:

**I spend points around what the Operator repeatedly does in combat, not around
the class name I picked several Cycles ago.**

<h2 id="cross-training">
  Cross Training is where I make an Operator's role more permanent
</h2>

Cross Training matters more to my long-term build than trying to make the
starting specialization perfect.

As Bonds improve, two Operators can receive a permanent bonus together.

The actual choices vary by pairing.

I have seen options including:

- **+1 Critical Damage**
- **+2% Critical Chance**
- **+3% Critical Chance**
- **+1 Range Damage**
- **+1 Melee Damage**
- **+1 Overwatch Damage**
- **+4 Max Health**
- **+0.5m First Movement**
- **+2 Focus Points**

I do not pick whichever bonus has the most exciting number.

I use the pairing to reinforce what those two Operators already do.

For a pair that spends most of its turns shooting, I am happy to take
**+1 Range Damage** when that option appears.

I used that kind of choice on ranged Operators because the bonus improves what
I already ask them to do every Mission.

For Kabb, **+1 Melee Damage** makes far more sense when I am actively building
around Uppercut.

I do not give a melee character ranged damage just to make the stat sheet look
balanced.

And I do not give a ranged Operator melee damage because maybe one day they
will need to punch something.

Cross Training is where I stop trying to make every Operator average at
everything.

I use it to make a defined role better.

<h2 id="my-first-run-picks">
  The specialization roles I would use on another first run
</h2>

If I started another campaign without knowing which map was coming next, I
would not try to field all eight specializations.

I would build around jobs.

For **Hawks**, I would start with **Gunslinger + Blaster Rifle**.

If Hawks keeps spending turns pushing forward and exploiting exposed enemies,
I would move toward **Assault**.

If the squad already has enough finishers and needs Advantage, Spot, and better
target setup, I would use **Scout**.

If I want Hawks creating Vulnerable targets and building attacks for other
Operators, I would consider **Scoundrel**.

I want **Medic** somewhere in the active roster because the free Medpack,
Combat Stim, and full-health Rally provide a kind of safety that another pure
damage class does not replace.

I use **Heavy** when somebody needs to deliberately hold enemy attention.

I use **Sharpshooter** when the Operator can spend several turns working from a
stable long-range position.

And I keep **Soldier** when I am genuinely using its blaster, grenade, melee,
and explosive flexibility rather than leaving half the skill tree idle.

The opening choice matters because it shapes my first missions.

It does not trap the campaign.

I start with the role that solves my current problem, watch how that Operator
actually spends AP over several fights, and then let specialization, weapon
class, locked talents, Focus Points, and Cross Training converge on the role I
am really playing.
