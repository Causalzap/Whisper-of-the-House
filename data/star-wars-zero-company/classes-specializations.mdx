<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
For Hawks, **Scout is the specialization I would keep on a first campaign once Personnel opens**. Assess Threats feeds the squad with Advantage, Scout can Spot targets, and later kills can generate more Advantage, so Hawks keeps helping even when somebody else takes the finishing shot.

If I want Hawks dealing more direct ranged damage, **Gunslinger + Blaster Rifle** is my simpler alternative.

For the rest of the early squad, I like **Kabb as Heavy** and **Trick as Medic**. Jay can work as **Scoundrel** when I want Vulnerable and assist setup, or **Sharpshooter** when I care more about reliable ranged pressure.

The opening choice is not permanent. I can change an Operator's specialization later, and changing it refunds the points already spent in that specialization.

<h2 id="best-starting-specialization">
  What I would pick for Hawks on a first run
</h2>

There are **eight** specializations in *STAR WARS Zero Company*:

**Assault, Gunslinger, Heavy, Medic, Soldier, Sharpshooter, Scout, and
Scoundrel.**

I started out interested in Scoundrel because Vulnerable and assists fit Hawks'
role as the captain. Once I understood how often the whole squad wants
Advantage, though, I moved toward **Scout**.

That is now the Hawks setup I find easiest to justify over a long campaign.

**Assess Threats** can generate Advantage from enemies caught in its area.
Scout can also develop a **20% chance to gain one Advantage after defeating an
enemy**, while blaster damage can **Spot** the target.

The important part is that Advantage is not just a Scout resource. Other
Operators want it for some of their strongest abilities.

That means Hawks can spend a turn setting up the board instead of competing
with the rest of the squad for every kill.

A typical Scout turn can give me more value than its raw damage suggests:

**reveal or assess several enemies → build Advantage → Spot the target I care
about → let another Operator spend the opening.**

That became more useful as the roster grew.

Kabb can finish enemies at close range. A ranged Operator can capitalize on a
better target. A Jedi has expensive abilities worth feeding. Once I have those
pieces, I would rather let Hawks make the squad more efficient than force the
captain to be the main damage dealer.

Scout is therefore my **best all-round Hawks specialization**, not because it
wins every damage comparison, but because its useful turns are hard to waste.

If I want something more straightforward, I fall back to **Gunslinger**.

<h2 id="can-you-change-specialization">
  Yes, I change specializations instead of restarting the campaign
</h2>

The opening class screen looks more permanent than it really is.

Once Personnel becomes available, I can change an Operator's specialization.
When I changed roles during the campaign, the points already committed to the
old specialization were refunded rather than lost.

This is why I would **not restart because I regret Hawks' opening
specialization**.

My first Hawks moved from **Scoundrel to Scout** after I understood Advantage
better.

I also changed companion roles after seeing how their unique talents worked in
actual fights.

The point where I reconsider a specialization is usually obvious: several
abilities have been sitting unused for multiple missions while the Operator
keeps solving the same problem another way.

At that point I change the role.

I would rather concentrate Focus Points into abilities I use every mission
than keep a theoretically flexible build whose best branches never leave the
menu.

<h2 id="best-operator-specializations">
  Kabb, Trick, and Jay changed how I think about the eight classes
</h2>

The eight specializations make much more sense once I stop trying to rank them
in isolation.

A class that feels unnecessary on Hawks can be exactly what another Operator
needs.

### Kabb makes Heavy feel natural

Kabb is the clearest example.

I originally had more damage-oriented options around him, but **Heavy** fits
what I already want Kabb doing: move into the dangerous part of the fight,
pull attention away from the back line, and punish enemies that engage him.

The Heavy setup I tested immediately gave useful durability:

* **+2 Defense**
* **+4 Health**
* basic blaster attacks that can Taunt
* **Bulwark** for area Taunt and more Defense
* **Unstoppable** for retaliation

That pairs much better with Kabb's existing melee identity than trying to turn
him into another generic ranged attacker.

His **Uppercut** already gives me a reason to bring him close.

Heavy gives the rest of that plan a purpose.

Instead of asking whether Heavy is a good Hawks class, I ask whether the squad
already has somebody willing to stand where Heavy wants to stand.

With Kabb available, the answer is usually yes.

### Trick is the Medic I did not expect to keep

Medic was always a role I wanted somewhere in the squad.

The surprising part was deciding that **Trick** could carry it.

Trick initially looks comfortable in a more offensive role, but his unique
survivability changes the calculation. When he goes down, his recovery talent
can bring him back immediately and recover an Action Point, and his first
downing can be protected from causing an Injury.

That makes him a very forgiving support character.

Medic then adds the tools I already want nearby when something goes wrong.

The setup I used gives a **Medpack that does not occupy the normal utility
slot**, with healing available **twice per battle**.

**Combat Stim** costs **1 AP** and applies Stimulant for **2 turns**, increasing:

* Max Health
* Melee Damage
* Ranged Damage

I use it before an important attack sequence just as often as I use it to
protect somebody who is already hurt.

**First Responder** also makes Rally much stronger by bringing the rescued
Operator back **Fully Healed**.

That does not erase the campaign's Injury system. Downing an Operator can still
matter after the fight.

It does mean I am far less likely to lose control of the current battle.

For difficult missions, I would rather have one dependable Medic than squeeze
another small damage increase out of the fourth squad slot.

### Jay can go in two genuinely useful directions

Jay gave me the closest call between **Scoundrel** and **Sharpshooter**.

Her own kit already rewards ranged attacks and finishing weakened enemies, so
Sharpshooter is an obvious fit. Better hit chances and stronger blaster
pressure reinforce something she is already good at.

But I also like Scoundrel on her because it adds something the rest of the
squad can use immediately.

**Identify Weakness** applies **Vulnerable**.

Instead of asking Jay to take every important shot herself, I can have her open
a target for whoever has the best follow-up.

That is especially useful against one high-health enemy that I want removed
before its next turn.

I lean **Scoundrel** when the squad needs setup and assists.

I lean **Sharpshooter** when I already have enough support and just want Jay
landing ranged attacks as consistently as possible.

Neither choice changes what makes Jay unique. Her personal talent remains the
reason the build works.

<h2 id="gunslinger">
  Gunslinger is still my simplest damage-focused Hawks
</h2>

Scout asks me to think about the next Operator.

Gunslinger is simpler: Hawks gets a good firing lane and starts contributing
damage.

**Keen Eye** begins with **+6% ranged critical chance**, while **Quick Draw**
gives me another way to fit an attack into a turn without always committing to
a more expensive firing action.

That matters because many missions do not let Hawks spend every turn standing
behind the same piece of cover.

I may need to:

* advance toward an objective;
* interact with something;
* rotate away from reinforcements;
* reach an extraction zone;
* or move far enough to regain a useful angle.

I like Gunslinger in those situations because it does not need an elaborate
setup before it starts contributing.

The one thing I would not do is automatically pair it with a Blaster Pistol
just because the names sound right together.

Specialization and weapon class are separate decisions.

The pistol setup I tested has **6 Weapon Damage** and **10 Melee Damage**.

The 10 is not the gun's base ranged damage.

Its real advantage is the **1 AP firing cost**. If I am already at the correct
distance, that cheap shot can make a turn feel excellent.

The problem is reaching that distance without giving up the cover or movement
I wanted.

I had stretches where several displayed **50–70% shots** failed. I do not blame
the weapon for probability doing what probability does, but those turns made
the range problem obvious: short-range shots were forcing Hawks to work too
hard for the angle.

That is why I prefer a **Blaster Rifle** for a blind Gunslinger run.

It gives me more comfortable reach without turning every attack into the kind
of stationary commitment I associate with a dedicated long-range setup.

<h2 id="scoundrel">
  Scoundrel is better when one dangerous enemy needs to disappear
</h2>

Scoundrel was my original Hawks choice, and I still like what it does.

I just no longer need Hawks to stay there for the whole campaign.

**Easy Mark** helps prepare an enemy for an ally's attack.

**Identify Weakness** applies **Vulnerable**.

Those abilities become much more valuable when one target is more dangerous
than everything standing around it.

Instead of spreading mediocre attacks across a group, I can start with Hawks,
open the priority target, and let the Operator with the best follow-up finish
the sequence.

This is the practical difference between Scout and Scoundrel for me.

**Scout** improves the squad's broader turn economy through Advantage and Spot.

**Scoundrel** is more direct when I already know exactly which enemy has to
die.

I would happily return Hawks to Scoundrel for a squad built heavily around
assists, Vulnerable, and focused bursts.

I just find Scout easier to use across more mission types.

<h2 id="assault-soldier-sharpshooter">
  Assault, Soldier, and Sharpshooter depend much more on the map
</h2>

These three are not weak.

They are simply easier for me to judge after I know what I actually ask an
Operator to do.

### Assault pays me for moving forward

Assault makes sense when Hawks is repeatedly advancing instead of setting up
the squad from behind.

The opening abilities I tested included:

**Fleet of Foot — +1.0m First Movement**

and

**Strike the Flanks — +20% Critical Chance against Exposed enemies.**

That creates a natural rhythm on objective-heavy maps:

**advance → break or bypass cover → attack the exposed target → keep moving.**

Assault also has **Bull Rush** for pushing an enemy and **Sweeping Salvo** for
a larger cone attack.

The Sweeping Salvo setup I used cost **4 Advantage**.

Four is expensive enough that I think about it before spending, but it is still
a realistic squad resource rather than an ability I need to save for all
mission.

When Hawks keeps reaching the fight before everyone else, Assault becomes much
more attractive.

When Hawks spends most turns standing back and coordinating the squad, I would
rather be Scout or Gunslinger.

### Soldier looked better to me on the tree than it did in my turns

Soldier has one of the broadest toolkits.

The setup I inspected could improve **Blaster Damage and Grenade Damage by 2**,
support melee, and work toward **Rocket Strike**.

That breadth is useful only if I actually use it.

When Hawks was mostly firing from range, too much of the tree was waiting for a
grenade, melee opportunity, or explosive sequence that never came.

Soldier becomes much better if I genuinely want one Operator doing all of
those things.

I would not choose it just because it appears to cover every situation.

A flexible class can still waste points if my own playstyle is not flexible.

### Sharpshooter wants time and a firing position

Sharpshooter has the clearest answer to bad accuracy.

One setup gives **3 stacks of Lethal**, worth:

**+15% chance to hit**

and

**+9% critical chance**.

That is excellent when the mission lets me claim good cover and stay there.

The cost becomes obvious on objectives that keep moving.

A long-range shot can require **2 AP**. If I just spent most of the turn
crossing the map, reaching an objective, or escaping reinforcements, those two
AP may be exactly what I do not have.

Sharpshooter therefore gets better the more predictable the battlefield is.

I like it on an Operator who can establish a lane and keep firing.

I do not make it my automatic first Hawks choice when I have no idea what the
next mission will demand.

<h2 id="weapon-class">
  The weapon has to fit the turn, not the class name
</h2>

The four weapon classes available from the opening are:

**Blaster Rifle**

**Repeater**

**Blaster Pistol**

**Longarm Blaster**

The useful question is not which weapon visually belongs to a specialization.

It is:

> **Where will this Operator usually stand, and how many AP will remain once
> they get there?**

The Blaster Pistol's **1 AP** shot is valuable because it leaves room for
movement and follow-up actions, but its short range can make me spend those
savings just getting into position.

A longer-range setup solves that problem from the opposite direction, but a
**2 AP** attack becomes awkward if the mission keeps forcing movement.

The Blaster Rifle is the middle ground I am most comfortable putting on Hawks.

That is also why I do not lock every Sharpshooter into the longest weapon or
every Gunslinger into a pistol.

I build around the turns I actually expect them to take.

<h2 id="locked-talents">
  The unique talent is what keeps an Operator from becoming a blank class slot
</h2>

Specializations can move around.

Named Operator talents are the part I keep coming back to.

Hawks has **Fearless Leader**, which can strengthen the rest of Zero Company
instead of forcing the captain toward one specific damage role.

Kabb's **Uppercut** gives him a reason to stay comfortable near enemies.

Trick's recovery talent changes how much risk I am willing to put on him and
helps explain why Medic worked better than I first expected.

Other named Operators bring their own mechanics as well.

That distinction keeps me from asking the wrong question.

I am not trying to find the one best class and copy it onto every character.

I am trying to put a specialization around a talent that already gives that
Operator something worth building around.

<h2 id="cross-training">
  Cross Training is where I start committing to the role
</h2>

I am relaxed about the opening specialization because later progression gives
me better places to make permanent-looking decisions.

Cross Training is one of them.

As Bonds improve, paired Operators can choose bonuses together. The available
options depend on the pairing.

I have seen choices including:

* **+1 Critical Damage**
* **+2% or +3% Critical Chance**
* **+1 Range Damage**
* **+1 Melee Damage**
* **+1 Overwatch Damage**
* **+4 Max Health**
* **+0.5m First Movement**
* **+2 Focus Points**

This is where I stop trying to keep everyone balanced.

If two Operators spend most of the campaign firing from range, **+1 Range
Damage** is easy to justify.

If I am leaning into Kabb's Uppercut and Heavy role, **Melee Damage** reinforces
something I already use instead of preparing for a hypothetical ranged build.

Overwatch bonuses are also much more attractive than they first looked.
Repeated reinforcement waves made Overwatch useful often enough that I no
longer treat it as a backup action I take only when nothing else fits.

I use Cross Training to sharpen an identity, not erase one.

<h2 id="best-first-run-squad">
  The first-run roles I would build around now
</h2>

If I restarted the campaign, I would not try to cover all eight
specializations just because all eight exist.

I would make sure the squad can solve four jobs.

For **Hawks**, I would use **Scout** as the general-purpose leader. Advantage
and Spot remain useful whether Hawks takes the final shot or not.

For **Kabb**, I would use **Heavy**. His melee kit already wants him closer to
danger, and Taunt, Defense, Health, and retaliation give that position a job.

For **Trick**, I would use **Medic**. His recovery talent gives the support slot
better survivability, while Medpacks and Combat Stim keep the rest of the squad
working.

For **Jay**, I would start with **Scoundrel** if the squad still needs
Vulnerable and assist setup. If Hawks is already handling enough support, I
would move her toward **Sharpshooter** and let her concentrate on ranged
pressure.

That still leaves room to change things.

If Hawks needs more personal damage, I can move to **Gunslinger**.

If the campaign starts demanding constant forward movement, **Assault** becomes
more appealing.

If I am genuinely rotating between blasters, grenades, melee, and explosive
abilities, **Soldier** finally earns its flexibility.

And on a mission where I can hold a lane instead of chasing the objective,
**Sharpshooter** gets much easier to exploit.

The useful part of Zero Company's class system is not finding one answer on
the character-creation screen.

It is realizing that the answer can change once I know the Operator, the rest
of the squad, and the kind of turns the campaign keeps asking me to take.
