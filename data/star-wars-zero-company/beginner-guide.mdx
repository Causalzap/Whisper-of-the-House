<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Before I commit to the next job, I check **Critical status and expiry first**.

In my opening campaign, **Operations did not advance the Cycle; Tactical
Missions did**. That matters when an Operator is healing or an upgrade is under
construction, because running more Operations does not move those timers
forward.

I treat serious injuries early, keep at least one useful backup Operator, check

a threshold. In combat, I protect positioning before chasing marginal shots,

Advantage belongs to the whole squad.

<h2 id="plan-each-cycle">
  How I decide what deserves the next Cycle
</h2>

The first habit I had to unlearn in *Zero Company* was trying to clear every
icon that appeared on the Holotable.

The campaign regularly gives me more work than I can comfortably fit into one
route.

Missions and Operations can have limited windows, Critical objectives move the
story forward, and new work can appear while older opportunities are still
waiting.

So I read the map in this order:

**Is it Critical?**

**How soon does it expire?**

**Do I have the Intel and healthy Operators to handle it now?**

Only after those three questions do I compare rewards.

If one job expires in one Cycle while another still has two, the shorter timer
usually wins. If both are safe, then Credits, Influence, equipment, Bonds, or a
specific roster need can break the tie.

That has been more reliable than simply selecting whichever card shows the
largest reward.

I also stop before launching a Tactical Mission if I have an injured Operator
in treatment or an upgrade being built. Those timers interact with the same
Cycle system, so advancing the campaign can also finish something back at the
Den.

<div className="my-6 rounded-xl border border-slate-700/70 bg-slate-900/40 p-5">
  <strong>I plan from the timer outward.</strong> The number beside an
  opportunity tells me how long I can leave it, not how important its reward
  is. I protect expiring Critical progress first, then spend the remaining room
  on optional work.
</div>

<h2 id="operations-and-cycles">
  Operations do not move time the way Tactical Missions do
</h2>

This was one of the least obvious systems during my first few Cycles.

**Operations** are resolved from the Holotable. I spend Intel, make a decision
when one is offered, collect the result, and stay on the strategic layer.

One early Operation cost me **50 Intel** when I only had **100 Intel** available.

That immediately made me more selective. Spending half my pool on one optional
result can leave me unable to execute another Operation that matters more.

The more important discovery came while I was waiting on Den timers.

I executed Operations and noticed that the **Cycle had not advanced**.

In the opening campaign I played, Tactical Missions moved the Cycle forward;
Operations did not.

That distinction affects anything waiting on campaign time, including treatment
and construction, so I check those timers before choosing another Operation.

For the detailed MedBay recovery rules, I use my
<Link href="/star-wars-zero-company/permadeath-injuries">
  {" "}Permadeath and injuries guide
</Link>
.

Several early Tactical Missions also returned **100 Intel**, so I naturally
fell into a useful rhythm:

**spend Intel on worthwhile Operations → launch the Tactical Mission I actually
want to advance → refill Intel → reassess the next Cycle.**

I leave Operations alone when their timers give me room rather than spending
Intel simply because I can.

<h2 id="injuries-medbay">
  I check injuries before choosing the next Mission
</h2>

After a Mission where somebody goes Downed or comes back injured, I check the
roster before committing the next job.

An injured Operator may be weaker and can eventually become a permanent loss,
so I prefer rotating in a healthy replacement when the schedule gives me room.

Injuries can also come from risky **Operations**, which means leaving somebody
out of the combat squad does not automatically remove them from danger.

My
<Link href="/star-wars-zero-company/permadeath-injuries">
  {" "}Permadeath and injuries guide
</Link>
covers the full Downed, Rally, Operation risk, MedBay, treatment, Bacta Tank,
and permanent-death rules.

For a first run, the habit I keep is simple: **check the injured roster before
spending the next Cycle.**

<h2 id="influence-rewards">
  I check the Influence track when a Zone crosses a threshold
</h2>

Influence is easy to treat like background reputation until the rewards start
changing the company itself.

Missions and Operations increase Influence in their respective Zones.

When a threshold is reached, I open the **Influence tab** and see what that Zone
has unlocked.

One early Zone Aurek track gave me:

**20 Influence — Crew Quarters: +1 Roster Capacity**

**30 Influence — Extra Padding: +4 Max Health for all Operators**

**50 Influence — Medpac Mk. IV**

Those rewards change real decisions.

An extra roster slot lets me carry another backup.

Four Max Health across every Operator gives me far more long-term value than a
small one-off payout.

A stronger Medpac changes how aggressively I can handle later Tactical
Missions.

After a Mission pushes a Zone across a visible threshold, I check the
Influence screen before I forget about it.

I am not trying to turn every Cycle into an Influence farm. I simply do not
want useful company-wide progression sitting unnoticed while I spend Credits
solving a problem the Zone track could already help with.

That makes the first hires more important than simply picking whichever portrait
or damage ability I like most.

I want **role overlap without role duplication**.

If my usual healer is unavailable, somebody else needs to keep the squad alive.

If my close-range Operator is badly injured, I want another character who can
work around the objective without forcing that damaged Operator back into a
Critical Mission.

The campaign later gives me another reason not to rely on one fixed lineup:
story events can temporarily change who is available.

So I think about Zero Company as a roster rather than a four-character party.

I still care about good Specializations and weapon pairings, but those choices
have more flexibility than the opening screens suggest. I keep the detailed
role and build decisions in my

  {" "}classes and specializations guide
</Link>
.

For a first run, a reliable replacement is often more useful than squeezing one
more damage-focused Operator into the same role.

<h2 id="den-conversations">
  I make a lap around the Den after important Missions
</h2>

The Den is not just a set of menus placed inside a walkable ship.

Crew conversations can move relationships forward and open new work.

One of the clearest early examples came after I returned from a Mission and
checked on Trick.

A normal conversation turned into a distress call from **Sawtooth**, someone
from the old unit whose position had collapsed under Separatist pressure.

That conversation opened another thread of work.

I would have missed it by going straight from the results screen back to the
Holotable.

After a major Mission or a new Cycle, I usually do a quick loop:

**check injuries → look at new facility notifications → speak to crew with new
dialogue → return to the Holotable.**

There is no need to exhaust every ambient line after every small Operation.

When the story has just moved, though, I assume the Den may have changed too.

That habit keeps paying off later as personal stories and Operator availability
become more important.

<h2 id="explore-before-combat">
  I check useful interactions before triggering the next fight
</h2>

Free movement between tactical encounters is not just downtime.

Hawks can inspect parts of the environment, and some conversations or
interactions give resources that carry directly into the next part of the
Mission.

During one early exploration section, I picked up **+3 Advantage** from an
interaction.

Another gave me **+10 Intel**.

The tutorial also introduces Hawks' electroscope as something worth using on
interesting objects because those checks can provide Intel or an advantage for
the next battle.

That changes how I move through non-combat spaces.

I am not searching every corner for ten minutes before touching the objective.
When an NPC, console, device, or obvious interaction sits directly along my
route, I check it before crossing the next combat trigger.

Three extra Advantage can mean an important ability is available earlier.

Ten free Intel can be the difference between executing the next Operation and
having to leave it on the Holotable.

That is enough value for me to spend a few seconds looking around.

<h2 id="bonds-focus">
  I check Bonds and Focus Points without turning every Cycle into build maintenance
</h2>

Bonds are more than a relationship meter.

As Operators grow closer, they can earn Focus Points and eventually unlock
**Cross Training**, which gives both characters a permanent improvement.

On a first run, I let the pairings grow around Operators I actually need in the
field, then periodically check Personnel for new training.

The exact bonus is a build decision.

Some pairings can lean toward ranged damage, critical stats, Overwatch, melee,
movement, Focus Points, or other improvements. I prefer reinforcing the job an
Operator already performs instead of choosing a stat simply because its number
looks large.

I handle those choices in my
<Link href="/star-wars-zero-company/classes-specializations">
  {" "}classes and specializations guide
</Link>
.

The beginner habit is simpler:

**I spend earned Focus Points and check available Cross Training before several
Missions pass without using them.**

Focus Points are Operator progression, not a tactical consumable I need to save
for some unknown late-game emergency.

I spend them on abilities I am already using, then reconsider the
Specialization if that role eventually stops fitting.

  I spend early upgrades on problems that keep coming back

Construction takes at least **one Cycle** and requires an available build slot.

Early on, I only had one build slot.

That makes the first upgrade an opportunity-cost decision.

If Operators keep returning injured, treatment capacity has immediate value.

If my roster is too shallow, Recruitment and capacity improvements matter more.

If those systems are already comfortable, roster-wide weapon bonuses or other
combat upgrades become easier to justify.

I spend the first build slot on whichever recurring problem is already costing
me the most flexibility.

The same applies to shopping.

I buy a heal when I need Health.

An Injury is a different problem, so I handle that through the appropriate
recovery system rather than assuming another healing item will solve everything.

I also refuse to distort all of my early spending around the enormous Hutt debt
number. That chapter has its own progression solution, which I keep in my

<h2 id="action-economy">
  I plan the whole turn before spending the first Action Point
</h2>

Most Operators start a turn with **three Action Points**.

Three sounds like a lot until I spend two moving and discover that the attack I
wanted also costs two.

Movement can consume one, two, or all three AP depending on distance, and
weapons do not all use the same amount.

Before I confirm a long move, I ask:

> **What will this Operator still be able to do after arriving there?**

That question saves me from a lot of bad positions.

If a move leaves me exposed and without enough AP for the attack I planned, I
either shorten the move, use different cover, or let somebody else act first.

I would rather finish a turn one piece of cover short of the enemy than arrive
at the perfect range with no useful action remaining.

The other easy value is **Call for Backup**.

Each turn, one Operator can Call for Backup from a teammate, and the assisting
Operator automatically joins the next attack.

Because the call itself is a **free action**, I use it whenever concentrating
two Operators on one dangerous target gives me better value than spreading
damage across several enemies.

<h2 id="advantage-overwatch">
  I treat Advantage as a squad resource and positioning as protection against bad rolls
</h2>

Special abilities can cost **Advantage** instead of Action Points.

The squad gains Advantage as it damages enemies, and the pool is shared.

That last part changes how I spend it.

If Hawks burns most of the pool, another Operator does not get a separate
supply.

Before pressing an expensive ability simply because it is ready, I check
whether somebody else can change the fight more with those same points.

I like spending the pool on something that changes the board:

- removing a dangerous target;
- breaking a defensive position;
- controlling several enemies;
- preventing a bad next turn.

I think about Overwatch the same way.

When reinforcements clearly have to enter through a door or lane, I would
rather leave several Operators covering that route than spend every last AP on
weak shots.

Against enemy Overwatch, I look for a safer Operator who can damage the watcher
first instead of automatically running the marked character through the danger
zone.

Displayed hit chance also never becomes my entire plan.

I have watched a **90% shot miss**.

I have also had stretches where several **50–70%** attempts failed in a row.

The percentages still matter. What matters just as much is whether the board
remains safe after the roll goes against me.

If a mediocre shot requires me to abandon strong cover, I look for another
answer first:

move for a better angle, break cover, Spot the target, use an Assist, choose a
different exposed enemy, or prepare Overwatch.

One miss rarely ruins the turn by itself.

The worse outcome is leaving the whole squad exposed because I planned as if
the shot could not fail.

<h2 id="first-run-routine">
  The routine I settle into between major Missions
</h2>

Once the systems stop feeling new, I stop treating the Den like a dozen
unrelated menus.

I come back from a Tactical Mission and check the roster first.

Then I look for any Zone threshold I just crossed, spend useful Focus Points,
and see whether a familiar Bond pair has new Cross Training.

I make a quick pass through the Den for new conversations and only visit
Recruitment when I actually have a roster gap worth solving.

Then I open the Holotable.

At that point I know who is available, what the company gained, and which
opportunities are about to disappear.

My next decision usually comes down to:

**Critical status → expiry → available squad → Intel → reward.**

Once I know which story objective I am aiming for, I use the
<Link href="/star-wars-zero-company/walkthrough">
  {" "}Zero Company walkthrough
</Link>
for the campaign order and keep this page focused on getting the company to
that next Mission in good shape.
