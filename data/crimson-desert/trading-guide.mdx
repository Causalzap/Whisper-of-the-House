<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
I make money trading in Crimson Desert by finding the **buyer before I fill the
wagon**.

I check the current price for the packaged good I want to move, find where I can
actually sell it, and then build one route around several useful stops.

I do **not** use one permanent "best trade route." Prices can change, so a route
that looked excellent on one run may not deserve the same wagon space later.

One of my runs included **150 Packaged Cheese bought at 118 each for 17,700**,
bringing my total Cheese load to **186**. On the same broader trading run, I
later sold **150 Packaged Salt at 563 each for 84,450**.

My rule is:

**find the buyer → compare the spread → check the detour → load the wagon → stop
changing the plan once the trip is good enough**
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="how-trading-works">
  I decide where the cargo is going before I load it
</h2>

Trading became much easier once I stopped treating Packaged Trade Goods like
ordinary vendor loot.

The important part is not simply finding something cheap.

I need somewhere worthwhile to take it.

Packaged Trade Goods can be transported on a horse or wagon and delivered to a
Trading Post. The early trading tutorial also warns that recalling a horse
carrying packaged goods from a distance can cost me some of that cargo.

My early mistake was doing the opposite.

I would see a discounted product and think:

**That is cheap, so I should buy it.**

Then I would load it and only afterward start looking for a buyer.

That left me with too many half-planned trades.

Now the first question I ask is:

**Who is going to buy this, and does that destination fit the trip I am willing
to make?**

<h2 id="check-buyer-first">
  I find the selling destination before I spend the Silver
</h2>

A low buy price is only half of a trade.

If the useful buyer is far away, in the wrong direction, or behind a route I do
not want to drive, the discount may not be worth much to me.

So I open the Trade Map and check the exact good before I buy.

If I am considering Cheese, I check Cheese.

If Salt looks discounted, I look for the Salt buyer.

If Ceramic is already on my wagon, I check whether its buyer fits the route I
am already building.

The Cheese run is the clearest example.

I found a **Royal Trading Post in Varnia** showing **1,000 per Packaged Cheese**
during that run.

That gave the Cheese a destination before I spent anything.

The useful habit is checking the current destination before buying.

I rebuild the decision from the current prices.

<h2 id="build-one-route">
  I care more about the whole wagon trip than one perfect percentage
</h2>

Once Cheese gave me a reason to travel toward Varnia, I checked what else could
make money without tearing the route apart.

During that planning session I was comparing goods such as Packaged Salt,
Packaged Ceramic, Ceremonial Armor, Packaged Sugar, and the Cheese I already
wanted.

Some Armor and Ceramic opportunities were showing spreads around the **48–52%**
range.

That looked excellent.

But I still did not choose them only because the percentage was high.

The useful question was:

**Can I unload this somewhere I am already passing?**

That is how I turn one long wagon journey into several sales.

I load goods whose destinations fit together, unload intermediate cargo when I
reach the right Trading Post, and keep the farthest load for the later leg.

A 50% opportunity that sends me in the wrong direction can be worse than a
smaller trade sitting almost directly on my road.

The route is the product.

The individual good is only one part of it.

---

<h2 id="when-i-take-a-detour">
  A higher price is not automatically worth the detour
</h2>

I ran into this with Ceremonial Armor.

One option was around:

**36%**

Another was close to:

**52%**

The obvious reaction is:

**take 52%.**

I do not make that decision until I look at the route.

If the stronger buyer is a short deviation from the road I am already taking,
the extra margin is easy to justify.

If reaching it means reversing direction, taking bad terrain, or extending the
dangerous section of an already expensive trip, the calculation changes.

I ask:

**How much extra profit am I getting for the extra distance and risk?**

That is much more useful than asking:

**Which Trading Post has the biggest percentage?**

The more Silver I already have sitting on the wagon, the less interested I am
in making the trip complicated for a marginal improvement.

<h2 id="buy-sell-confusion">
  I verify whether the Trading Post is buying or selling before I change the route
</h2>

One surprisingly expensive mistake was reading the right number on the wrong
side of the transaction.

I would open a Trading Post, see an attractive percentage, and immediately start
working it into the route.

Then I would realize:

**the merchant was buying the product, not selling it.**

I ran into this while checking Ceramic with an already busy wagon.

For a moment I thought I had found another purchase.

I had not.

Now, before adding anything, I stop and answer two separate questions:

**Can I buy the product here?**

Then:

**Where am I selling it?**

If I cannot answer both, the trade is not ready.

This matters more once I am carrying several goods because I am moving quickly
between filters, Trading Posts, buy screens, and sell screens.

I would rather spend a few seconds checking the transaction than build another
detour around a product I cannot even buy there.

<h2 id="when-i-stop-buying">
  I stop loading the wagon before every slot is full
</h2>

Empty wagon space used to bother me.

If there was room left, I felt like I should find another profitable good.

That is how a clean route turns into a mess.

Once I already have Cheese with a destination, Salt with a buyer, and other
cargo that fits the same general journey, I do not need another product just
because one more slot exists.

There will always be another Trading Post.

There will always be another green percentage.

There will always be another possible detour.

At some point I need to stop planning and start driving.

I leave a trade behind when I cannot identify the buyer quickly, when its buyer
pulls me away from the rest of the route, when the extra profit is too small for
another stop, or when the wagon is already carrying enough value that I would
rather reduce risk than add more.

A wagon does not need to be completely full to be a good run.

It needs to contain trades I actually understand.

<h2 id="wagon-route-risk">
  Once the wagon is expensive, the safest road can be the profitable road
</h2>

Some of my worst trading decisions had nothing to do with prices.

They came from looking at the map and thinking:

**That shortcut is faster.**

Then I took a loaded wagon off the useful road and ran into rocks, slopes,
narrow passages, hostile territory, ranged enemies, or terrain that made the
wagon difficult to move.

Once the wagon was in bad terrain, I was no longer thinking about the market.

I was thinking about getting stuck.

I was watching enemies.

I was trying not to lose the wagon.

That makes the road itself part of the trade.

A route that is slightly longer but keeps me moving reliably can be better than
the shortest possible line.

The same applies when enemies appear.

If I am travelling normally, I may stop and fight.

With a valuable trade load, I usually keep moving when the road is open.

Winning a random fight does not improve the sale waiting at the next Trading
Post.

I would rather unload first and return without thousands of Silver tied up
behind me.

<h2 id="discover-trading-posts">
  I discover new Trading Posts when they are already on the way
</h2>

A small detour can still be worth taking when it improves my future information.

If I pass an undiscovered Trading Post while following a route I already chose,
I normally stop and interact with it.

I may not buy or sell anything there.

The current prices may be useless.

That is fine.

What I gain is another Trading Post I can check when I plan later routes.

The more posts I have discovered, the less often I am making decisions from an
incomplete market picture.

I do not turn an expensive loaded wagon around and cross half the region just to
discover one trader.

But if the post is already beside my route, I take the information.

<h2 id="trading-and-progression">
  I make another trading run when the Silver has a job waiting for it
</h2>

I can keep running wagons long after I have enough money for my immediate needs.

That does not mean I should.

Trading feels most useful when I already know why I want the Silver.

Sometimes I want another expensive purchase.

Sometimes camp progression is waiting on resources.

Sometimes I know I am about to spend heavily and want a larger buffer before I
do it.

That is when I make another run.

<h2 id="my-trading-loop">
  The trading loop I use now
</h2>

I no longer start at the nearest merchant.

I start with the Trade Map.

I choose a packaged good that looks promising and find the buyer I would
actually travel to.

Then I compare the current buy price, the expected sale, and the distance.

If the trade still looks good, I check whether another product can share the
same journey without pulling the wagon in a different direction.

Once I have enough useful cargo, I stop optimizing.

I load the wagon and leave.

I unload intermediate goods when their Trading Posts appear.

I keep the farthest cargo for the later leg.

I stay on a route I can drive reliably instead of risking the whole load for a
small shortcut.

And when another tempting price appears after the wagon is already carrying a
good trip, I am willing to say:

**next run.**

That is the change that made Crimson Desert trading work for me:

**I stopped hunting for one permanent best trade good and started building the
best trip I could make from the prices in front of me.**
