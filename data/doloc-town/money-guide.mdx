I make money in Doloc Town much more consistently when I stop looking for one
"best money method" and instead protect my cash from bad early purchases.

The game constantly gives me reasons to spend Gold before my income is stable:
seeds, bag space, tool upgrades, weapons, fishing gear, buildings, animals, and
convenience machines all compete for the same wallet. My goal is therefore not to
sell everything I touch. It is to keep enough cash available for the **next
progression gate**, while turning spare crops, quest rewards, scavenging, and later
production into a steady surplus.

My practical money route is:

**plant the starter crops → avoid gambling away the first few hundred Gold → buy the
first bag only when inventory pressure is real → stack side quests with normal
travel → sell low-priority loot but preserve progression materials → save 1,500G
when the Copper Pickaxe becomes the story gate → delay the 4,000G bag and other
convenience buys until the farm can refill the wallet quickly → use crop processing
and stronger production only after the underlying route is profitable.**

<h2 id="first-money">
  Protect the first few hundred Gold instead of chasing a lucky shortcut
</h2>

<AdUnit placement="article_after_first_h2" />

The first money mistake I avoid is spending cash just because something looks
discounted.

A good example is the **mixed seed pack**. It costs **100G** and can contain
different post-apocalyptic seeds. That sounds attractive because some individual
seeds are more expensive than others.

The problem is that the pack is still a gamble.

In one early purchase, I paid the full 100G and immediately judged the resulting
seed value at roughly **60G**, leaving me about **40G behind** if I looked only at
the immediate value of what came out.

  </figcaption>

That does not mean the pack is always bad. A high-value seed can make the purchase
look much better, and I may want the variety for research or collection.

It means I separate two decisions:

- **Do I want to gamble on seed variety?**
- **Do I need reliable money right now?**

If I need reliable money, I plant known seeds and keep the cash.

If I already have a working farm, enough inventory space, and no urgent tool
purchase coming up, then 100G is cheap enough that I can treat the pack as optional
fun rather than an investment that has to pay me back.

<h2 id="first-bag">
  The first 500G bag is worth buying when inventory is already wasting trips
</h2>

The first major convenience purchase I actually like is the bigger bag.

The first upgrade costs **500G**.

I do not buy it just because I finally reach 500G. I buy it when the current bag is
forcing me to leave useful drops behind, mail overflow items home, or return to the
farm in the middle of a productive route.

I have also bought that upgrade with exactly 500G available and immediately dropped
to **0G**.

That is the key distinction.

### I buy the first bag when

- my farm already has crops in the ground;
- I am regularly filling the starting inventory;
- exploration is producing items I actually want to keep;
- and spending 500G does not block a tool or quest purchase I need immediately.

### I wait when

- I still need seed money to establish the first harvest;
- I have not started earning from quests or exploration yet;
- or the next story step is already asking me to save for a tool.

Inventory space has real economic value because a full bag can turn a good trip into
a wasted trip. But I still need a way to recover after the purchase.

<h2 id="side-quests">
  Stack side quests with normal travel instead of grinding one low-value item
</h2>

Once the town opens up, side quests become one of my favorite ways to refill cash
because they can pay me while I am already doing something useful.

I do not run across the map for every request the moment it appears. I stack tasks
that overlap with my current route.

If I am already going toward River Valley, I check whether I can also:

- clear weeds or trash;
- bring back a requested crop;
- collect kelp or another common item;
- kill enemies that are already on the route;
- or turn in something sitting in storage.

That is much more efficient than dedicating an entire day to selling one cheap raw
resource.

One early quest handoff explicitly pays a cash reward, and shortly afterward I had
enough money to start choosing between bag space, tools, and other upgrades.

This is also why I like doing several small requests in one loop. A single quest may
not feel like a money strategy, but multiple turn-ins can move the wallet much faster
than selling a handful of common materials.

### My rule for board and side quests

I take the request when at least one of these is true:

- I already own most of the requested items;
- the target is on my current route;
- the task advances a faction, relationship, or system I also care about;
- or I need cash for a known purchase.

I skip or delay it when the request consumes a material that is currently blocking
my own progression.

Three Copper Ingots may be "easy money" later. They are not easy money if I am still
trying to build the tool, generator, or machine that needs those same Ingots.

<h2 id="progression-spending">
  Save for the progression gate before buying optional equipment
</h2>

The most important money decision is not "what sells for the most?"

It is:

> **What purchase opens more of the game right now?**

A better fishing rod can be tempting. Extra weapons can be tempting. A second bag
tier can be tempting.

But when the River Valley story turns the **Copper Pickaxe** into the physical gate,
I want **1,500G** available for that tool instead.

I use a simple spending ladder:

1. **current story gate**;
2. **inventory space if the bag is actively losing useful loot**;
3. **equipment that makes a repeated farm bottleneck disappear**;
4. **production upgrades that can repay themselves**;
5. **optional tools, furniture, luxury purchases, and collection goals**.

That order changes later, but it keeps the early game from becoming a cycle of
"finally earned money → bought something interesting → immediately cannot afford
the thing the story actually needs."

<h2 id="scavenging">
  Treat scavenging as a sell-versus-recycle decision
</h2>

Trash and mechanical scrap are useful because they give me a choice.

Lank explains that mechanical rubbish can either be **sold directly** or sent
through the recycler for a chance at materials that are harder to find in ordinary
trash.

That sounds like "always recycle" until I notice the catch:

**recycling costs money.**

For an early Small Mechanical Scrap interaction, I saw a **20G** recycling cost.

    recycler. When I need rare industrial materials more than cash, the decision flips.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

This is one of the best examples of why I do not use a universal "sell everything"
rule.

### I sell scrap when

- I need Gold for a known purchase today;
- the material is common enough that I already have a reserve;
- or paying the recycling fee would leave me unable to buy something more important.

### I recycle when

- the wallet is comfortable;
- I specifically need the possible industrial output;
- and I would otherwise spend time farming that material somewhere else.

The recycler can save me a resource trip. Direct sale can save me from being broke.
I choose based on the bottleneck.

<h2 id="what-not-to-sell">
  Keep progression materials even when the sell button is tempting
</h2>

A money guide becomes dangerous if it encourages me to liquidate everything.

I keep a protected reserve of materials that repeatedly appear in important
systems.

That includes categories such as:

- Copper and other Ingots while tool or machine upgrades are still pending;
- Electrical Wires, Batteries, Rubber, Chips, and other industrial parts;
- a first or unique quest item;
- useful gene seeds and Gene Capsules;
- seasonal fish I know I will need as breeding parents;
- the first Egg while the livestock tutorial is waiting for it;
- and any material already listed in my active story objective.

I do not need to know the perfect long-term use of every item before deciding not
to sell it.

If an item is uncommon, appears in a machine recipe, or has already shown up in a
quest chain, I would rather store a small reserve than earn a little Gold and then
have to farm the same item again.

The dedicated system pages own the exact reasons those materials matter. For
example, livestock progression and the first Egg stay in the <Link href="/doloc-town/animals" className="font-semibold text-sky-700 underline decoration-sky-300 underline-offset-4 hover:text-sky-900">Animals and Livestock Guide</Link>, while Small Batteries and the Environmental Modifier repair stay in the <Link href="/doloc-town/environmental-modifier" className="font-semibold text-sky-700 underline decoration-sky-300 underline-offset-4 hover:text-sky-900">Environmental Modifier Guide</Link>.

<h2 id="crop-money">
  Use crops as the stable income base, but do not duplicate the crop calculator here
</h2>

Crops are the income source I trust most because I control the input, timing, and
scale better than I do with random drops.

But this page does **not** need another giant crop table.

The money decision I make here is simpler:

- plant what I can actually support;
- do not spend the whole wallet on seeds;
- keep enough cash for the next progression purchase;
- and process crops only when the extra value is worth the machine time and
  infrastructure.

Once I am choosing between specific crops, climates, genes, direct sale, and
processing, I switch to the <Link href="/doloc-town/best-crops" className="font-semibold text-sky-700 underline decoration-sky-300 underline-offset-4 hover:text-sky-900">Best Crops and Profit Calculator</Link>.

That page owns the actual rankings.

The Money Guide only owns the budgeting rule:

> **A crop is not a good money crop for me if buying the seed leaves me unable to
> fund the next important thing.**

A theoretically stronger crop can still be the wrong purchase if it ties up my
cash for too long while I am saving for a tool or story gate.

<h2 id="fish-and-processing">
  Do not force fishing into an early money grind
</h2>

Fishing can produce sellable items, but I do not treat the Old Fishing Rod as an
automatic early cash machine.

With the weak starting setup, harder catches can take noticeably longer, and the
actual item pulled from the water can be random enough that I may get more Sardines
or junk when I was trying to find something specific.

That makes fishing useful when I already need:

- a quest fish;
- breeding parents;
- Salmon during the festival window;
- or another resource from the same water.

It is less attractive when the only goal is "I need 1,500G as fast as possible."

Later, processing can make fish more valuable than raw sale. The item data I am
using shows, for example, a **Sardine raw sell value of 30G**, while one simple
Sardine-based cooked combination is modeled at a **100G output** and **+60G net**
over its listed ingredient cost.

I use that as a reminder that "sell raw immediately" is not always optimal.

I do **not** turn this section into a fishing-location or fish-value database.
Catching routes, rods, fishing mechanics, and fish availability belong on the
dedicated Fishing Guide; breeding ratios already belong to the <Link href="/doloc-town/fish-breeding" className="font-semibold text-sky-700 underline decoration-sky-300 underline-offset-4 hover:text-sky-900">Fish Breeding Guide</Link>.

<h2 id="second-bag">
  The 4,000G bag is a different decision from the first 500G upgrade
</h2>

The next bag tier costs **4,000G**.

That is not "just another inventory upgrade."

The jump from 500G to 4,000G is large enough that I treat it as a midgame budget
decision.

When I first saw the 4,000G price, it was painful even though the extra capacity was
clearly useful.

I buy it when:

- the first bag is filling repeatedly during normal routes;
- I can earn several thousand Gold without selling protected materials;
- the next story purchase is already funded;
- and paying 4,000G will not shut down seed purchases or machine progression.

I wait when:

- 4,000G is most of my total cash;
- I still need a required tool;
- or my current bag is annoying but not actually causing me to abandon valuable loot.

The first bag can rescue the early game. The second bag is convenience I want the
economy to support.

<h2 id="broke-recovery">
  My one-day recovery route when I spend down to almost zero
</h2>

When I empty the wallet, I do not panic-sell the industrial box.

I use one recovery loop:

1. harvest anything already mature;
2. sell ordinary produce that is not reserved for research, quests, or processing;
3. check nearby board and side quests for turn-ins I can finish with existing items;
4. clear trash or enemies only if they are already on the route;
5. sell genuinely low-priority junk;
6. avoid new seed gambling, furniture, optional tools, and animals for the rest of the day;
7. plant enough of the next crop cycle to keep tomorrow's income alive.

That route usually fixes the real problem: I have **cash tied up**, not a permanently
broken economy.

The mistake would be solving a temporary 0G balance by selling the Copper, Wire,
Battery, gene seed, or quest item that I will have to repurchase or refarm
immediately.

<h2 id="money-priority">
  My final rule: spend Gold to remove the current bottleneck
</h2>

I do not use one fixed money strategy for the whole save.

Early game, the bottleneck is usually **cash and inventory**.

Then it becomes **tool progression**.

Later it becomes **power, processing capacity, automation, specialized systems, and
large optional purchases**.

So before I spend, I ask one question:

> **What is actually slowing me down right now?**

If the answer is "my bag is full every ten minutes," I buy space.

If the answer is "the story is blocked by rocks," I save for the Pickaxe.

If the answer is "I have crops but chose the wrong thing to scale," I use the crop
calculator.

If the answer is "I have plenty of Gold but no rare materials," I stop selling
everything and use scavenging or production to solve the material problem instead.

That is the money loop I trust in Doloc Town:

**earn from the systems I am already using → protect scarce materials → fund the
next real bottleneck → only then spend on convenience.**
