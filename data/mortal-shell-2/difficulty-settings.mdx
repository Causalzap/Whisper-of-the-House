<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Mortal Shell 2 does **not** ask me to choose Easy, Normal, or Hard when I start
a new game.

Instead, I manage difficulty through systems inside the playthrough.

The **Slayer Seal** is the closest thing to an Easy Mode. It gives my Sidearm
Break Damage, improves riposte recovery, and adds Slayer Punch as a protected
mobility tool.

The important warning is much bigger than a normal difficulty toggle:

**equipping the Slayer Seal disables achievements on that save.**

At the other end, **Night** is the optional harder world state. I use it when my
build is already working and I deliberately want greater challenge and better
rewards.

My rule is:

**learning or progressing → stay Standard**

**combat itself is stopping me and achievements do not matter on this save →
consider Slayer Seal**

**Standard already feels comfortable and I want harder encounters → use Night**

<h2 id="no-normal-difficulty-menu">
  Mortal Shell 2 does not have a normal Easy, Normal, or Hard menu
</h2>

The difficulty system made much more sense to me once I stopped looking for a
traditional setting screen.

I do not start a save by permanently choosing:

**Easy**

**Normal**

or:

**Hard**

before I know how the combat actually feels.

Instead, Mortal Shell 2 lets me change the kind of challenge I want through
systems I discover inside the game.

That matters because I get to learn the standard combat first.

I can find a Shell.

I can test a weapon.

I can reach different regions.

I can fight several bosses.

Only then do I decide whether the problem I am having is actually the game's
difficulty.

That distinction saves me from changing the entire experience every time one
enemy hits harder than expected.

<h2 id="how-i-read-difficulty">
  I separate difficulty problems from build and progression problems first
</h2>

This is the check I make before changing anything.

If normal enemies suddenly feel much stronger than everything I fought ten
minutes earlier, I ask:

**Did I walk into a harder region too early?**

If my damage feels terrible, I ask:

**Do I actually like and understand the weapon I am using?**

If my survivability feels wrong, I ask:

**Have I found a Shell I am comfortable with and invested in it at all?**

And if one boss destroys me several times, I ask:

**Am I learning the fight, or am I genuinely no longer enjoying this level of
challenge?**

Those are four different problems.

Only the last one is automatically a difficulty problem.

Mortal Shell 2 lets me reach dangerous content surprisingly early, so the fact
that I *can* enter an area does not mean changing difficulty is the best answer
to what happens there.

<h2 id="slayer-seal">
  Slayer Seal is the closest thing Mortal Shell 2 has to Easy Mode
</h2>

The **Slayer Seal** is the system I look at when I genuinely want combat to
become more forgiving.

The game is unusually direct about its purpose:

**it reduces combat difficulty.**

What I like about the system is that I do not have to decide before playing.

I can learn the normal combat first.

If I am enjoying the challenge, I leave everything alone.

If I eventually reach the point where combat itself is preventing me from
continuing the game, the easier option is still there.

That is very different from discovering ten hours later that I chose the wrong
difficulty when I created the save.

<h2 id="what-slayer-seal-does">
  What the Slayer Seal actually changes
</h2>

The Slayer Seal does not simply make an invisible enemy-damage number smaller.

It changes the tools I have available in combat.

My **Sidearm attacks deal Break Damage**, which gives me another way to expose
enemies instead of relying entirely on close-range pressure.

A successful **riposte restores some Health and Resolve**.

And I gain **Slayer Punch**.

The tutorial describes Slayer Punch as giving me invulnerability while dashing
a long distance.

Those changes solve several problems at once:

**more ranged Break pressure**

**more recovery after a successful riposte**

**another protected way to reposition**

That is why I think of Slayer Seal as an alternate combat toolset rather than
just a hidden percentage modifier.

It changes how safely I can approach a difficult encounter.

<h2 id="achievement-warning">
  The Slayer Seal achievement warning is the decision that matters most
</h2>

This is the part I would never click through casually.

The game explicitly warns:

**equipping the Slayer Seal disables achievements on that save.**

Because the warning says achievements are disabled **on this save**, I treat the
choice as consequential.

I do **not** equip the Seal temporarily just to see whether I like it.

I also do not assume that unequipping it later will restore achievement
eligibility.

If this is my achievement or completion save, I leave Slayer Seal alone.

If I only care about finishing the game and normal combat has become the thing
that is making me want to stop playing, the tradeoff is much easier to accept.

That is the situation where the Seal makes sense to me.

<h2 id="when-i-use-slayer-seal">
  When I would actually use Slayer Seal
</h2>

I consider Slayer Seal only after I have ruled out simpler problems.

I first check whether:

- I am pushing an area earlier than I need to;
- my weapon's moveset simply does not fit me;
- I have neglected my Shell;
- I have upgrades available that I already planned to use;
- the boss is still teaching me something each attempt.

If one of those is the problem, I fix that first.

The Seal becomes attractive when I know:

**my build is functional, my route makes sense, and I simply do not want combat
to demand this much from me anymore.**

That is a much better reason to give up achievement eligibility than one bad
fight.

<h2 id="boss-stuck">
  One hard boss is not enough for me to change the save
</h2>

I do not equip Slayer Seal because I lost a boss fight three or five times.

Death count by itself tells me very little.

If every attempt shows me something new — a punish window, an unblockable, a
better dodge direction, or a bad habit in my own attacks — I stay with Standard.

I reconsider only when the fight has crossed from:

**challenging**

to:

**I no longer want to spend my time doing this.**

That is a personal threshold rather than a fixed number of attempts.

<h2 id="standard-difficulty">
  Standard is the state I use for normal progression
</h2>

For most of my first playthrough, I stay with the standard experience.

That gives me the cleanest baseline for learning:

- enemy damage;
- my weapon's real performance;
- my Shell's survivability;
- boss timing;
- dungeon layouts;
- Beacon locations;
- which routes are intended for my current progression.

I want that baseline before adding another modifier.

If an enemy feels too durable on Standard, I can reasonably ask whether my
weapon or route is the problem.

If I immediately change several systems at once, that becomes much harder to
judge.

So whenever my goal is:

**explore a new region**

**learn a dungeon**

**find a Shell**

**reach a Beacon**

or:

**figure out where the story goes next**

Standard is my default.

**Should I turn Night on at all?**

If I am still struggling to progress on Standard, my answer is usually no.

If my build is mature, I already know the area, and I deliberately want more

<h2 id="slayer-vs-night">
  Slayer Seal and Night move difficulty in opposite directions
</h2>

This is the simplest way I keep the systems separate.

**Slayer Seal** answers:

**How can I make combat more forgiving?**

**Night** answers:

**What if Standard is already comfortable and I want a harder world?**

I do not see them as three levels in one menu.

I see them as different tools for different goals.

Slayer Seal is a decision about reducing the barrier to finishing the game.

Night is a decision about deliberately increasing challenge.

Standard stays in the middle as the experience I use when I am simply trying
to play and progress normally.

<h2 id="which-difficulty">
  Which Mortal Shell 2 difficulty option I choose
</h2>

My final decision is straightforward.

I stay **Standard** when:

- I am learning the game;
- I am exploring new areas;
- my build is still changing;
- I care about keeping achievement eligibility intact.

I consider **Slayer Seal** when:

- combat itself has become the reason I am no longer enjoying the run;
- I have already ruled out a bad route or underdeveloped build;
- I care more about continuing than achievements on that save.

I choose **Night** when:

- Standard already feels comfortable;
- my build is established;
- I deliberately want harder encounters and better rewards.

I do not change difficulty because one enemy killed me.

I change it when the kind of experience I want from the next part of the game
has changed.

That is the part of Mortal Shell 2's system I find most useful:

**I make the difficulty decision after I understand what problem I am actually
trying to solve.**
