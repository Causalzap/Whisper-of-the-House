The clearest endgame signal is not a random dungeon entrance or another boss
marker.

It is Undermether telling me that the Ova I have recovered are finally enough.

At that point, the problem is no longer finding more eggs.

There is still something preventing them from being sent where they are meant
to go:

**the foul passenger.**

That is when the objective changes to:

**walk the Unfound Path.**

<h2 id="is-unfound-path-point-of-no-return">
  I treat the Unfound Path as a cleanup warning, not a proven hard lockout
</h2>

I would not describe **Walk the Unfound Path** as a hard point of no return
based on what happens afterward.

After completing the main ending, I regain control and can continue interacting
with the world.

That means I do not need to delay the ending just because I am afraid the save
will disappear the instant I finish the final boss.

At the same time, I do not use that to promise that every unfinished quest,
missable interaction, or NPC state remains unchanged.

Those are separate questions.

For my first clear, I use a simple rule:

**finish anything I personally care about before the final stretch, but do not
turn the whole map into mandatory cleanup just because the Unfound Path
appeared.**

That distinction saves a lot of unnecessary endgame grinding.

<h2 id="what-unfound-path-means">
  What “walk the Unfound Path” actually means
</h2>

The objective makes more sense once I stop reading it as the name of one more
ordinary area.

Undermether explains the purpose directly.

The recovered Ova are enough to secure the future of the kin, but they cannot
be sent to the heavens while the **foul passenger** remains.

So the Unfound Path is the final act of separating that corruption from
Undermether before the kin can leave.

That also connects back to something the game establishes much earlier.

The foul passenger has been inside Undermether's remains since before the
Harbinger's journey began.

This is not a new villain introduced just for the final hour.

The ending is finally resolving the threat that has been present behind the
entire Ova recovery mission.

<h2 id="before-final-boss">
  What I do before the final boss
</h2>

Before I commit to the last fight, I make fewer changes than I normally would
before a difficult boss.

I do **not** rebuild everything.

By this point I would rather take:

- the Shell whose dodge, healing, and recovery rhythm I already understand;
- the weapon whose attack windows I already know;
- the upgrades I have actually used in difficult fights;
- enough healing and Resolve support to recover from mistakes.

The final stretch is a bad place for me to discover that I dislike a new
weapon's running heavy or that a new Shell's healing mechanic works differently
than I expected.

I also spend resources I was intentionally saving.

There is no value in carrying a pile of upgrade materials through the final
fight if I already know where they should go.

The difference is that I do not spend just for the sake of seeing empty
currency counters.

If an upgrade does not improve the setup I am taking into the fight, I keep it.

When I am ready, I return to the final interaction with Undermether.

The journey was not about collecting them as trophies.

<h2 id="harbinger-stays-behind">
  Why the Harbinger stays behind
</h2>

Undermether tells the Harbinger that he was **not made for the heavens**.

Instead, he has to stay behind and:

**find his own meaning.**

For me, this is the actual point of the ending.

Most of the game is structured around purpose.

The Harbinger is told where to go.

The Ova need to be recovered.

The foul passenger needs to be removed.

Shells are bodies whose old lives already had stories, duties, failures, and
identities attached to them.

Then the ending takes that structure away.

The kin have somewhere to go.

The Harbinger does not.

He survives, but survival now comes without a mission being handed to him.

<h2 id="opening-connection">
  The ending pays off what the opening says about the Harbinger
</h2>

The ending makes more sense to me when I think back to the beginning.

The Harbinger is told very early that although he hatched here, he does not
truly belong with the others in the same way.

At the time, that mainly functions as the reason I am different.

At the end, it becomes the reason I am left behind.

I do not read that as Undermether rejecting him.

The farewell is much more personal than that.

I read it as the Harbinger finally becoming something other than a tool for
someone else's purpose.

He has spent the game inhabiting other people's bodies and recovering fragments
of other people's lives.

Now he is the one who has to decide what his own life means.

<h2 id="is-there-ending-choice">
  I do not get a final good-or-bad ending choice
</h2>

I do not encounter a final dialogue wheel asking me to choose between sending
the kin away or keeping them behind.

The main ending sequence I reach follows the same outcome:

**the foul passenger is removed → the kin are sent on → the Harbinger stays.**

That is important because I would not restart the final fight looking for a
different answer just because the scene feels like it could support one.

The ending is a story consequence, not a last-second morality selection.

<h2 id="after-ending">
  Can I keep playing after the ending?
</h2>

Yes — completing the ending does not simply delete my run or leave me trapped
in the final cutscene.

After the farewell, I regain control and can continue interacting with NPCs.

That is useful because it changes how aggressively I clean up before Zmey.

I still finish anything I personally want to see before the finale.

I just do not assume that the entire world disappears afterward.

What I **do not** assume is that every missable remains safe forever.

Being able to keep playing and proving that every quest state remains available
are two different things.

That is why I am comfortable finishing the story without calling the ending a
universal cleanup reset.

**Undermether farewell = Mortal Shell 2 ending.**

<h2 id="what-ending-means">
  What I think the Mortal Shell 2 ending means
</h2>

The ending lands differently for me because the Harbinger spends almost the
entire game borrowing identity.

I fight through Shells.

I uncover their memories.

I build their abilities.

I inherit the strengths created by lives that already happened.

Even the central story gives me a purpose before I am capable of choosing one:

recover the kin and help Undermether.

By the time I defeat the foul passenger, that purpose is complete.

The kin leave.

Undermether's role is finished.

And the Harbinger is still here.

So when Undermether tells him to find his own meaning, I do not read it as a
setup for one more objective marker.

I read it as the first time the Harbinger is **not given one**.

That is why the ending works for me.

The game starts with a character whose identity comes from inhabiting other
lives.

It ends with that character finally being left alone to decide what his own
life is for.
