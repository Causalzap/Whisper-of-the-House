<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
For a straightforward first party, put a **Paladin and Warrior in front**, with a **Ranger and Voidcaller in back**. This covers melee damage, healing, ranged attacks, and status removal. Choose races according to each class's important attributes and equipment requirements. A Blood Mage is a good alternative if you want Bone-powered spells and bleeding damage.
</GuideQuickAnswer>


Underkeep gives you four permanent party positions to fill before your adventure begins. The decision matters because the classes do more than deal different types of damage. They also determine how you heal, control dangerous enemies, spend resources, and handle fights against multiple targets.

You do not need a Thief simply to open locked chests, and you do not need to put every spellcaster in the back row. A party that covers frontline survival, healing, ranged attacks, and sustained damage is more useful than four characters chosen independently for their largest damage number.

The strongest choice also depends on the races and initial attributes you roll. A high-Strength character can use equipment that a high-Wisdom character may not qualify for, while a Paladin can benefit from having several reasonably high attributes rather than maximizing Strength alone.

<h2 id="party-formation">
  How Does Party Formation Work in Underkeep?
</h2>

Your party has **four positions: two in front and two in back**. Front-row characters can use melee weapons and usually take the brunt of enemy attacks. Characters in the rear normally rely on ranged weapons or spells, although a weapon with **Reach** allows them to make melee attacks from the back row.

Formation affects how your characters' abilities work together. A frontliner who applies Bleed can create opportunities for another character's follow-up damage, but even a well-protected front row will not prevent every attack from reaching the back.

When deciding where to place a character, consider its equipment and the range of its attacks rather than relying on class names alone. For movement, potion use, and recovery during fights, see the <Link href="/underkeep/beginner-guide">Underkeep combat basics</Link>.

<h3>Warrior — Physical Damage and Reliable Stun Attempts</h3>

Warrior primarily uses **Strength** and is suited to the front row.

One of its most useful starting options is **Skull Rattle**, a Mace attack that deals damage and has a chance to stun the target. Its advantage over a stun-only ability is straightforward: when the stun fails, the attack can still damage the enemy.

That distinction matters against elites and bosses. Losing an entire character action on an unsuccessful control effect can be expensive, particularly when the enemy is capable of attacking several party members.

Warrior also has two useful directions to consider:

- **Cleave:** attacks two enemies when using a two-handed weapon, making it useful against groups.
- **Battle Roar:** improves physical and critical-hit damage for the party, particularly when several characters rely on weapon attacks.

These options favor different equipment and party compositions. Skull Rattle requires a Mace, while Cleave requires a two-handed weapon. You should not select several weapon-specific abilities without checking whether you can use them together with your intended weapon.

A Warrior is an appealing option when you want an uncomplicated physical frontliner. However, a party already receiving strong healing and defensive support from Paladin and Voidcaller may benefit more from another damage type or utility skill.

<h3>Thief — Evasion, Status Effects, and Damage Amplification</h3>

**You do not need a Thief to pick locks.** Lockpicking is not an exclusive Thief ability, so creating one only because you expect locked doors or chests is unnecessary.

Thief is more interesting as an evasive melee character who makes enemies easier to kill.

Its early **Nightshade Mastery** ability works with Daggers or Swords and adds a chance for basic attacks to inflict harmful effects, including Bleed, Poison, Blind, or Burn.

**Mark of the Dead** is another strong option. At its initial rank, it increases the damage an affected enemy receives by 20%, allowing other party members to benefit from the mark as well.

For survival, **Evasive Roll** raises Evasion for two rounds and increases the normal Evasion cap as the skill improves. The ordinary cap is 60%; investing in Evasive Roll can raise that cap to 70%.

This creates two distinct Thief approaches:

- Use Nightshade Mastery and enemy marks to support the party's offensive damage.
- Emphasize Evasion when the Thief must survive in a front-row position.

Thief becomes especially interesting beside Ranger or Blood Mage, because these classes can also exploit marked enemies or harmful status effects.

<h3>Paladin — Zeal-Based Healing and Strong Melee Damage</h3>

Paladin is a Strength-based class that also receives **+1 Wisdom**.

Its defining resource is **Zeal**, generated through basic attacks. Rather than relying on a conventional mana pool for its signature abilities, Paladin can build Zeal during combat and spend it on healing or attacks.

**Lay on Hands** is a particularly useful early ability. It restores Health by consuming Zeal, with healing based on the number of Zeal points spent. Its initial healing rate is three Health per Zeal.

This gives the Paladin a reason to keep attacking even when the party does not need immediate healing: those attacks build a resource that can be used when someone is injured.

Another useful skill is **Zealous Resolve**, which increases melee damage according to the Zeal currently held.

At Level 6, **Judgment** becomes available. It deals stronger melee damage, scales with Zeal, and is particularly useful against Demons and Undead.

The practical trade-off is deciding when to spend Zeal. Using it immediately for minor healing may prevent you from benefiting from a larger later heal or a more powerful attack. Holding every point indefinitely is not useful either if a party member is in danger.

For races, Human is a flexible Paladin choice because Paladin benefits from Strength, Endurance, and enough Wisdom to qualify for certain equipment. Dwarf can provide a more physically focused alternative.

<h3>Ranger — Reliable Ranged Attacks and Marked Targets</h3>

Ranger focuses on **Dexterity** and is one of the easiest classes to justify in the back row.

Its normal arrow attacks do not require a separate ammunition supply. This allows a Ranger to damage enemies as they approach without consuming the same type of resources as a spellcaster.

The class becomes stronger when its accuracy and damage bonuses work together.

**Cloak of Swiftness** increases Evasion, ranged hit chance, and ranged critical-hit chance. Investing in hit chance is especially useful because a powerful ranged attack still contributes nothing when it misses.

**Hunter's Prey** marks an enemy for several rounds, increasing the damage that enemy receives.

**Execution Shot** then deals additional damage against marked targets. Its initial marked-target bonus is 40%, giving the Ranger a reason to attack enemies already affected by Hunter's Prey or another compatible mark.

A useful approach is to improve reliable ranged hits, obtain a marking option, and then strengthen Execution Shot as it becomes available.

Ranger fits especially well alongside Thief or Blood Mage, but those classes do not need to be present for a Ranger to contribute meaningful damage.

<h3>Voidcaller — Healing, Status Removal, and Party Auras</h3>

Voidcaller primarily uses **Wisdom** and provides some of the most versatile defensive support.

Its healing skill, **Void Mend**, has an important advantage: it can both restore Health and remove negative status effects. As it improves, it can remove more harmful effects.

This makes Voidcaller valuable when enemies repeatedly inflict Bleed, Burn, or other conditions that cannot be solved simply by restoring Health.

Voidcaller also has three party auras:

- **Aura of the Void:** provides damage absorption for the party.
- **Aura of Thorns:** reflects melee damage back at attackers.
- **Aura of Blades:** increases melee attack damage.

The auras share progression, and the active aura can be changed when the Voidcaller levels up.

Aura of the Void is attractive during the earlier stages, when its barrier can absorb meaningful damage. Later, enemies may destroy the barrier quickly, making Aura of Thorns a stronger alternative in fights involving frequent melee attacks.

**Power of the Void** further improves healing effectiveness, supporting a character built around party recovery rather than direct damage.

A Voidcaller can occupy the front row, but that choice requires attention to survivability. Its Wisdom focus does not automatically provide the Strength needed for heavy armor. If you cannot make it durable enough for direct pressure, move it to the back and use a sturdier frontliner instead.

<h3>Wizard — Elemental Damage With Significant Mana Costs</h3>

Wizard is a **Wisdom** class with Fire, Lightning, and Ice spells.

Its spell selection has a major resource consequence: improving spells can increase their Mana cost. A stronger spell may be less useful if you can cast it only a few times before running out of Mana.

For a Fire-focused Wizard, **Fire Spark** provides an early damage option with a Burn chance, but it targets enemies directly in front of the caster.

At Level 6, **Fireball** offers a more flexible ranged attack capable of damaging two enemies. Fireball can also inflict Burn, making it useful against enemies approaching along a corridor or standing close together.

A Fire-focused Wizard should consider:

- **Fire Spark** for an initial Fire attack.
- **Field of Flow** to increase the available Mana pool.
- **Fireball** once unlocked for stronger ranged damage.
- **Dragon Arcana** for additional Fire damage and Burn effectiveness.

Lightning offers attacks that can affect multiple enemies, while Ice provides chances to Freeze targets. Both can be useful, but their effectiveness depends on enemy resistance, the chance for secondary effects to activate, and available equipment.

Do not assume that increasing Wisdom directly increases spell damage. Wisdom contributes to Mana and elemental resistance; the relevant spell descriptions and equipment effects determine damage.

Wizard is a good choice when you want elemental flexibility and are willing to manage Mana carefully.

<h3>Blood Mage — Bone-Powered Spells and Bleeding Damage</h3>

Blood Mage primarily emphasizes **Endurance** and uses an unusual casting resource: **Bones**.

When Bones are available, Blood Mage can consume them to cast spells. When they are unavailable, the class can use its own blood instead, making resource availability directly relevant to survival.

An important difference from Wizard is that improving a Blood Mage spell does not automatically increase its Bone cost. A spell can remain one Bone per cast even after receiving additional ranks.

**Blood Lance** is a useful starting attack because it damages enemies at range and can apply Bleed.

**Mark of the Dead** helps other attackers deal more damage to a target, while **Omen of the Veins** improves critical-hit potential for blood spells.

For fighting multiple enemies, **Bloodfall** is an alternative worth considering. It attacks multiple targets and can apply Bleed.

Blood Mage also has abilities that interact with existing Bleed effects, including **Exsanguinate**, which increases the damage and duration of Bleed on a target.

This makes Blood Mage a natural partner for characters who already inflict Bleed, particularly Thief or Ranger.

The main caution is resource management. A Blood Mage with Bones available can sustain spellcasting more comfortably; one repeatedly spending Health to cast needs closer attention to survival.

<h2 id="races">
  Which Race Should You Choose in Underkeep?
</h2>

Underkeep has six playable races. They do not simply change character appearance: they affect starting attribute distributions and provide racial traits that can influence equipment choices or combat.

  rows={raceRows}
/>

The easiest way to choose is to begin with the class you want to play and then compare how each race supports its attributes.

**Human** is the flexible option. Its balanced starting attributes are useful when a character needs multiple stats, rather than relying on one exceptionally high value. The additional 10% experience is helpful, but it does not automatically make Human the strongest race for every class.

**Dwarf** suits characters needing Strength and Endurance. Its elemental resistance bonus provides some protection, although its lower Wisdom can offset part of that advantage. Dwarf also has Infravision, but you should not select it solely on the assumption that Infravision will reveal a particular secret or hidden route.

**Elk** emphasizes Dexterity. It is a natural candidate for Ranger and other characters who benefit from ranged accuracy or Evasion. The Beast Warden racial benefit adds Armor against animals, though this is not a substitute for checking overall survivability.


**Stoneborn** offers very high Strength and useful physical durability, but it cannot equip body armor. Its **Stonebound** trait grants +1 Armor at levels 1, 5, 9, and 13, for a total of +4 Armor by Level 13. This partially compensates for the missing armor slot, but it does not replace the special effects available on body armor. Before choosing Stoneborn, consider whether your build depends on those equipment bonuses.

<h2 id="attributes">
  How Do Attributes and Stat Rerolls Work?
</h2>

You do not distribute starting attribute points one by one. Your chosen class and race influence the ranges, and the character creator lets you reroll the initial results.

The four main attributes serve different purposes.

### Strength

Strength affects melee damage, Health, and the ability to equip certain weapons and armor.

It is especially important to Warrior and Paladin. High Strength can also allow a character to use heavier equipment that other classes cannot equip at the same level.

### Dexterity

Dexterity influences ranged accuracy, ranged damage, Evasion, and critical-hit potential.

It matters most for Ranger and evasion-oriented characters, including many Thief builds.

A character with poor Dexterity may struggle to make effective use of equipment or strategies that depend on Evasion.

### Endurance

Endurance contributes to Health growth, Stamina regeneration, and resistance to some negative effects.

It is useful to frontliners who need to survive repeated attacks and to Blood Mage, whose casting system can involve spending Health.

### Wisdom

Wisdom contributes to Mana growth and natural elemental resistance.

It also matters for equipment requirements. Some items intended for Paladin or other support characters can require Wisdom even when the class primarily uses another attribute.

Higher Wisdom does not, by itself, mean that every spell deals more damage.

### Should You Keep Rerolling Your Starting Stats?

Reroll with a purpose rather than trying to maximize every displayed number.

For a Paladin, check Strength, Endurance, and Wisdom together. A slightly lower Strength roll may be worthwhile if the character gains enough Wisdom to equip useful Paladin gear while retaining adequate Health.

For Ranger, prioritize a strong Dexterity result, but do not overlook survivability entirely.

For Blood Mage, Endurance deserves particular attention because the class may use Health to cast when Bones are unavailable.

For Wizard or Voidcaller, look for sufficient Wisdom to support the intended spellcasting and equipment. If the character must stand in front, its other defensive attributes also matter.

Once the roll supports the character's actual role, continuing to reroll for a marginal improvement may not change how the party plays.


<h2 id="skill-points">
  How Should You Spend Your First Skill Points?
</h2>

Underkeep has a **Level 15 cap**, so your skill choices matter throughout the adventure. Many upgradeable skills have up to five ranks, but not every ability can be leveled. For example, the Paladin's **Stun Undead** is a starting skill that cannot be upgraded.

Before spending a point, check three things.

### Does the Skill Require a Specific Weapon?

Some abilities work only with certain weapon types. Warrior's Skull Rattle requires a Mace, while Thief's Nightshade Mastery works with Swords or Daggers.

A skill that does not match your equipped weapon cannot contribute to your intended combat strategy. Check its requirements before committing points, particularly if you plan to replace your starting equipment.

### When Does the Skill Become Available?

Not every class ability is available at Level 1. Some stronger options unlock later, such as the Paladin's Judgment and the Wizard's Fireball at Level 6.

Spend early points on abilities that help your party now while considering which later skills you want to support. You do not need to invest in every available option just because it appears in the skill list.

### What Does Another Rank Actually Improve?

Additional ranks may increase damage, healing, effect duration, or the chance of applying a status condition. They can also change resource costs, depending on the ability.

The Paladin's Stun Undead illustrates why checking the tooltip matters. It consumes all available Zeal charges and has a 20% chance to stun per Zeal consumed, but it cannot be upgraded with additional skill points.

<h2 id="starting-party">
  What Is a Good Starting Party in Underkeep?
</h2>

A balanced Underkeep party needs reliable frontline damage, healing, ranged attacks, and ways to handle dangerous status effects. You can cover those roles with several class combinations, but two arrangements are particularly practical.

### Recommended First Party: Paladin, Warrior, Ranger, Voidcaller

**Front row: Paladin + Warrior**

The Paladin provides melee damage and Zeal-based healing, while the Warrior handles direct physical attacks and offers control options such as Skull Rattle.

This pairing gives you two characters designed to fight in melee rather than relying on a support-oriented caster to survive at the front.

**Back row: Ranger + Voidcaller**

Ranger provides consistent ranged damage, including ordinary arrow attacks that do not require a separate ammunition supply.

Voidcaller adds healing, harmful-status removal, and defensive auras. Keeping this character in the rear also reduces the pressure to meet the equipment requirements of a durable frontliner.

This is a practical first-party choice because its responsibilities are clear and it does not require unusual equipment combinations to function.

### Alternative Party: Paladin, Voidcaller, Ranger, Blood Mage

This composition trades the Warrior's conventional frontline role for additional spellcasting and status-based damage.

**Front row: Paladin + Voidcaller**

Paladin still provides melee damage and Zeal-based healing. Voidcaller contributes another recovery option and party-wide defensive effects.

The important limitation is the Voidcaller's survivability. A Wisdom-focused character does not automatically qualify for heavy armor, so check its attributes and equipment before keeping it in front.

**Back row: Ranger + Blood Mage**

Ranger supplies reliable ranged attacks and marked-target damage.

Blood Mage adds Bone-powered spells, Bleed effects, and another way to exploit weakened or marked enemies.

This arrangement can provide useful offensive interactions, but it requires more attention to the Voidcaller's defenses and the Blood Mage's resource use.

### Can You Replace One of These Classes?

Yes. A **Thief** can replace a frontliner if you prefer Evasion, status effects, and damage amplification. A **Wizard** can replace a backline damage dealer if you prefer Fire, Lightning, or Ice spells over Blood Mage's Bone-based casting.

The best replacement depends on what the rest of your party already provides. If your group lacks reliable healing, changing its only support character for another damage dealer may create more problems than the extra damage solves.

Choose a party whose abilities work together rather than selecting four characters solely for their individual damage potential.


<h2 id="race-class-pairings">
  Which Race and Class Combinations Make Sense?
</h2>

Match your race to the attributes your class uses most often, while checking whether the racial limitation affects your intended equipment.

A few starting combinations are particularly straightforward:

- **Human Paladin:** a balanced choice for a character that benefits from Strength, Endurance, and Wisdom.
- **Dwarf Warrior:** emphasizes Strength and durability for direct melee combat.
- **Elk Ranger:** supports Dexterity-focused ranged accuracy and Evasion.
- **Slugman Blood Mage:** offers high Endurance for a class that may spend Health to cast spells, but its double fire damage remains a serious disadvantage.
- **Human Voidcaller:** provides a flexible attribute distribution when building a support character.

Stoneborn can also be attractive for physical builds, but losing the body armor slot changes your equipment options. Raven is more variable because its starting mutations are random.

These are starting combinations rather than mandatory pairings. The class's equipment requirements, your attribute roll, and the other three party members should determine the final choice.

<h2 id="character-creation">
  What Should You Check Before Starting the Game?
</h2>

Before confirming your four characters, review the choices that are difficult to evaluate once you have already committed to a build.

1. **Check each attribute roll.** Make sure the character has suitable values for its intended class and role. A Paladin may need more than Strength alone.
2. **Review equipment requirements.** Some weapons and armor require specific attribute values, and Stoneborn cannot equip body armor.
3. **Check weapon-specific skills.** Do not select a Mace-only or Dagger-only ability without considering the weapon you intend to use.
4. **Read the starting skill tooltip.** Some abilities cannot be upgraded, and others consume resources such as Zeal, Mana, Stamina, Bones, or Health.
5. **Confirm the four party positions.** Make sure your frontline can withstand direct attacks and that the backline characters can use their intended weapons or spells.

You do not need a perfect starting attribute roll to begin. A party with compatible skills, equipment, and clear roles is more useful than one built around isolated maximum numbers.
