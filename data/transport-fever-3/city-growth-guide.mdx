A town does not grow because you spent more money around it.

Transport Fever 3 reacts to what the settlement is actually receiving. A town
with no useful passenger movement can stagnate even while roads surround it.
Another can have excellent buses and trains but still be missing the cargo its
commercial or industrial districts want. Later, a successful transport network
can create enough noise or pollution to become part of the next growth problem.

Spalding shows the progression well. It began with **0% growth**, no useful
transport, poor supplies, and a **Stagnant** status. Passenger movement got the
town growing. Cargo improved the situation further.

That makes the town panel more useful than guessing from the map.


<h2
  id="read-town-panel"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Read the Town Panel Before Building Another Route
</h2>

A town can tell you what is holding it back.

At the beginning of the Spalding start, the panel showed:

- **0 transport**;
- no meaningful cargo supply;
- a **Very Poor** Supplies Rating;
- **0% growth**;
- and **Stagnant** growth.

Those numbers point to two immediate weaknesses: residents are not moving
usefully around the settlement, and requested goods are not reaching it.

The town is divided into residential, commercial, and industrial development,
so passenger movement and cargo supply can affect different parts of the same
settlement.

Do not read every weak rating as a reason to build something large.

A town with no passenger movement does not need an intercity railway first. A
town with strong passenger service but missing cargo does not need more buses.
The useful investment is the one aimed at the rating that is currently weak.


<h2
  id="public-transport-growth"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Passenger Transport Can Move a Town Out of Stagnation
</h2>

Spalding began responding before all of its cargo needs were solved.

Once the first local passenger service started moving residents, the town
changed from **stagnant** to **growing slowly**. The transport rating was not
perfect, but the town was no longer stuck.

That is the first thing I check when a small settlement has no growth at all.

The important part for growth is not the number of vehicles on the road. It is
whether residents can actually reach useful parts of the town.

Stop placement and the first local route are covered in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">beginner setup</Link>.
From the growth side, watch what happens after that service begins carrying
people.

Large towns need another check later: **did the town grow beyond the network
that originally served it?**

Warwick eventually reached about **970 population**. By that point, its early
passenger layout no longer covered the settlement as well as it had at the
start, so the local network had to expand with the town.

<h2
  id="cargo-supplies"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Busy Passenger Lines Do Not Replace Missing Cargo
</h2>

A town can move people well and still have incomplete Supplies.

Spalding requested vegetables and planks. Vegetables began arriving before the
plank supply was solved, and the town improved even though the entire panel was
not complete.

Chiswick later reached the opposite situation: passenger movement was strong and
Supplies were generally good, while some requested goods were still incomplete.

So when buses and trains look healthy but growth remains weaker than expected,
open the cargo bars rather than adding more passenger capacity.

The growth question is simple:

> **Is the good the town is asking for actually reaching the district that
> consumes it?**

How that good is produced, transferred, stored, and loaded belongs in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry network</Link>.

For town growth, you only need to identify the missing demand and confirm that
the delivery reaches the right part of the settlement.


<h2
  id="growth-delay"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Give the Town Time to React Before Changing Everything Again
</h2>

A better rating does not instantly produce hundreds of residents.

When Spalding's first passenger service came online, the growth state improved,
but the population still needed time to build. Cargo deliveries then improved
the town further.

That delay matters when troubleshooting.

Suppose you rebuild the passenger route, start a new cargo delivery, add more
vehicles, and demolish several buildings within a few moments. The town may
improve, but you no longer know which change mattered.

After fixing a visible weakness, let the simulation run long enough to see
whether the growth state responds.

You do not need every bar at maximum before the town can progress.


<h2
  id="town-levels"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Town Levels Come From Sustained Growth
</h2>

Population growth eventually raises the settlement level.

Spudsbury reached **Small Village** after its passenger and supply network had
been operating successfully. Later growth pushed it into **Large Village**.

Chiswick also leveled up while one part of its network was still imperfect.

That is useful because it means a town does not require every rating to be
maxed before development can continue.

Once growth is already positive, I work on the weakest meaningful rating rather
than trying to make every number perfect at the same time.


<h2
  id="headquarters"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Headquarters Bonuses Accelerate a Town That Already Works
</h2>

The Headquarters can directly increase the growth rate of the nearby town.

An early Headquarters placement showed a **+5% town growth rate**. A later
extension displayed **Town Growth +6%**.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/transport-fever-3/transport-fever-3-headquarters-growth-bonus.webp"
    alt="Transport Fever 3 Headquarters extension showing a plus six percent town growth bonus"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Headquarters modules can add a direct growth bonus, but they amplify an existing town network rather than replacing passenger transport or cargo supply.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

That distinction is important.

A growth percentage does not repair a town with no useful transport or no
requested goods. I treat the Headquarters as an accelerator after the basic
network is already functioning.

Company ranks determine which Headquarters pieces and other tools are available,
but the progression system itself belongs with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">company economy and progression</Link>.


<h2
  id="environment"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Noise and Pollution Can Become the Next Limit After Supplies Improve
</h2>

Transport can solve one town problem while creating another.

Chiswick reached a point where its supplies were strong and the town had already
leveled up, yet noise was still causing unhappiness.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/transport-fever-3/transport-fever-3-town-noise-rating.webp"
    alt="Transport Fever 3 town panel showing strong supplies while noise remains a weaker town rating"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Once transport and Supplies are strong, noise can become the rating that limits further improvement.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Dense development makes infrastructure placement matter more.

One Road Depot shown during the campaign produced **60 noise and 90 pollution**.
Putting infrastructure like that deep inside the town creates a different
tradeoff from placing it near the edge.

The same issue becomes more noticeable over time because the town can grow
toward infrastructure that was originally isolated.

Something placed safely outside a hamlet may end up surrounded by development
years later.

When noise or pollution becomes the weak rating, first identify **which
infrastructure is creating it**. The road and junction changes used to reduce
traffic pressure belong in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road network</Link>.

From the town-growth side, the important part is recognizing that more transport
is not always more growth when the environmental cost is increasing at the same
time.


<h2
  id="reputation"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Better Infrastructure Can Still Cost Reputation While You Build It
</h2>

A mature town sometimes needs surgery.

A wider road, new station, or redesigned passenger network can require
demolishing buildings. The placement preview can show a reputation loss even
when the finished transport system will work better.

Warwick reached that point after its original passenger layout no longer matched
the larger town. Rebuilding the network meant removing parts of the old setup
instead of simply adding vehicles.

The reputation hit is easier to justify when the change fixes a lasting problem.

It is much harder to justify for cosmetic cleanup or a route that could have
worked without demolition.

Before removing development, check whether the transport problem can be solved
with a smaller change. Once the demolition happens, the new infrastructure
should improve something substantial enough to make the temporary loss
worthwhile.


<h2
  id="landmarks"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Use Landmarks Against a Specific Municipal Weakness
</h2>

Landmarks are not interchangeable growth bonuses.

Their effects target particular town problems.

Examples include:

- the **Amphitheater**, which reduces road-vehicle noise within its municipal
  area;
- **White Herring Castle**, which reduces train pollution within its area;
- the **Taj Mahal**, which reduces aircraft noise.

That makes the current town rating more important than the fact that a landmark
has unlocked.

A town with zero useful passenger transport does not need an Amphitheater first.
A town whose road noise remains weak after the transport network is otherwise
healthy is a much better candidate.

Some landmarks also require a substantial logistics commitment. One
Amphitheater required **800 stone**.

The cost of supplying the landmark still matters. Do not create a large new
logistics network to solve a minor rating that was not materially holding the
town back.


<h2
  id="marketing"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Marketing Is a Temporary Boost, Not a Permanent Fix
</h2>

The **Marketing Campaign** provides a different kind of town support.

It lasts **1 year**, has a **5-year cooldown**, and boosts town reputation while
encouraging more residents to use public transport.

That makes timing important.

A town with spare passenger capacity can make useful use of the extra demand.
A town already leaving crowds behind at every stop may simply create a larger
queue.

The reputation effect has the same limitation.

Marketing can temporarily improve the town, but it does not remove the cause of
repeated demolition, heavy pollution, or poor transport coverage.

Use it after the underlying network is ready to benefit from the temporary
increase.


<h2
  id="why-town-not-growing"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Why Is My Town Not Growing?
</h2>

Do not answer that question from the map view alone.

Open the town panel and find the first rating that does not match what you
expected.

### The town is Stagnant

Start with passenger transport and requested cargo. A settlement with neither
has two basic growth inputs missing.

### Public transport is busy but growth is still weak

Check the Supplies bars. Busy passenger lines do not mean the commercial and
industrial districts are receiving their requested goods.

### Cargo is arriving but the town still shows poor supply

Check where the cargo is being delivered. It has to reach the buildings that
actually consume it.

### Growth improved, then stopped again

The town may have expanded beyond the original passenger coverage, or a new
cargo demand may have appeared after the settlement leveled up.

### Supplies look strong but the overall rating is falling

Noise, pollution, or reputation has probably become more important than adding
another transport service.

### A recent rebuild made the town worse

Look at the reputation cost of demolition and the environmental effect of the
new infrastructure before assuming the new route needs more vehicles.

### The town is growing but has not leveled up yet

Give it time. Town levels follow sustained population growth rather than one
successful delivery.

The useful response is usually visible in the town panel before it is visible
in the skyline. Fix the rating that changed, let the town react, and only then
decide whether another major project is necessary.
