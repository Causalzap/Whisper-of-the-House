<section
  id="quick-answer"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  <GuideQuickAnswer title="How Do Production Chains Work in Transport Fever 3?">

  Start with the cargo you actually need and work backward through its required
  inputs. Raw producers such as logging camps, mines, farms, and oil sources can
  create their base cargo without another industry feeding them. Processing
  industries need their listed inputs, while cargo boosters and workers increase
  production rather than replacing those required materials.

  When a chain stalls, check the factory's **input stock first, then its output
  stock**. If a required input keeps running out, work upstream. If finished
  cargo keeps piling up, production is working and the bottleneck has moved
  downstream.

  </GuideQuickAnswer>
</section>


<div className="my-4 text-sm text-slate-400">
  Last updated: October 3, 2026.
</div>


Transport Fever 3 production chains become much easier to manage once you stop
treating every industry as an isolated building. Finished cargo can depend on
several raw producers, intermediate factories, and transport legs, and each part
of that chain can become the limiting step.

I usually start at the destination and work backward. If a town needs
**Machines**, the Machine Factory needs **Steel and Plastic**. Steel leads back
to **Iron Ore and Coal**, while Plastic leads through **Chemicals** and
ultimately **Crude Oil**. Once those dependencies are clear, the real job is
keeping each stage supplied at roughly the rate the next one can use it.

There is one important distinction before building anything: not every cargo
shown around an industry is a required ingredient. Raw producers can accept
Machines, Tools, or Fertilizer as production boosters, and workers can also
increase production speed. Those are useful only when production itself is
actually the bottleneck.

<h2
  id="required-inputs-vs-boosters"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Required Inputs and Production Boosters Are Different
</h2>

A required input is consumed as part of the recipe. An Oil Refinery therefore
needs Crude Oil before it can make Fuel, Fertilizer, and Chemicals.

A booster behaves differently. It makes an industry that can already produce
work faster.

Crop Farms make this distinction easy to see. Their fields can generate Grain
and Vegetables without Fertilizer being delivered first. Fertilizer increases
production speed, and workers can increase it further.

<h2
  id="workers"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Workers Can Move the Bottleneck Instead of Removing It
</h2>

Workers are useful when an industry is genuinely production-limited, but a
successful boost can expose the next weak part of the chain.

In one forestry setup, the Saw Mill had enough workers to receive a strong
production-speed boost. The immediate problem then changed: Logs were being
consumed faster than the upstream route could replace them.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image

That is the pattern worth watching. Increasing production may turn an industry
problem into an **input transport problem**, or it may produce cargo faster than
the outbound route can remove it.

After adding workers or a cargo booster, check the stocks again instead of
assuming the whole chain is now balanced.


<h2
  id="how-to-read-industry-panel"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Read the Industry Panel Before Changing the Network
</h2>

Most production problems can be narrowed down from the industry panel before you
follow individual trucks or trains.

The most useful values are the production cycle, required input stock, finished
output stock, boosters, and the connections showing where cargo can come from or
go next.

An empty number by itself is not enough to diagnose a problem. What matters is
how that number behaves while the game is running.

If a required input repeatedly reaches zero and production pauses, look
upstream. If the input remains available while finished cargo accumulates, the
factory is doing its job and the problem has moved downstream.

The supplier and destination controls are also much faster than scanning a
large map manually. Use them to find where the next required material can come
from or where the current output is accepted.


<h2
  id="input-stock-empty"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  An Empty Input Stock Does Not Always Mean the Chain Is Broken
</h2>

A factory can consume cargo almost as soon as it arrives, so its input stock may
hover near zero even while production continues.

The important distinction is whether the production cycle keeps moving.

If production repeatedly pauses because the required material has run out,
check whether the previous producer has cargo waiting. If it does, the transport
leg between the two industries is too weak. If the producer itself is empty,
then the shortage begins farther upstream.

Typical causes include too little vehicle capacity, an unnecessarily long route,
or a raw producer that cannot generate cargo as quickly as the factory consumes
it.

If cargo is already waiting but refuses to load, unload, transfer, or reach the
correct terminal, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry guide</Link>
for the transport side of the problem.


<h2
  id="output-stock-full"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A Growing Output Stock Usually Points Downstream
</h2>

If the required inputs keep arriving and finished cargo begins filling the
industry's output stock, adding more upstream vehicles solves the wrong problem.

At that point, check whether the next route has enough capacity, whether its
vehicles can carry the cargo, whether they are queuing at one terminal, and
whether the destination actually accepts that output.

This becomes especially important at industries that create more than one
product. Each output can have a completely different destination and a
different transport bottleneck.


<h2
  id="forestry-chain"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Forestry Example: Logs to Planks
</h2>

Forestry is a useful first chain because the dependency is easy to follow:

**Logging Camp → Logs → Saw Mill → Planks → Town**

The Logging Camp can produce Logs on its own. The Saw Mill cannot produce its
main outputs until Logs arrive.

Its base recipe is:

**4 Logs → 8 Planks + 4 Sawdust**

  <Image
    src="/images/transport-fever-3/transport-fever-3-sawmill-logs-planks-recipe.webp"
    alt="Transport Fever 3 Saw Mill production panel showing logs as the input and planks as an output"
    width={400}
    height={100}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The Saw Mill turns incoming Logs into Planks and Sawdust, so both inbound Logs and outbound cargo capacity can become bottlenecks.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

That creates two separate questions. Can the Logging Camp and inbound route keep
the Saw Mill supplied, and can the outbound network remove its finished cargo
quickly enough?

I prefer to get both legs working before trying to optimize away empty return
trips. A clever combined route is not an improvement if its longer cycle leaves
the Saw Mill waiting for Logs.

When Planks are going into a town, also check that the unload point covers the
industrial district requesting them. Correct cargo delivered outside the
required catchment will not fix the town's supply rating.

**6 Chemicals → 2 Dyes + 5 Plastic**

This is why I do not build every possible refinery branch before the first oil

stocks. If Chemicals are piling up while Fuel is moving cleanly, the next
investment belongs on the Chemical branch rather than the Crude Oil route.


<h2
  id="steel-machines-chain"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Read Steel and Machine Chains Backward
</h2>

Machines are easier to plan from the finished product backward.

The Machine Factory uses:

**7 Steel + 4 Plastic → 7 Machines**

Steel comes from:

**6 Iron Ore + 6 Coal → 3 Steel + 4 Sheet Metal**

Plastic comes from:

**6 Chemicals → 2 Dyes + 5 Plastic**

and those Chemicals ultimately come from Crude Oil.

A Machine Factory can therefore depend on Iron Ore, Coal, and Oil networks that
are nowhere near one another on the map.

Do not try to give every branch identical transport capacity. Watch which
required input is actually disappearing. If Steel continues to accumulate while
Plastic stays empty, expanding the Iron Ore and Coal side will add cost without
raising Machine production.

<h2
  id="production-chain-diagnosis"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Diagnose a Broken Production Chain in This Order
</h2>

When a chain stops progressing, start at the industry that is not giving you the
output you expect and read its stocks before rebuilding anything.

<GuideTable
  columns={diagnosisColumns}
  rows={diagnosisRows}
/>


Only add vehicles after you know which part of the chain is limiting throughput.
Extra vehicles can turn a production problem into terminal congestion while
leaving the original shortage untouched.


<h2
  id="production-ratios-and-route-capacity"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Production Ratios Are Not a Fixed Fleet-Size Calculator
</h2>

The recipe numbers tell you how inputs and outputs balance inside an industry.
They do not tell you a universal number of trucks, wagons, or ships to buy.

Production speed can change as raw industries expand or receive workers and
boosters. Route length, vehicle speed, loading time, terminal queues,
maintenance, and terrain all change how much cargo a transport line can move.

Use the stocks as feedback.

If Crude Oil accumulates before the refinery while the refinery is consuming it
quickly, the inbound route may need more capacity. If Chemicals pile up after
production, strengthen the Chemical route instead. If neither side is building a
large backlog and vehicles are running well loaded, the chain is much closer to
balance.


<h2
  id="when-to-use-warehouses"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Add Warehouses Where the Network Needs a Buffer
</h2>

Warehouses are useful where several transport modes meet, where a high-output
factory needs buffer space, or where multiple downstream routes draw from one
location. They are not a mandatory step between every pair of industries.

A Logging Camp already has usable cargo access, so a simple road chain can move
Logs directly toward the Saw Mill.

An offshore oil network is different. Several Oil Platforms can feed ships into
one coastal transfer point before a higher-capacity rail route takes Crude Oil
to an inland refinery. That is a natural place for liquid storage because the
warehouse is solving a real transfer and buffering problem.

If production is working but cargo will not load, unload, transfer, or reach the
correct stop, continue with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="production-profit"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  More Production Is Only Useful When the Network Can Use It
</h2>

A successful booster does not automatically make a company more profitable.

Faster Forestry production may require another Log vehicle, more outbound
capacity, or a worker route whose running cost has to be justified. Oil and
Machine chains multiply that effect because each additional production stage
creates another transport leg.

I expand production when the existing cargo is actually being consumed rather
than because an industry panel shows another possible booster or destination.

If the chain works technically but the company is still losing money, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">economy and money guide</Link>
to check route profitability, fleet cost, loans, and overbuilt capacity.


<h2
  id="production-chain-rule"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  The Rule I Use for Every Production Chain
</h2>

Work backward from demand, build the smallest functional version of each
required leg, and then let the stocks tell you where to expand.

A required input that repeatedly runs out sends you upstream. Finished cargo
that keeps accumulating sends you downstream. A raw producer that is constantly
being emptied may benefit from workers or a cargo booster, while one sitting on
hundreds of units usually needs better transport instead.

That same method works from a simple Logs → Planks route all the way through
Steel, Chemicals, Plastic, Machines, and the longer manufacturing chains.
