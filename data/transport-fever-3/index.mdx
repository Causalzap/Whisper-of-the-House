Transport Fever 3 gives you more things to build than you need at any one
moment.

A new locomotive can unlock while the first passenger line is still half empty.
A subsidy can point toward an industry on the other side of the map. Another
town can look tempting even though the settlement you already serve is missing
cargo, losing money, or barely connected by public transport.

The useful question is not what became available. It is what the network has
stopped doing well.

That distinction matters from the first town to a mature company. An empty
factory, a crowded stop, trucks backing through a town center, and trains
waiting outside a station can all look like capacity problems from a distance,
but they do not need the same solution.


<h2
  id="first-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Build Around One Town Before You Build Around the Map
</h2>

Pause before spending heavily and inspect the town you want to use as your
starting base. Its districts and Supplies tell you more about a useful opening
than the vehicle catalogue does.

Give residents a practical connection between the important parts of town
first. Keep the initial passenger fleet small, let it make several trips, and
watch what happens at the stops. A growing queue is a reason to think about more
capacity; vehicles circulating with empty seats are not.

Cargo can come next. Pick one nearby good the town already wants rather than
opening the most complicated production chain on the map. A short freight line
lets you learn how cargo reaches the town without consuming the rest of the
opening budget.

If you are still deciding where to place the first stops, where the depot
belongs, or what Capacity, Utilization, Frequency, Rate, and Balance are telling
you, start with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">Transport Fever 3 beginner guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="money-or-flow"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When the First Network Stalls, Decide Whether Money or Flow Is the Problem
</h2>

A company can show several million in cash and still be in a weak position if
most of that money came from borrowing and the next project cannot be completed
far enough to earn anything.

If construction is stopping because the company cannot afford the whole
connection, stay with the finances. Price the project through its first useful
trip, not just the first station or terminal. Existing line profit, loan
payments, recurring vehicle costs, subsidies, and the cash left after
construction matter more than the number shown in the bank account.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">money and economy guide</Link>
covers the decisions that follow from that situation: how much to borrow, when a
line is actually profitable, when replacement makes sense, how subsidies and
debt change the budget, and when the company can safely start another large
project.

A different problem appears when the money is available but cargo still refuses
to move correctly.

One factory input can overflow while another never arrives. Vehicles can reach
a terminal but be unable to carry the required cargo. Storage can fill because
the next stage is missing something else, and an industry that recently
increased production can outgrow a line that had been perfectly adequate before.

In that situation, trace the missing material backward rather than adding
vehicles everywhere. The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry guide</Link>
goes through terminals, vehicle compatibility, production ratios, worker
transport, warehouses, transfers, loading, unloading, and industry throughput.


<h2
  id="road-or-rail"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When Capacity Runs Out, Find Out Whether the Road or the Transport Mode Is the Limit
</h2>

A successful route eventually creates its own next decision.

Passenger stops may keep filling after another coach is added. A freight route
may require enough trucks that local buses and deliveries start spending their
time behind long-haul traffic. Those are signs that the original connection has
proved its value, but they do not automatically mean every busy road should be
replaced by rail.

Start with the road when the vehicles are doing useful work but moving through
the network badly. Long-distance freight may be cutting through the town center.
A bypass may exist but need a waypoint before trucks use it. A roundabout may
have enough physical space but still allow lane movements that force vehicles
across one another.

For bypasses, waypoints, junctions, lane control, traffic lights, bus lanes,
maintenance coverage, trams, underground passenger sections, and queues that
actually begin at a cargo terminal, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road guide</Link>.

Rail becomes more attractive when the long-distance movement itself has
outgrown road transport.

Once trains enter the network, buying more capacity is only part of the job.
Crossovers determine which tracks and platforms can be reached. A signal facing
the wrong way can produce No Path even when the rails look connected. Faster
trains can spend their journey trapped behind slower services, while an extra
station platform adds nothing if the approach cannot reach it.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link>
covers the move from road to rail, station placement, double track, crossovers,
signal direction, depot access, train consists, mixed-speed traffic,
electrification, and station bottlenecks.


<h2
  id="town-changes"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A Town That Grows Successfully Can Create a Different Problem Later
</h2>

The network that helped a small settlement grow does not stay correct forever.

New development can spread beyond the original stop coverage. A town with
excellent passenger transport can still be missing requested cargo. Noise may
only become the weak rating after buses, trams, depots, and freight routes have
become busy. Infrastructure that began comfortably outside town can eventually
have new development growing toward it.

When growth slows, open the town panel before rebuilding the transport network.
Passenger coverage, Supplies, noise, pollution, and reputation point toward
different causes.

Headquarters bonuses, landmarks, and marketing can strengthen a town that is
already functioning, but they do not replace the passenger and cargo
connections underneath it. The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/city-growth-guide">city growth guide</Link>
covers stagnant settlements, town levels, Headquarters growth effects,
environmental ratings, reputation, landmarks, marketing, and towns that stop
responding after years of expansion.


<h2
  id="campaign"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Campaign Missions Keep Changing What the Network Has to Solve
</h2>

Free play gives you time to recover from an expensive experiment. Campaign
objectives are less forgiving because the condition that matters can change
completely from one mission to the next.

**Saving Mardi Gras** is mostly sequential: clear the logging camp, repair the
road, move the required wood, extend the passenger service, and finish the
parade without turning every temporary task into permanent infrastructure.

**Alpine Crossing** makes time the constraint. The railway has to move
**300 passengers through the tunnel**, so unnecessary construction works
directly against the tighter completion target.

**Desert Adventure** turns vehicle condition into part of route planning. A road
can be connected from end to end and still be unsafe when trucks spend too long
between maintenance areas.

**Biggest Festival Ever** stacks several jobs onto the same campground. Stage
construction, food, medicine, sewage, road traffic, storm supplies, passenger
growth, the instrument search, and the final helicopter connection all compete
for attention.

**Oil for the People** is a live race against Astrid. Prospecting and platform
construction matter only because they feed the larger objective: move
**1,000 oil** to the inland refinery before the competing operation gets there
first.

**Island Expansion** changes the rhythm again. Contracts are timed, several
islands have to share the same growing network, and the three star thresholds
come from completing **5, 8, and 12 subsidiary contracts**. The
Trans-Filippine Railroad runs alongside those contracts as a separate long-term
objective.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/campaign-walkthrough">campaign walkthrough</Link>
covers Missions 1–6 with their required quantities, objective order, star
conditions, special awards, and the specific steps that can stop each scenario
from progressing.


<h2
  id="later-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Do Not Treat Every New Transport Option as the Next Upgrade
</h2>

As the years advance, the company gains access to more vehicles, larger
infrastructure, better trains, trams, underground passenger stops, maritime
transport, landmarks, and other ways to move or support the network.

An unlock is an option, not evidence that the current system needs replacing.

A newer truck matters when it can remove several older vehicles from a crowded
road. A tram matters when a local passenger corridor has outgrown the smaller
fleet. Electrification matters when the trains using that corridor can benefit
from it. A warehouse matters when cargo genuinely needs a transfer or storage
stage. Ships become useful when water connects places more naturally than a
costly land route.

The same test applies to every later investment: give it a job before giving it
a budget.

If the current service is still moving what it needs to move without creating a
new bottleneck, there is no need to replace it simply because another option
has unlocked. Let the network show you what has stopped working, then spend on
that problem.
