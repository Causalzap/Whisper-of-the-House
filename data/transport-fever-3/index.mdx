The difficult part of Transport Fever 3 is not building a route
between two places. It is deciding whether that connection is
worth the money, what should travel along it, and whether the
rest of the network can handle the additional traffic.

Early on, a bus may be enough to connect two parts of town.
A few years later, the same corridor might need trams because
passengers are waiting at every stop. A growing factory creates
a different problem: its production may be increasing while
the freight vehicles still carry the same amount. Eventually,
adding more trucks can make deliveries slower because they
are blocking one another at the same terminal.

I look at the part of the network where progress has stopped
before changing anything. Cargo accumulating at a station,
empty factory input storage, passengers waiting for transport,
and vehicles queuing outside a junction all point toward
different problems. The useful upgrade depends on which one
is actually happening.

<h2
  id="first-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  What Should You Build First in Transport Fever 3?
</h2>

A small, working connection is worth more than several
unfinished routes.

Choose a town with nearby demand that can be served without
expensive infrastructure. Connect useful passenger stops,
assign a modest number of vehicles, and watch what happens
after several journeys. The route needs time to demonstrate
that passengers can reach it and are willing to use it.

If passengers begin waiting between departures, the service
may need more capacity. If the vehicles remain mostly empty,
check stop coverage and the route itself before increasing
the fleet.

Cargo offers another practical opening. A nearby farm supplying
a town can establish freight income without requiring several
factories to operate together. A more complicated industrial
chain introduces additional places where one missing delivery
can stop the entire network.

The first investment should leave enough money for a depot,
vehicles, and any corrections needed after the line starts
operating.

If you are still setting up your first transport service,
the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">beginner guide</Link> covers what to build first, stop placement, depots, and
the early decisions that determine whether a line can
carry passengers or cargo.

<h2
  id="money-or-profit"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Why Are You Losing Money Even With Vehicles Running?
</h2>

A busy-looking network can lose money for several reasons.

Perhaps the initial railway cost more than expected and
there is not enough cash left to purchase a complete train.
Another possibility is that the service works but carries
too few passengers to justify its vehicles. Freight can
be more misleading because goods transferred into
intermediate storage do not produce an immediate payment.

The important distinction is between a temporary investment
and a recurring loss.

When I see a negative line balance, I check whether the
vehicles are carrying useful demand and whether the
connection has had enough time to operate normally. A
new freight service waiting for the rest of its delivery
chain to become functional is different from an established
passenger route that still runs almost empty.

The same caution applies when borrowing. Cash from a
loan is available to spend, but it is not income earned
by the company.

Before another major investment, compare what the
existing network earns with its vehicle costs and
debt obligations. The <Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">money and economy guide</Link> explains cargo payments, line profit, loan terms,
subsidies, maintenance costs, and when repayment is
safer than further expansion.

<h2
  id="cargo-not-moving"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Why Is Cargo Not Loading, Unloading, or Reaching a Warehouse?
</h2>

An industry can have goods ready for collection while
the vehicles assigned to it keep leaving empty.

The first thing to determine is whether the cargo
actually exists at the pickup point. If it does,
the problem is likely somewhere between the producer,
the loading terminal, the vehicle, and the next
destination.

A few situations are particularly useful to distinguish:

- **Goods remain at the producer:** The pickup stop may not reach the building, or the vehicle and line may not be configured for that cargo.
- **A loaded vehicle returns without unloading:** The receiving building may be full, outside terminal coverage, or unable to accept the intended delivery.
- **Goods reach a warehouse but go no farther:** Check the storage connection and whether another line can collect the cargo.
- **Trucks queue outside a terminal:** The loading positions may be restricting the flow rather than the number of vehicles.

Do not add more trucks until the first vehicle can
complete the intended movement.

Warehouses are useful where several routes or transport
modes genuinely need to exchange goods. They do not
automatically repair an invalid connection, and adding
another transfer creates another place where cargo
can become stuck.

For cargo loading settings, compatible vehicles,
warehouse connections, terminal coverage, and moving
goods through several transport stages, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide"> cargo and industry guide</Link>.

<h2
  id="production-problems"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Why Has an Industry Stopped Producing?
</h2>

A factory with no output is not necessarily waiting
for another freight vehicle.

Look inside the industry first. If a required input
is empty, production can stop even while other
materials are arriving in large quantities. Increasing
the already sufficient delivery only fills more
storage.

The opposite situation is an industry producing
normally while its finished goods accumulate.
That points toward the outbound transport connection,
not the factory itself.

Production improvements can change the balance again.
Workers and certain cargo boosters may increase
output, meaning the transport lines that were adequate
earlier can become too small.

For example, improving a sawmill does not help indefinitely
if the incoming Logs supply cannot keep up with its
increased consumption.

Before increasing transport capacity, identify whether
the building lacks a required input, has reached
a production limit, or cannot move its finished goods.
The <Link href="/transport-fever-3/production-chains">production chains guide</Link> covers industry recipes, required materials, workers,
boosters, and the reasons production stops or
fails to increase.

<h2
  id="road-capacity"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  How Do You Fix Traffic Jams Without Adding More Trucks?
</h2>

A profitable freight route can eventually create
its own delays.

At first, a few trucks move smoothly between the
industry and town. As production increases, additional
vehicles begin using the same streets. They may
interfere with buses, queue at cargo terminals,
or converge on a junction that cannot process
the growing traffic.

The location of the queue matters.

If trucks are waiting to enter the same loading
position, the cargo terminal is the immediate limit.
If vehicles leave the terminal but stop at the
next intersection, the road layout is now holding
them back.

A bypass only helps when vehicles can use it
to avoid the congested movement. Simply constructing
a wider road somewhere nearby does not guarantee
that traffic will redistribute.

I would separate long-distance freight from local
town traffic where that produces a shorter, clearer
route. Passenger corridors also become easier
to manage when they are not constantly interrupted
by heavy deliveries.

When you need to change junctions, lane use, waypoints,
traffic lights, bypasses, trams, or underground transport,
continue with the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road guide</Link>.

<h2
  id="road-to-rail"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When Should You Switch From Trucks or Buses to Trains?
</h2>

Rail becomes attractive when an established corridor
has enough demand to justify a larger investment.

A growing queue of passengers between two towns
may support a railway once buses can no longer
carry everyone efficiently. Freight presents
a similar opportunity when a long industrial
connection requires so many trucks that road
traffic and operating costs become difficult
to manage.

The important part is knowing what the railway
will carry before construction begins.

A train cannot fix missing passenger access
at its stations. It also cannot reach an
assigned platform when the required crossover
does not exist.

Once multiple trains share a corridor, the
problems change. Opposing services may need
passing loops, faster trains can become trapped
behind slower traffic, and incorrectly placed
or oriented signals can prevent a valid route.

If a train reports **No Path**, inspect its
physical connections and signal restrictions
before adding more track or vehicles.

The <Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link> explains station approaches, path signals, passing
loops, depots, crossovers, electrification,
and trains waiting at red signals.

<h2
  id="town-growth"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Why Has Your Town Stopped Growing?
</h2>

A transport network that supported a town
for several years can become insufficient
as the settlement expands.

New buildings may appear beyond the coverage
of existing passenger stops. Additional
demand can develop for goods that the
freight network does not yet deliver.
Roads and industries that once stood
outside the residential area may eventually
be surrounded by housing.

That last change can introduce noise
and pollution problems without any major
alteration to the original infrastructure.

When growth slows, open the town's information
rather than purchasing more vehicles immediately.
Determine which condition is weakest and
whether a transport improvement can actually
change it.

Better connections can improve passenger access
and deliveries. They cannot, by themselves,
solve every environmental or reputation
problem affecting the settlement.

Company Headquarters effects, landmarks,
and marketing may also become relevant
as the town develops, but those investments
make more sense after its essential
transport and supply requirements work.

The <Link href="/transport-fever-3/city-growth-guide">city growth guide</Link> covers town levels, supply ratings, population
growth, environmental conditions, reputation,
and what to change when development stops.

<h2
  id="campaign"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  How Do You Get Past a Stuck Campaign Mission?
</h2>

The eight campaign missions use familiar
transport systems, but each changes which
part of the network matters most.

**Saving Mardi Gras** begins with a sequence
of local tasks rather than a large transport
economy. **Alpine Crossing** adds the pressure
of completing railway work and passenger
movements before a deadline.

Later missions become less forgiving.

In **Desert Adventure**, sending trucks far
beyond maintenance coverage can cost you
the no-truck-loss star. **Oil for the People**
becomes a delivery race against Astrid,
while **Island Expansion** asks you to
complete contracts without wasting their
limited time on unfinished infrastructure.

**Big City Problems** rewards improving
several dissatisfied districts quickly
enough to win voter support. The final
mission, **Final Countdown**, depends
on industrial supplies arriving at the
launch site before the schedule expires.

When an objective stops progressing,
read the exact requirement before
building another transport line. Some
tasks depend on moving a specified
quantity, while others require an
interaction, a particular connection,
or enough time for the objective to
register.

The <Link href="/transport-fever-3/campaign-walkthrough">campaign walkthrough for all eight missions</Link> covers the objective sequence, required quantities,
star conditions, special awards, and
specific obstacles in each scenario.

<h2

  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Which Transport Fever 3 Achievements Should You Complete First?
</h2>

Transport Fever 3 has **41 achievements**.

Several unlock naturally while establishing
your first company. Early passenger trips,
cargo deliveries, town development, company
progression, and subsidies can all contribute
to completion without requiring a separate
network.

Later achievements are more demanding.
Some involve campaign results, while others
require substantial passenger or cargo
totals, different climates, particular
industries, or uncommon vehicle and
construction setups.

I would not disrupt a profitable young
company just to complete a requirement
that will be much easier after more
vehicles, technologies, and money
become available.

Keep track of what has already been
completed as the company grows. The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/achievements">41-achievement checklist</Link> lets you mark completed requirements, monitor
your progress, and identify which
remaining tasks need a campaign result,
a different climate, or a specific
transport setup.

<h2
  id="later-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  What Should You Upgrade as Your Transport Network Gets Bigger?
</h2>

Later years introduce more powerful
locomotives, larger road vehicles,
trams, ships, aircraft, improved
infrastructure, and additional
company options.

The mistake is treating every new
unlock as an immediate replacement
for what already works.

A larger truck is valuable when it
can carry the same freight with fewer
vehicles on a congested road.
A tram becomes useful when a busy
passenger corridor needs more capacity
than buses can provide. Electrification
matters once a railway has suitable
trains and enough demand to benefit
from the change.

Ships and aircraft solve different
geographical problems. Water transport
can connect places that are expensive
to reach by land, while aircraft can
serve distant destinations without
building a continuous surface route.
Neither should be purchased simply
because it is faster or newer.

The same judgment applies to expanding
terminals, adding platforms, and
replacing vehicle fleets.

When a working route begins to fall
behind demand, find out whether the
limit is its vehicle capacity, travel
time, infrastructure, or destination.
Buying more powerful vehicles may help
one of those problems while leaving
the others unchanged.

And when an older service is still
profitable, reaches the required
destinations, and has room for more
passengers or cargo, it can remain
in operation.

There is no benefit in rebuilding
that connection until the replacement
can perform a useful job that
the current network cannot handle.
