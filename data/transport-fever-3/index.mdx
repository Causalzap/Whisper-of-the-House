Transport Fever 3 can make expansion feel urgent long before the first network
actually needs it.

A new locomotive may unlock while the first bus route is still half empty. A
subsidy can pull your attention toward an industry several towns away. Another
settlement may look ready for development even though the town you already serve
is still missing Supplies, struggling with weak passenger coverage, or barely
using the vehicles you bought for it.

Those opportunities do not all deserve money at the same time.

A growing queue, an unfinished cargo chain, a road full of trucks, a train
waiting outside a station, and a factory with full input storage can all look
like capacity problems from a distance. One may need another vehicle. Another
may need a better route. A third may already have enough transport and simply
be waiting on production.

The network becomes much easier to manage when expansion follows a visible
problem instead of an unlock.


<h2
  id="first-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Build Around One Town Before You Build Around the Map
</h2>

Pause before committing much of the starting money and inspect the town you want
to serve first. Its districts and Supplies tell you what people need nearby,
while stop coverage shows whether a proposed passenger route will actually
reach the places residents use.

Give the town one practical passenger connection and keep the opening fleet
small. Let those vehicles complete several trips before judging the route. Stops
that begin filling tell you the service has demand; coaches circulating with
empty seats tell you something very different.

Cargo can follow once that passenger network has settled. Choose a nearby good
the town already requests instead of opening the largest production chain on the
map. The first freight line is more useful when it starts delivering quickly and
leaves enough cash to correct a mistake.

If you are still deciding where the opening stops belong, where to place the
Road Depot, or what Capacity, Utilization, Frequency, Rate, and Balance mean in
the Line Manager, start with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">Transport Fever 3 beginner guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="money-or-flow"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When the First Network Stalls, Decide Whether Money or Flow Is the Problem
</h2>

A healthy bank balance does not automatically mean the company can afford its
next project. Borrowed money may already be committed to unfinished track,
stations, terminals, and vehicles that are not yet earning anything.

When construction keeps stopping because the company cannot finish a usable
connection, calculate the project through its first revenue-producing trip.
Loan payments, recurring vehicle costs, subsidies, replacement spending, and
the cash remaining after construction matter more than the balance shown before
you start building.

If the problem is financial rather than physical, the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">money and economy guide</Link>
covers starting loans, line profit, recurring costs, subsidies, debt, Company
Rank, vehicle replacement, and the point where another expansion becomes
affordable.

Cargo failures look different.

A factory may have plenty of one input while another never arrives. Trucks can
reach the correct terminal but carry the wrong cargo type. A warehouse can fill
because the next stage of the chain is not moving quickly enough. An industry
can also grow beyond a line that handled its earlier production without any
difficulty.

When that happens, follow the missing material backward through the chain before
adding vehicles. For industry terminals, vehicle compatibility, production
ratios, worker transport, warehouses, transfers, loading, unloading, and
throughput problems, continue with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="road-or-rail"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When Capacity Runs Out, Decide Whether the Road or the Transport Mode Is the Limit
</h2>

A successful road route eventually creates a harder question than simply adding
another vehicle.

Passenger stops can keep filling after another coach enters service. A freight
line can require enough trucks that its own success starts slowing local buses
and deliveries. That proves the connection has demand, but it does not
automatically mean the route should become a railway.

Stay with the road network when the vehicles themselves are still appropriate
but their paths are poor. Long-distance freight may be cutting through a town
center unnecessarily. A bypass may exist but remain unused until a waypoint
changes the route. A large roundabout may still jam because lane movements force
vehicles across one another.

For bypasses, waypoints, junctions, lane control, traffic lights, bus lanes,
road maintenance, trams, underground passenger sections, and queues whose real
cause is farther ahead at a cargo terminal, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road guide</Link>.

Rail becomes more attractive when a proven long-distance corridor has outgrown
what road vehicles can move cleanly.

At that point, train capacity is only one part of the decision. Crossovers
control which tracks and platforms are reachable. Signal direction can create a
No Path error on track that appears physically connected. Fast trains can spend
most of their journey behind slower traffic, and an extra station platform does
nothing when the station throat cannot reach it.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link>
covers the move from road to rail, station placement, double track, crossovers,
signals, depot access, train consists, mixed-speed traffic, electrification, and
station bottlenecks.


<h2
  id="town-changes"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A Town That Grows Successfully Can Create a Different Problem Later
</h2>

The transport network that helped a small settlement grow will not remain
correct forever.

New buildings can spread beyond the original stop coverage. A town with strong
passenger transport can still be missing one of its requested Supplies. Noise
may only become the weak rating after buses, trams, depots, trains, and freight
traffic have become busy. Infrastructure that once sat well outside town can
eventually have development growing toward it.

When growth slows, open the town panel before rebuilding anything.

Poor passenger coverage, weak Supplies, noise, pollution, and reputation point
toward different causes. Headquarters bonuses, landmarks, and marketing can
strengthen a town whose basic transport already works, but they do not replace
the passenger and cargo connections underneath it.

For stagnant settlements, town levels, Headquarters growth effects,
environmental ratings, reputation, landmarks, marketing, and towns that stop
responding after years of expansion, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/city-growth-guide">city growth guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="campaign"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Each Campaign Mission Changes What Matters Most
</h2>

Free play gives you time to recover from an expensive experiment. Campaign
missions are less forgiving because the thing holding progress back can change
completely from one scenario to the next.

**Saving Mardi Gras** is largely sequential. Clear the logging camp, restore the
road, move the required wood, extend passenger service, and finish the parade
without turning every temporary objective into permanent infrastructure.

**Alpine Crossing** makes the calendar part of the railway problem. The route
must move **300 passengers through the tunnel**, so track work that does not
improve tunnel throughput works directly against the stronger result.

**Desert Adventure** turns maintenance coverage into route planning. A road can
be connected all the way across the desert and still be unusable when trucks
spend too long outside protected areas.

**Biggest Festival Ever** keeps several temporary systems active around the same
campground. Construction material, food, medicine, sewage, passenger growth,
traffic, storm supplies, the instrument search, and the final helicopter
connection all compete for space and attention.

**Oil for the People** becomes a direct race against Astrid. Prospecting and
platform construction only matter because they feed the larger objective:
deliver **1,000 oil** to the inland refinery before the competing operation
finishes first.

**Island Expansion** is much less linear. Timed contracts appear across several
islands, the star thresholds come from completing **5, 8, and 12 subsidiary
contracts**, and the Trans-Filippine Railroad develops alongside those shorter
jobs.

**Big City Problems** begins with an established Adelaide already suffering from
traffic, noise, weak passenger service, industrial pressure, and delayed cargo.
You do not need all five districts to be perfect at the same time: keeping at
least **3 of the 5 districts satisfied** allows voter support to keep rising
while you repair the rest of the city.

**Final Countdown** turns the last mission into a deadline across several linked
production chains. The launch site needs **400 machines, 150 oxidizer, and 200
fuel**, while factories need workers, the fuel chain changes after the additive
requirement appears, and the strongest result depends on launching without
increasing the mission budget.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/campaign-walkthrough">campaign walkthrough</Link>
covers all eight missions with their objective order, required quantities, star
conditions, special awards, and the problems that can stop each scenario from
progressing.


<h2
  id="later-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Give Later Transport Options a Job Before You Give Them a Budget
</h2>

Later years unlock larger road vehicles, stronger trains, trams, underground
passenger infrastructure, ships, aircraft, bigger terminals, landmarks, and
more ways to reshape the network.

Their value depends on the problem in front of them.

A newer truck matters when its capacity lets you remove several older vehicles
from an overloaded road. A tram matters when a local passenger corridor has
outgrown the smaller fleet. Electrification matters when the trains using that
corridor can benefit from it. A warehouse is useful when cargo genuinely needs
a transfer or storage stage, while a ship can replace an awkward land route
when water already connects the places you need.

Aircraft make the same tradeoff at a larger scale. Mission 8 shows how an
airport connection can move cargo across a long distance quickly, but airport
construction and larger aircraft can consume a large part of the budget. The
fastest-looking transport mode is not automatically the best choice when a
smaller existing connection can meet the deadline for much less money.

Large-city passenger systems follow the same rule. Mission 7 shows how
underground transport can take pressure away from noisy, congested streets, but
building underground everywhere is expensive and unnecessary while surface
capacity is still working.

Before replacing a service, look at what the current one is failing to do. If
cargo is moving, passengers are being collected, vehicles are not creating a new
queue, and the route still has room to grow, another unlock can wait.
