Transport Fever 3 rarely punishes you for lacking another vehicle. It punishes
you for spending money on the wrong part of the network.

A factory can stop because one required material never arrives. Cargo can sit
ready for collection while the wrong vehicle keeps visiting the terminal. A
profitable road corridor can become slow only after its trucks begin blocking
local traffic. A town can have strong passenger service and still stop growing
because one supply or environmental rating has become the weak point.

Those situations may all look like "I need more capacity" from a distance, but
they need very different fixes.

Before buying anything, open the part of the network that is failing and look at
what is actually accumulating, what is empty, and where vehicles are spending
their time. That one habit prevents a large amount of unnecessary rebuilding.


<h2
  id="first-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Start With One Useful Connection, Not the Whole Map
</h2>

The opening map can tempt you into planning rail corridors, distant industries,
multiple towns, and long cargo chains before the first route has earned enough
money to prove itself.

Keep the first network smaller.

Choose a town with a practical nearby connection, place passenger stops where
people can actually reach them, and start with a modest fleet. Several completed
trips will tell you much more than the route preview does. Passengers building up
at stops point toward real demand; nearly empty vehicles point toward a route,
coverage, or frequency problem rather than a need for more capacity.

For the first freight service, a short chain feeding cargo the town already
wants is easier to recover from than a large industrial project spread across
the map. It starts earning sooner and leaves more cash available when a depot,
stop, road, or vehicle choice needs correcting.

When you are still learning depot placement, stop coverage, Line Manager values,
or what to build during the first hour, follow the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">beginner route and first-network setup</Link>
before committing to larger infrastructure.


<h2
  id="money-or-profit"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A Route Can Work and Still Be a Bad Investment
</h2>

The balance at the top of the screen is only part of the financial picture. A
company may still have cash while several unfinished stations, vehicles, and
track sections are consuming money without producing revenue.

Before starting a large connection, think through the project until its first
paid trip. The expensive mistake is not borrowing money by itself; it is using
borrowed money on infrastructure that cannot begin earning before the remaining
cash disappears.

Once a route is operating, its own behavior matters more than the construction
price alone. Empty return trips, oversized fleets, long detours, unnecessary
maintenance infrastructure, and vehicles that spend too much time waiting can
turn a seemingly busy service into a persistent loss.

For starting loans, recurring vehicle costs, route balance, subsidies, debt,
replacement decisions, and deciding when the company can afford another major
project, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">economy and money guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="cargo-not-moving"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When Cargo Exists but Does Not Move, Check the Transport Chain
</h2>

Cargo sitting at an industry or station means something different from an
industry that never produced the cargo in the first place.

When goods already exist, follow their physical route.

Check whether the line reaches the correct loading point, whether the assigned
vehicle can carry that cargo class, whether the next stop accepts or transfers
it, and whether the final stop covers the part of town or industry that needs
it. Warehouses can help when several modes or routes meet, but inserting one
does not fix an incorrect terminal or an invalid destination.

Queues also need context. Ten trucks waiting at a station may look like a fleet
shortage elsewhere, yet adding five more trucks only makes the queue longer when
the terminal itself is the limiting step.

For cargo that will not load, unload, transfer, reach the correct terminal, or
move through a warehouse as expected, continue with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="production-problems"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When an Industry Stops Producing, Read Its Stocks Before Adding Vehicles
</h2>

Production failures begin inside the industry rather than on the road outside it.

Processing industries need their required inputs. Raw producers can work
without another material feeding them, while cargo such as Machines, Tools, or
Fertilizer may increase production instead of acting as mandatory ingredients.
Workers can raise production speed as well.

That creates two very different bottlenecks.

A required input that repeatedly reaches zero can pause the next production
cycle. In that case, work backward toward the missing material. An output stock
that keeps filling means the factory is already producing; the weak point has
moved to the next transport leg.

Boosters can move the bottleneck rather than remove it. A faster Saw Mill, for
example, can begin consuming Logs more quickly than the inbound route can supply
them. Raising production again at that point only makes the imbalance worse.

For complete input and output recipes, production ratios, workers, cargo
boosters, climate-specific industries, and diagnosing empty input or full output
stocks, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/production-chains">production chains and industry production guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="road-capacity"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  More Trucks Are Not Always the Next Road Upgrade
</h2>

A successful road route eventually creates pressure somewhere else.

Long-distance freight can begin cutting through streets that also carry buses
and local traffic. One unloading stop can block a lane every time a truck
arrives. Vehicles may ignore the bypass you built because the original town
route is still shorter. A large junction may have enough lanes on paper but
still force too many vehicles across one another.

Those are road-layout problems, not fleet-size problems.

Adding another truck makes sense when cargo is waiting, the route itself is
moving cleanly, and existing vehicles are consistently full. Once extra vehicles
start creating their own queue, the road network or terminal needs attention
before the fleet grows again.

For bypasses, waypoints, junction design, lane use, traffic lights, bus lanes,
road maintenance, trams, underground passenger sections, and congestion caused
by cargo terminals farther along the route, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="road-to-rail"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Move to Rail When a Proven Corridor Has Outgrown Road Capacity
</h2>

Rail is strongest when it replaces a transport job you already understand.

A busy long-distance corridor with sustained passenger or freight demand gives a
train something useful to do immediately. Building rail first and hoping enough
demand appears later ties up much more capital.

Once trains enter the network, capacity is only half of the job. A platform can
exist but remain unreachable from the approach track. A signal facing the wrong
direction can create a No Path error even when the rails appear connected. Fast
trains lose much of their advantage when they spend the route behind slower
traffic, and adding another platform does little when every movement still
passes through the same constrained station throat.

For double track, crossovers, signal direction, station access, depot
connections, train length, mixed-speed traffic, electrification, and rail
bottlenecks, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="town-growth"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Recheck a Town After It Grows
</h2>

The transport network that helped a small town expand will not remain correct
forever.

New buildings can spread outside the original stop coverage. Passenger demand
changes. Additional cargo can become necessary. Freight traffic that once stayed
well outside the settlement may end up beside new residential development, and
noise or pollution can become the weakest rating only after the town has grown
toward previously isolated infrastructure.

Open the town panel when development slows rather than rebuilding the whole
network from instinct.

Weak passenger coverage, missing supplies, reputation, noise, and pollution are
different limits. Headquarters bonuses, landmarks, and marketing can strengthen
a town, but they cannot replace basic passenger and freight connections that are
still missing.

For town levels, supply ratings, growth limits, Headquarters effects,
environmental pressure, reputation, landmarks, marketing, and settlements that
stop responding after years of expansion, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/city-growth-guide">city growth guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="campaign"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Treat Each Campaign Mission as a New Transport Problem
</h2>

The Campaign uses the same transport tools as Free Game, but the objective that
matters changes from mission to mission.

One scenario may reward clean sequencing with very little permanent
infrastructure. Another puts the calendar against your rail construction. Later
missions introduce vehicle condition, several temporary services competing for
the same roads, a direct cargo race, timed contracts spread across multiple
locations, citywide satisfaction, and finally linked production chains working
toward a deadline.

That makes large prebuilt networks risky. Read the active objective first, build
for the current requirement, and reconsider temporary vehicles once that task is
finished instead of leaving every mission-specific route running indefinitely.

When a mission stalls, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/campaign-walkthrough">all 8 missions campaign walkthrough</Link>
for the objective order, quantities, star conditions, special objectives, and
the blocker specific to the scenario you are playing.


<h2
  id="achievement-progress"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Let Normal Play Clear the Early Achievements Before Chasing the Rare Ones
</h2>

Transport Fever 3 has **41 achievements**, and many of the first ones arrive
through ordinary Free Game progress: moving the first passenger, delivering the
first cargo, gaining a company rank, developing a town, and completing an early
subsidy.

The later requirements ask for a very different scale of play. Some depend on
Campaign completion, medals, and stars. Others build across long-running Free
Games, multiple climates, very large passenger or cargo totals, specific
industry conditions, vehicle collections, or unusual one-off actions.

Do not distort a young company to chase a condition that becomes much cheaper
later. A stable network can keep accumulating passengers, cargo, population,
money, and distance while you work on other goals. Artificial setups are easier
to finish once money and infrastructure are no longer scarce.

Use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/achievements">41-achievement tracker and requirement guide</Link>
when you want to see which remaining objective needs Campaign cleanup, another
climate, a specific setup, or simply more time in a successful save.


<h2
  id="later-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Give Every New Transport Option a Specific Job
</h2>

Later years unlock larger road vehicles, stronger locomotives, trams,
underground passenger infrastructure, ships, aircraft, larger terminals,
landmarks, and more ways to reshape the company.

None of them needs to be adopted just because it became available.

A larger truck earns its place when its extra capacity lets you remove several
older vehicles from a crowded road. A tram becomes useful when a local passenger
corridor has outgrown buses. Electrification matters when the trains on that
corridor can benefit from it. A warehouse makes sense where cargo genuinely
needs storage or a transfer between transport stages.

Ships and aircraft solve the same kind of problem at a different scale. Water
can make an awkward land connection simple, while aircraft can move cargo or
passengers across very long distances quickly. Their infrastructure is more
expensive, so speed alone is not enough reason to replace a cheaper connection
that already meets demand.

Before rebuilding a working service, ask what the current one can no longer do.
When cargo is moving, passengers are being collected, vehicles are not creating
new queues, and the route still has room to absorb growth, the latest unlock can
wait.
