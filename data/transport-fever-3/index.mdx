Transport Fever 3 gives you far more things to build than you need at any one
moment.

A new locomotive can unlock while your first passenger line is still filling.
A subsidy can point toward an industry on the other side of the map. Another
town may look ready for expansion while the settlement you already serve still
has missing cargo and barely any useful local transport.

The important question is not what the game has made available. It is what the
network is failing to do right now.

That distinction keeps the early company small enough to understand and remains
useful much later. An empty factory, a growing passenger queue, trucks backing
through a town center, and trains waiting outside a station can all look like
capacity problems from a distance, but each one needs a different fix.


<h2
  id="first-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Build Around One Town Before You Build Around the Map
</h2>

Pause before spending heavily and open the town you want to use as the first
base. Its districts and Supplies tell you more about the useful opening than the
vehicle catalogue does.

Start by giving residents a practical way to move between the important parts
of town. Keep the first passenger fleet small, let it complete several trips,
and watch what happens at the stops. A growing queue means something very
different from vehicles circulating half empty.

Cargo can follow once the passenger connection has started proving itself.
Choose a nearby requested good rather than the most ambitious production chain
you can see. The first freight route is more useful when it teaches you how the
town accepts cargo without consuming the rest of the opening budget.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">beginner guide</Link>
covers that opening in detail, including stop coverage, depot placement, the
first line metrics, and the point where the initial setup has given you enough
information to stop following a fixed build order.


<h2
  id="money-or-flow"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  After the First Routes Work, Separate a Money Problem From a Transport Problem
</h2>

A company can have several million in cash and still be in a weak position if
the money came from borrowing and most of it is already committed to unfinished
infrastructure.

When construction is stopping because the company cannot afford the complete
next project, stay with the finances. Price the route up to the point where it
can begin earning, not merely the first station or terminal. Existing lines,
loan payments, recurring vehicle costs, subsidies, and the cash left after
construction matter more than the headline bank balance.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">money and economy guide</Link>
covers loans, line profitability, subsidies, vehicle replacement, debt
repayment, and company progression.

A different situation appears when the money is available but cargo still does
not move correctly.

Factories can have one input overflowing while another never arrives. Trucks
can reach a terminal but carry the wrong cargo type. Storage can fill because
the next production stage is missing something else. A line that worked at the
start can also become too small after industry output increases.

That is no longer a financing problem.

For production chains, industry terminals, vehicle compatibility, line-rate
matching, worker transport, warehouses, transfers, and cargo that refuses to
load or unload, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="road-or-rail"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When Capacity Runs Out, Find Out Whether the Road or the Railway Is Actually Limiting You
</h2>

A successful route eventually creates its own next decision.

Passenger stops may keep filling after another coach is added. A freight
connection may require so many trucks that the vehicles begin interfering with
local buses and deliveries. Those are signs that the original service has
proved its demand, not signs that every route now needs a train.

If the road vehicles are doing useful work but choosing bad paths, fix the road
network first. Through-freight does not need to cross the town center when an
outside connection can carry it around. A new bypass may still need a waypoint
before trucks use it. A large roundabout may still fail when lane choices allow
vehicles to cross one another unnecessarily.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road guide</Link>
covers those routing problems, along with junctions, traffic lights, bus lanes,
road maintenance coverage, trams, underground passenger sections, and traffic
queues that actually begin at a cargo terminal.

Rail becomes the stronger question when a proven intercity connection has
outgrown the road mode itself.

Once trains enter the network, vehicle capacity is only part of the problem.
Crossovers decide which tracks and platforms trains can reach. Signal direction
can create a No Path error on physically connected track. A faster service can
spend its trip behind a slower one, and adding another platform does nothing
when the approach cannot reach it.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link>
covers the move from road to rail as well as station placement, double track,
signals, depot access, train consists, mixed-speed traffic, electrification, and
station bottlenecks.


<h2
  id="town-changes"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A Town That Grows Successfully Can Still Create a New Problem
</h2>

The network that helped a small settlement grow does not remain correct forever.

New development can spread beyond the original passenger coverage. A town with
excellent transport can still be missing a requested cargo. Noise may become
the weak rating only after buses, trams, depots, and freight traffic have become
busy. Infrastructure that began outside town can eventually find development
growing toward it.

When growth slows, avoid treating town size as the problem by itself. Open the
town panel and identify what changed.

Passenger coverage, Supplies, noise, pollution, and reputation point toward
different fixes. Headquarters bonuses and landmarks can improve an already
functional settlement, but they do not replace the passenger and cargo network
underneath it.

The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/city-growth-guide">city growth guide</Link>
covers stagnant towns, settlement levels, Headquarters growth bonuses,
environmental ratings, reputation, landmarks, marketing, and towns that stop
responding after years of successful growth.


<h2
  id="campaign"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Campaign Missions Change Which Constraint Matters Most
</h2>

Free play gives you time to recover from an expensive experiment. Campaign
objectives are less forgiving because the rule that matters can change from one
mission to the next.

**Saving Mardi Gras** in New Orleans is mostly sequential. Find the alligators,
repair the road, move the required wood, extend the passenger service, and get
the parade running without turning each objective into a larger network than it
needs.

**Alpine Crossing** adds a clock. The railway has to move **300 passengers
through the tunnel**, and unnecessary construction works directly against the
three-star deadline.

**Desert Adventure** makes maintenance coverage part of the route. A connected
road can still be unsafe when a truck spends too long between protected areas.

**Biggest Festival Ever** stacks several pressures together. Stage construction,
food and medicine, sewage, road traffic, storm supplies, passenger growth, the
instrument search, and the final helicopter connection all compete for
attention. Starting passenger transport too late can leave the final attendance
push much slower than necessary.

For these first four missions, the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/campaign-walkthrough">campaign walkthrough</Link>
gives the objective order, required quantities, star conditions, special
objectives, and the points where progress can stall.


<h2
  id="later-network"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Do Not Treat Every New Transport Option as the Next Upgrade
</h2>

As years pass, the company gains access to more vehicles, larger infrastructure,
new road tools, better trains, trams, underground passenger stops, landmarks,
and other ways to move or support the network.

An unlock is an option, not evidence that the existing system should be
replaced.

A newer truck matters when it can replace several older vehicles on a crowded
road. A tram matters when a local passenger corridor has outgrown the smaller
fleet. Electrification matters when the train using that corridor can benefit
from it. Warehouses, landmarks, and larger terminals are the same: they become
useful when the existing network gives them a specific job.

If the current service is still moving what it needs to move without creating a
new bottleneck, there is no need to replace it just because another option has
unlocked. Let the network show you what has stopped working, then spend on that
problem.
