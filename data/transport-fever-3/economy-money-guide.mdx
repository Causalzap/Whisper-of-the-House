A company with $20 million in the bank can be in worse financial
shape than one holding $3 million. The first might have borrowed
heavily, purchased more vehicles than its routes can fill, and
committed to a railway that still lacks its destination station.
The second might already have several profitable services and
enough cash to keep operating.

Transport Fever 3 makes that distinction especially important
because spending and earning do not always happen at the same
point in a journey. Construction removes cash immediately.
Vehicle expenses continue while trains and trucks are running.
Cargo revenue may not appear until goods have passed through
several transport stages and reached their destination.

When I inspect a company's finances, I want to know whether
its existing transport can pay for its operations, what it
still owes, and how much cash remains after the next project
becomes usable. A large balance is useful, but it says little
about those obligations on its own.

<h2 id="starting-loan" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How Much Should You Borrow at the Start?
</h2>

Some Free Game starts leave little usable construction money.
Taking a loan can be necessary to get the first transport
services operating, particularly when the company has no
existing routes generating income.

The amount should follow the cost of the connection you intend
to finish.

Two early starts illustrate the differences between available
loan terms:

- **$12 million over 108 months at 10% interest.**
- **$9 million over 48 months at 7% interest.**

The first provides more construction cash and spreads repayment
over a longer period. The second involves less borrowing and
a shorter repayment period, but it also leaves less room for
an unexpectedly expensive station or route.

Neither example is automatically the better choice. A smaller
loan can be sufficient for a short road connection, while a
more expensive opening project may require additional cash
before its first vehicle can earn anything.

I would rather borrow enough to finish one useful connection
than spend a large loan opening several unfinished ones.

That first investment should include its stops, depot, vehicles,
and required roads or tracks. Leave a reserve for operating costs
and adjustments after the service begins.

early transport decisions.

<h2 id="complete-project-cost" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Calculate the Full Cost Before Building a Route
</h2>

A construction preview rarely shows the total amount a new
service will cost.

The first railway station may be affordable, but the company
still needs its destination station, connecting track, depot,
locomotive, wagons, and possibly local passenger transport.
Terrain changes and additional junctions can make the final
cost substantially higher.

I estimate the point at which the route can complete its first
useful journey, not merely the cost of placing its first
building.

For a new passenger railway, that means both stations must be
reachable and trains must be able to enter service. Freight
needs a functioning producer-to-consumer connection, including
the right terminals and vehicles for its cargo.

Do not commit the entire bank balance to infrastructure if
the vehicles required to use it have not yet been purchased.

There is also a less visible cost: the time between starting
construction and receiving income. An unfinished railway has
already consumed money but cannot yet replace any of it.

When considering two projects with similar potential, the one
that can become operational sooner and leave a larger cash
reserve may be safer than the more ambitious connection.

<h2 id="line-profit" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Is a Transport Line Losing Money?
</h2>

A negative line balance does not always have the same explanation.

A passenger service can lose money because too few people use
it. A freight vehicle may travel empty because its loading
settings are wrong. A third route may be carrying goods to
an intermediate warehouse and waiting for a later delivery
before its revenue is credited.

Those situations require different decisions.

One established passenger railway showed approximately
**$1.5 million in positive balance over the previous year**,
while **19 passengers** were still waiting at its station.

This is a strong reason to examine whether more capacity would
be useful. The existing service already earns money, and demand
is not being fully served.

Compare that with a train repeatedly leaving almost empty.
Adding another train to the second route increases costs without
solving the lack of passengers or cargo.

For freight, the balance needs closer inspection because the
payment system works differently from simply earning money
whenever a vehicle unloads.

### When does cargo actually earn money?

In Transport Fever 3, cargo revenue is credited when the goods
reach the consuming destination for that shipment.

An oil train delivering directly from an oil well to a refinery
can receive payment after unloading at the refinery. But when
the same goods are moved into an intermediate warehouse, that
transfer does not produce an immediate payment.

The goods must continue to their intended consuming destination.

Consider a route with three transport stages:

**Oil well → Warehouse → Transfer station → Refinery**

The first train moves oil into storage. Another vehicle carries
it to the next transfer point. A final train delivers it to
the refinery.

The first two vehicles can complete their journeys without
showing immediate cargo revenue. Once the oil reaches the
refinery, the resulting payment is distributed among the
transport legs that carried it.

This changes how I judge an apparently unprofitable
distribution line.

A negative balance on the first warehouse connection does
not necessarily mean the entire shipment lost money. Look
at whether the goods eventually reach the refinery and what
the complete transport chain costs to operate.

If the final delivery never happens, the earlier vehicles
have incurred expenses without completing the shipment
needed for revenue.

### Does a longer railway earn more money?

A longer physical route does not necessarily increase the
payment for the same cargo delivery.

In one controlled comparison, a train delivered the same
quantity between the same two industries using a much
longer, winding section of track. The cargo payment remained
the same as on the more direct connection.

The results indicated that the distance between the
origin and destination, rather than the actual length of
the railway, was important to the payment.

For an oil delivery in that comparison, **8 units generated
$78,872**, equivalent to **$9,859 per unit** under the
selected game settings.

Extending the track without changing the shipment's
endpoints did not increase that payment.

This is why building an unnecessarily winding railway
is a poor way to pursue higher freight revenue. The
extra journey can increase travel time and operating
costs without providing a corresponding increase in
the delivery payment.

Distance can affect what a shipment earns, but a longer
detour is not the same as delivering cargo to a more
distant consumer.

### Do faster trains earn more per cargo delivery?

Vehicle speed should be separated from cargo payment
and overall route profitability.

In controlled deliveries between the same endpoints,
changing the locomotive and wagon speed did not increase
the observed payment per unit.

One comparison used a faster wagon carrying **22 units**.
The delivery earned **$216,898**, again corresponding to
approximately **$9,859 per unit**.

The larger total came from carrying more cargo, not from
a higher payment per item.

However, the operating costs of different vehicles can
vary considerably. A faster train might complete journeys
more frequently, but it may also cost substantially more
to operate.

The useful comparison is therefore not simply the
locomotive's maximum speed. It is how much cargo the
complete service moves and what it costs to move it.

A fast train can be worthwhile when it replaces several
slower vehicles or meaningfully increases the amount
delivered. It is a poor upgrade when the additional
running expense exceeds the benefit.

### Does cargo spoilage reduce the payment?

One controlled comparison delivered the same quantity of
cargo with **0% and 83% spoilage**. Both completed deliveries
showed **$78,872** in payment.

That result indicates that the displayed spoilage
difference did not reduce the payment for those particular
completed shipments.

It does not establish that spoilage can never affect a
network's overall performance. The amount ultimately
delivered, the time vehicles remain in service, and
operating expenses still matter.

I would not buy a much faster vehicle solely on the
assumption that completing the same shipment sooner
always increases its payment. Check the actual income
and costs after the replacement.

### Why a warehouse transfer can show a large loss

A later distribution network had several freight lines
carrying goods toward warehouses and distribution centers.

One vehicle delivery connection was losing roughly
**$2 million**, and multiple other distribution routes
were also operating at a loss.

Some of those routes were moving goods toward intermediate
storage, so their revenue had not yet been realized through
a completed delivery. They continued generating running
expenses while waiting for the onward movement.

One line also had a separate configuration problem: it
was not set to load the cargo it was intended to carry.

This is why I distinguish a legitimate but expensive
transfer from a line that is simply not working.

If the vehicle never collects the goods, check the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide"> cargo loading, warehouse connections, and line settings</Link>
before changing the financial plan.

If goods are reaching their final destination, compare
the total revenue against the operating costs of all
the transport stages involved.

A warehouse can still be valuable for distributing goods
across several routes. The financial question is whether
that extra handling solves enough of a transport problem
to justify its cost.

<h2 id="recurring-costs" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Reduce Running Costs Without Losing Useful Capacity
</h2>

Construction spending is visible immediately. The quieter
expense comes from vehicles that remain in service year
after year.

Every unnecessary truck or train adds operating costs,
even when it spends much of its journey empty or waiting.

I pay attention to three different situations.

- **Vehicles repeatedly run below capacity:** The fleet may be larger than demand requires.
- **Passengers or cargo are consistently left behind:** Additional capacity could generate useful income.
- **Vehicles travel full in one direction and empty on the return:** There may be an opportunity to carry goods in both directions.

The third situation is especially important for freight.

### Use return cargo when the route makes sense

A train delivering oil to a refinery might return to
its starting point with empty wagons. The vehicle still
incurs operating costs during that return journey.

If the refinery produces cargo needed near the train's
starting location, carrying it on the return can put
otherwise unused vehicle capacity to work.

One oil network collected crude oil, delivered it to
a refinery, and then carried refined products back
toward a warehouse near its starting area. Other
services moved those products onward to their consumers.

That arrangement reduced the amount of empty running
on the main rail connection.

It does not mean every freight service should be
converted into a complicated circular route. The
return cargo must have a real destination, and the
additional movement should not create expensive
detours or unnecessary transfers.

Before adding a return load, check the vehicle's
cargo compatibility and whether the required goods
are available at the return pickup point.

A simple direct route with predictable demand can
remain more profitable than a complicated one
designed only to keep every vehicle full.

### Bigger and faster do not always mean cheaper

A high-capacity vehicle may replace several smaller
ones, reducing the number of vehicles needed for
the same transport rate.

But vehicle capacity, speed, and maintenance costs
do not always improve together.

In one freight comparison, four slower trains carried
a combined rate of roughly **232 oil per year** at
about **$3.7 million in annual maintenance costs**.
A faster alternative could move similar cargo, but
its higher running expense initially made the
economic result less attractive.

A later comparison in an operating network produced
a different outcome: replacing trains with a newer
model maintained similar income while reducing
observed maintenance expenses.

The lesson is to compare performance in the actual
network rather than treating one locomotive speed
or model as universally the cheapest choice.

Before replacing a fleet, compare the existing
transport rate, annual expense, cargo waiting at
stations, and the proposed replacement's capacity.

<h2 id="maintenance-and-replacement" className="scroll-mt-24">
  When Should You Replace Old Vehicles?
</h2>

An older vehicle does not need to be replaced immediately
because a newer model becomes available.

The replacement should perform a useful job that
justifies its purchase. That might mean carrying more
passengers, reducing the number of trucks needed for
an industrial route, or improving reliability and
performance enough to reduce the operating burden.

A road network once replaced a large fleet of older
trucks with newer vehicles. Because each replacement
could handle more work, several old trucks were
sold while the route retained enough transport
capacity for its demand.

The savings came partly from removing vehicles that
were no longer needed.

Buying replacements but leaving the entire old fleet
running produces the opposite result. Both groups
continue adding expenses, even when the new fleet
already provides sufficient capacity.

I would replace vehicles in stages on an important
route rather than sell every old vehicle before the
new service has demonstrated that it works.

Inspect the transport rate after each meaningful
change. If cargo begins accumulating or passenger
queues rise, the reduced fleet may no longer be
sufficient.

### Maintenance access and vehicle condition

Vehicle condition also depends on how the maintenance
network supports the fleet.

A company operating large numbers of small road
vehicles may put pressure on its maintenance facilities.
Replacing some of those vehicles with higher-capacity
alternatives can reduce the number requiring attention.

Adding maintenance buildings everywhere is not
automatically the best solution. Check whether the
existing facilities can serve the affected vehicles
and whether the road connections are usable.

When maintenance coverage or surrounding traffic is
the actual problem, inspect the relevant layout in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide"> traffic and road guide</Link>.

The financial decision remains whether maintaining,
replacing, or reducing the current fleet provides
the needed service at a sustainable cost.

<h2 id="subsidies" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Which Subsidies Are Worth Taking?
</h2>

Subsidies can provide money much faster than an
ordinary transport route. The danger is accepting
a large reward without considering what must be
built to earn it.

One Chiswick cargo subsidy offered:

- **$3.15 million** on acceptance.
- **2× income in Chiswick for 5 years** after completion.
- **$8.4 million** as the completion reward.
- A failure penalty involving a fine and reputation loss.

That is a valuable offer when the requested cargo connection
already fits the company's transport network.

If the producer is nearby and the required vehicles can
serve an existing destination, the subsidy may help fund
a route that would have been useful anyway.

A distant contract can be much less attractive.

The company might need new roads, stations, freight vehicles,
and other infrastructure that continue costing money after
the reward has been collected.

I check the requirements before accepting:

1. Can the necessary goods actually be produced and delivered?
2. Which parts of the required route already exist?
3. What additional construction and vehicle purchases are needed?
4. Can the delivery conditions be completed before the deadline?
5. Will the connection remain useful once the temporary bonus ends?

The failure penalty should be part of the decision, not
something discovered after the route has missed its
deadline.

A subsidy is strongest when it helps finish a sensible
connection sooner. It becomes risky when the reward
encourages the company to build an isolated network that
it cannot afford to keep operating.

<h2 id="debt" className="scroll-mt-24">
  When Should You Repay or Refinance a Loan?
</h2>

Debt repayment is useful, but paying it off too early
can leave the company without enough cash to operate
or expand.

If a company repays its remaining loan and immediately
needs another one to buy replacement vehicles, it has
not gained much financial flexibility.

The important comparison is between the repayment
obligation, available cash, and the money the existing
routes are bringing in.

One early company replaced its original financing
with a **$21 million loan over 228 months** while
expanding its transport network.

The longer term reduced the immediate repayment
burden, allowing more cash to remain available during
construction. The earlier loan was repaid afterward.

That decision provided additional breathing room,
but a longer repayment period can also increase
the total amount paid over time. Lower monthly
payments and lower total borrowing costs are
not the same thing.

I begin reducing debt more aggressively once the
company has several functioning services and can
cover normal vehicle replacement without relying
on another loan.

A meaningful cash reserve still matters. The company
should be able to handle an unexpected capacity
problem or maintenance expense after the repayment.

A longer-running company reached a debt-free position
after approximately **11 years** on Normal difficulty,
while still holding around **$6 million in cash**.

borrowing.

That is a more useful financial milestone than reaching
zero debt while leaving the next essential purchase
unfunded.

<h2 id="company-rank" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How Does Town Population Affect Company Rank?
</h2>

Transport Fever 3's Free Game progression also connects
company development with the population of the towns
being served.

An early company began at **Junior** rank. In one
progression screen, the next advancement required
a population target of **2,912**.

Increasing town population contributes to company
advancement and unlocks additional options.

The early progression included:

- **Junior:** Headquarters.
- **Mechanic:** Medium Headquarters Building and Amphitheater.
- **Engineer:** Corner Headquarters Building and Greenify Industry upgrade.

Those unlocks can influence what the company is able
to build or improve later, but there is little benefit
in spending beyond the available budget simply to
reach the next rank.

Population growth depends on functioning town services
and supply connections. The transport network needs
to support that growth rather than only generate a
large fleet of vehicles.

For the residential, commercial, industrial, and
transport conditions that affect population, use
the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/city-growth-guide">city growth guide</Link>.

From a financial perspective, company rank matters
when a newly available upgrade offers a worthwhile
investment for the existing network.

<h2 id="when-to-expand" className="scroll-mt-24">
  When Is It Worth Expanding the Transport Network?
</h2>

The most useful expansion opportunities are often
visible before construction begins.

A station has passengers waiting after every departure.
An industry produces more cargo than the current vehicles
can collect. A town has unsatisfied demand that could
be reached through an existing nearby transport connection.

These situations are different from building a new
service and hoping demand develops.

I would examine the existing network before spending
on another major project. A short improvement to
a busy route can sometimes generate more useful
capacity than an entirely new connection.

For each proposed expansion, compare:

- **Demand:** Is enough passenger or cargo traffic already available?
- **Construction cost:** What must be purchased before the new capacity can operate?
- **Running expense:** How many additional vehicles and facilities will remain in service?
- **Available cash:** Can the company finish construction and continue operating afterward?

The most expensive option is not necessarily the most
profitable. A second platform, larger vehicle, or
better connection to an existing industry might solve
the current limitation without creating a new
transport network.

When road transport has reached a genuine capacity
limit, moving the longer part of the journey onto
rail may be worthwhile. Station access, crossovers,
and train movements then become the practical
concerns covered in the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link>.

The economic question is whether the additional
income and usable capacity justify the investment.

<h2 id="company-finances" className="scroll-mt-24">
  What Should You Do When the Company Is Running Out of Money?
</h2>

A company can have a difficult financial year
without being close to failure.

Construction spending may temporarily exceed
income even when several existing routes are
profitable. Company value can increase while
cash falls because money has been invested
in infrastructure.

The immediate problem is whether the business
can continue operating long enough for its
investments to become productive.

When cash becomes tight, I stop starting new
projects and examine the current network.

First, look at routes that are already carrying
substantial demand and earning money. Selling
their useful vehicles can produce immediate cash,
but it also removes capacity that was helping
the company recover.

Then inspect the largest persistent losses.
A line that has been running mostly empty for
years deserves different treatment from a new
freight connection whose cargo has not yet
reached its final destination.

An unfinished or broken route should be
evaluated before deleting it. Sometimes one
missing station connection or incorrectly
configured cargo line is the only thing
preventing an expensive investment from
beginning to earn.

For an established service with little demand,
reducing excess vehicles may be appropriate.
If a newer vehicle can maintain the required
transport rate with fewer units, replacing
the fleet in stages can reduce ongoing expense.

Finally, look at the company's debt obligations.
A lower repayment burden may provide time for
working services to rebuild cash, but taking
another large loan to continue uncontrolled
construction only postpones the problem.

The aim is to preserve the routes that can
support the company while reducing expenses
that are not buying useful transport.

Once the balance begins recovering, I would
return to the strongest unmet demand rather
than restart every postponed project. A
profitable network grows more safely when
each new investment has a clear passenger
or cargo flow capable of paying for it.
