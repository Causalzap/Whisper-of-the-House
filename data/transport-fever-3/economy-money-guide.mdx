A Transport Fever 3 company can have millions in the bank and still be in a
weak financial position.

Cash may have come from a loan. A profitable passenger line can be surrounded
by unfinished infrastructure that is still consuming money. A new freight line
may show a loss while the production chain it belongs to is only beginning to
operate.

I therefore separate three things when judging the company:

**cash** tells me what I can spend now;

**debt and recurring costs** tell me what the company has to keep paying;

**working lines** tell me whether the network can replace the money I spend.

A healthy company does not need every line to be profitable immediately. It
needs enough functioning transport that the next project is being funded for a
reason rather than because another loan is available.


<h2
  id="starting-loan"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Borrow Enough to Finish the First Working Network
</h2>

Some starts give you little or no usable construction cash, so borrowing is
part of the opening.

The useful comparison is not simply which loan gives you the most money.

One early company used a **$12 million loan over 108 months at 10%**. Another
opening used **$9 million over 48 months at 7%**. Those are not universal best
choices, but they show the tradeoff clearly: a shorter or smaller loan can cost
less overall while leaving less room for construction mistakes.

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Choose the loan around the network you can actually finish, not around the largest amount the bank will offer.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Before accepting the loan, estimate the complete first job:

**stops or stations + depot + vehicles + connecting infrastructure + a cash
reserve after construction.**

Borrowing too little can leave half a network on the map with no money to make
it operational. Borrowing far more than the first plan needs creates the
opposite temptation: several projects begin at once, but none receives enough
attention to prove whether it works.

During the first hour, the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">opening setup</Link>
is deliberately small for this reason. Get one town and one useful network
moving before turning the rest of the loan into expansion.


<h2
  id="complete-project-cost"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Price the Point Where the Project Starts Earning
</h2>

Large transport projects rarely cost what the first construction preview shows.

The first station or terminal is only one part of the bill. The second end of
the route, connecting infrastructure, depot, vehicles, feeder services, and any
road or terrain work still have to be paid for before revenue begins.

That changes how I read the bank balance.

Having enough money to place a station does not mean the company can afford the
route. I want enough cash to reach the first useful trip and still have some
room left if the initial setup needs correction.

A simple rule works well here:

> **Price the usable network, not the first object you can afford.**

This also prevents one of the easiest early mistakes: spending almost everything
on a large project, discovering that one final piece is missing, and taking
another loan simply to make the original investment functional.


<h2
  id="line-profit"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Give a Line Time to Prove Whether It Earns
</h2>

Line balance becomes useful only after the route has had a fair chance to do the
job it was built for.

A later passenger railway showed about **$1.5 million positive balance in the
previous year** while **19 passengers** were still waiting at the station.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/transport-fever-3/transport-fever-3-profitable-train-balance.webp"
    alt="Transport Fever 3 passenger train line showing a positive previous-year balance with passengers still waiting"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    A positive balance while demand is still waiting is strong evidence that the existing service can support more investment.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

That is very different from an empty route that keeps losing money.

New freight lines need more patience. A truck may temporarily run with little
useful cargo because the upstream producer, transfer point, factory, or return
delivery is not operating yet. A red balance during that setup period does not
automatically mean the line should be deleted.

First decide whether the route itself has been given the cargo it is supposed
to move.

When the financial loss is actually caused by a production or transfer failure,
fix that in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry network</Link>
before judging the line as an economic failure.

A mature service is different. If it has had time to run, still carries little
demand, and continues costing more than it earns, keeping it alive simply
because the infrastructure already exists is not a good reason to keep paying
for it.


<h2
  id="recurring-costs"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Cut Capacity You Are Paying for but Not Using
</h2>

Construction removes cash in obvious chunks. Vehicle costs are quieter because
they keep accumulating after the excitement of building is over.

Every bus, truck, tram, train, ship, or aircraft that remains in service keeps
charging the company whether its capacity is useful or not.

That makes excess vehicles one of the easiest recurring costs to control.

A route deserves more capacity when people or cargo are consistently being left
behind. The reverse is also true: vehicles repeatedly running with large amounts
of spare capacity are a reason to question the fleet size.

Technology upgrades make this more important.

A newer vehicle may replace several older ones while maintaining the transport
rate. In one road network, replacing a large old truck fleet with newer vehicles
allowed several trucks to be sold while the line still retained enough rate for
its job.

The economic benefit comes from the vehicles you remove, not merely from owning
the newer model.

The same applies to passenger transport. When trams replace the useful capacity
of several older coaches, leaving all of the coaches running means the company
continues paying for two fleets to serve the same demand.

When an upgrade is complete, look back at the old vehicles and ask whether they
still have a job.


<h2
  id="maintenance-and-replacement"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Treat Vehicle Condition as a Replacement Cost, Not a Reason to Duplicate the Fleet
</h2>

Older vehicles can remain operational long after a better replacement becomes
available.

That does not mean every unlock justifies an immediate fleet-wide purchase.
Replacement makes financial sense when the new vehicle reduces the number of
vehicles required, increases useful capacity, or solves a performance problem
large enough to justify the cost.

The expensive mistake is additive upgrading:

> buy the new fleet → leave the old fleet running → pay both.

Sell vehicles that have actually been replaced.

Maintenance facilities and their road coverage affect how efficiently the
network keeps vehicles in condition, but their placement is a road-network
decision. Once maintenance access itself becomes the issue, use the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road setup</Link>
rather than duplicating facilities simply because vehicle condition has become
visible.


<h2
  id="subsidies"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Let Subsidies Pay for Routes You Already Have a Reason to Build
</h2>

Subsidies can inject a large amount of money into the company, but the reward is
only useful when the required infrastructure still makes sense afterward.

One Chiswick cargo offer provided:

- **$3.15 million** when accepted;
- **2× income in Chiswick for 5 years** after completion;
- an additional **$8.4 million** completion reward;
- and a failure penalty involving both a **fine** and **reputation loss**.

  <Image
    src="/images/transport-fever-3/transport-fever-3-subsidy-reward.webp"
    alt="Transport Fever 3 cargo subsidy showing upfront payment double income reward completion payout and failure penalties"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Subsidies are strongest when the reward accelerates infrastructure that already fits the network.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

That offer is attractive when Chiswick already needs the service and the
required producer or connection is practical.

A distant opportunity can be much less valuable even with a large payout. If
the company has to construct a long isolated network, buy a new fleet, and
maintain infrastructure that serves little purpose after the contract ends, the
headline reward is not the real profit.

Before accepting, I check four things:

**What must be built that I do not already have?**

**Will the resulting route still carry useful demand after the reward ends?**

**Can the requirement realistically be completed before its deadline?**

**What does failure cost?**

The best subsidy is often attached to something the company was already close
to doing.


<h2
  id="debt"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Repay or Refinance Debt Around Cash Flow, Not Pride
</h2>

Paying every loan off as quickly as possible feels safe, but a transport company
still needs working cash.

Repaying debt and then borrowing again to buy the next vehicles does not create
much financial freedom. It simply moves the borrowing to a later date.

One early company replaced its original loan with a **$21 million loan over 228
months** because the longer term produced a lower monthly burden while a larger
network was being built. The original loan was then repaid.

That does not make a longer loan automatically better. It shows why the payment
burden matters alongside the total principal and interest.

I begin paying debt down aggressively when the network can do all of the
following:

- cover normal vehicle replacement and small capacity increases;
- leave a meaningful cash reserve after repayment;
- support several lines that are already generating revenue;
- and avoid borrowing the same money back for the next planned project.

A longer-running company eventually became completely debt-free after roughly
**11 years** while still holding around **$6 million in cash** on Normal
difficulty.

That same company had years where displayed expenses exceeded income while its
company value continued rising. Construction can make an individual year look
ugly even when the money is creating useful infrastructure.

A negative year is therefore not enough information by itself.

Look at what the company bought, how much debt remains, whether working lines
are earning, and whether cash is still available for normal operation.


<h2
  id="company-rank"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Town Population Also Advances Company Rank
</h2>

Free Game progression ties the transport company to the population of the towns
it serves.

An early company began at **Junior** rank and showed a population requirement
for the next stages. In one run, the progression screen displayed a target of
**2,912 population** while explaining that growing the towns raises the company
rank. 
Higher ranks unlock practical options rather than only cosmetic titles.

The early progression shown included:

- **Junior** with the Headquarters;
- **Mechanic** with a Medium Headquarters Building and Amphitheater;
- **Engineer** with a Corner Headquarters Building and the Greenify Industry
  upgrade.

That gives town growth an economic consequence: a stronger transport network
raises population, population advances company progression, and new ranks open
additional tools for the company.

Do not spend money merely to chase the rank bar, though. Population still comes
from towns whose transport and supply needs are functioning. The actual growth
mechanics remain with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/city-growth-guide">city growth setup</Link>.

From the company side, the important point is that rank unlocks can change what
you are able to invest in next.


<h2
  id="when-to-expand"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Expand When the Existing Network Can Absorb the Cost
</h2>

The safest expansion usually begins with something already visible on the map:
waiting passengers, cargo the current fleet cannot move fast enough, or a town
with demand that can be reached without constructing an entirely separate
network.

Before committing to a large project, I want three answers.

### Can the current company keep operating while I build it?

The project should not consume the cash needed to keep the existing fleet
running and replace vehicles that actually need replacement.

### Can I reach the first revenue trip without another emergency loan?

Estimate the complete path to operation, not only the first terminal or piece of
infrastructure.

### Is there demand waiting for the new capacity?

Known demand reduces the amount of time a major investment has to sit idle
before earning.

Once the problem becomes *which transport mode* should handle that demand, the
economic question is finished. Passenger or freight lines that have outgrown
road capacity should then be evaluated in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals setup</Link>
rather than turning this page into a second rail-planning guide.

<h2
  id="company-finances"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When Cash Gets Tight, Stop Building Before You Sell Useful Capacity
</h2>

A low bank balance does not always mean the company is failing. It often means
construction has moved faster than the routes already on the map can repay it.

Freeze new projects first and leave profitable services alone. Remove vehicles
that no longer have enough demand to justify their running cost, then look at
lines that have had enough time to settle but still carry very little.

Selling useful vehicles or cutting a healthy line may create cash immediately,
but it also removes the service that was helping the company recover. If the
existing network is earning, give it time to rebuild the balance before taking
on another large project or another loan.

Once the company has room to spend again, return to the problem that was already
visible on the map instead of restarting every project that was postponed.
