Transport Fever 3 gives you enough systems at the start to make overbuilding
very easy. Roads, cargo, ships, trains, landmarks, subsidies, and new towns can
all look like useful first moves, but the opening is much easier when you make
one town work before trying to build a transport empire.

My first useful check is not the vehicle list. I pause the game, click the town
I want to develop, and look at what it is actually missing. A town can want
better public transport and specific cargo at the same time, so the first
network should solve those visible needs rather than follow a fixed build order.

Once passengers are moving and the first cargo is arriving, the game starts
giving you much better information about what deserves money next. Waiting
passengers can justify another vehicle or a train. A nearby producer can justify
a cargo route. A distant opportunity that needs an expensive new network can
wait.


<h2
  id="check-town-needs"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Pause First and Read One Town's Supplies
</h2>

A clean 1900 start can begin with no spendable company cash, so taking a loan
may be unavoidable. That does not mean the whole loan should immediately become
roads, depots, and vehicles.

Pause after taking enough money to begin building, select the town you want to
use as your first base, and open **Supplies**.

The panel shows two things that matter immediately: which parts of town still
lack useful passenger transport, and which cargo the settlement is asking for.
Use the district overlay to identify the residential, commercial, and
industrial areas, then make the passenger connection your first small network.

Do not worry about maximizing every town at once. One functioning town gives
you passengers, income, visible demand, and a place to test how your network is
behaving. Spreading the opening budget across several settlements makes it much
harder to tell which investment is actually working.

Loan size, repayment timing, subsidies, running costs, and cash flow become more
important once the first network exists. If debt rather than route design is
what is stopping expansion, continue with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">money and economy decisions</Link>
instead of taking another loan just because the button is available.


<h2
  id="first-passenger-loop"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Build the First Passenger Loop Around Coverage, Not Road Length
</h2>

Road stops show a coverage radius before placement. Use that circle.

The first loop does not need to trace every street. It needs stops that place
the important residential, commercial, and industrial buildings inside
coverage. On the Spalding route, one stop covered most of the residential
district, another covered the commercial buildings, and another covered the
industrial side of town. An extra residential stop was only added because a
group of houses sat outside the existing circles.

    alt="Transport Fever 3 road stop coverage radius over residential, commercial, and industrial buildings"
    width={1600}
    height={900}

  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Place stops by the buildings inside the coverage circle, not by trying to fill every road with stops.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The two-sided road stop is useful early because it can serve passengers and
cargo unloading, and buses, trucks, and trams can use it. It is not a universal
cargo pickup point, though, so do not assume every road stop can collect goods
from a producer.

After placing the stops, create the line in the order vehicles should visit
them. Pay attention to which side of the road each stop uses. A stop on the
wrong side can make the route cross or turn in a way you did not intend; fixing
that before buying vehicles is cheaper than redesigning the line after traffic
starts moving.

A compact local loop is usually enough for the first test. You are trying to
prove that people can move between the town's districts, not build the final
public-transport network on day one.


<h2
  id="road-depot"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Put the First Depot Near the Route, Not in the Middle of Town
</h2>

The first depot should be close enough that new vehicles can reach their lines
without a long empty drive, but there is little reason to drop it into the
densest residential streets. Depots add noise and pollution, so the edge of
town or a road already used by your first routes is usually a cleaner starting
position.

For the first passenger loop, buy a small fleet and let it run before adding
more vehicles. Demand should decide the next purchase.


<h2
  id="run-before-expanding"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Let the Line Run Before You Decide It Needs More Vehicles
</h2>

Unpause after assigning the vehicles and watch what actually happens at the
stops. The first useful confirmation is simple: passengers appear, board the
line, and reach another district.

After that, open the line information instead of guessing from how busy the road
looks. **Capacity, utilization, frequency, rate, and balance** tell you
different things about the same service.

In one early Spalding route, the line showed **24 capacity, 100% utilization,
about 14.5 minutes frequency, a rate of 10, and a positive balance**.

Do not treat those values as targets for every route. Read them together:
utilization tells you whether the existing capacity is being used, frequency
shows how long passengers wait between vehicles, rate shows how much transport
the line can provide over time, and balance tells you whether the route is
paying for itself.

A nearly empty route does not need another coach because its frequency looks
slow. A full route with people accumulating at the stops does. That distinction
keeps the opening budget focused on actual bottlenecks.


<h2
  id="first-cargo-route"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Add One Nearby Cargo Need After the Passenger Line Works
</h2>

Once the local passenger service is moving people, open Supplies again and pick
one requested cargo that can be reached without committing to a large network.

In the Spalding start, vegetables were the practical next step because the
producer was nearby. That is the kind of first cargo route I want: short,
visible demand, and cheap enough that a mistake does not consume the remaining
opening budget.

Do not start with a distant production chain just because it looks more
valuable. For terminals, vehicle compatibility, production chains, loading,
warehouses, and delivery problems, continue with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry setup</Link>.


<h2
  id="when-to-buy-more"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Add Another Vehicle Only When the Existing Line Is Full
</h2>

Waiting passengers are one of the clearest early expansion signals, but watch
the queue across more than one trip before spending.

If vehicles arrive full and passengers are still left behind, the line needs
more capacity. Add one vehicle, let the route settle again, and check
utilization and frequency before buying another.

If vehicles are leaving with empty seats, a slow-looking frequency by itself is
not a reason to expand the fleet.

When a road connection stays overloaded even after sensible capacity increases,
it may be ready for rail. The
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link>
covers when that upgrade is worth the cost and how to build the first railway.

<h2
  id="stable-opening"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  What a Stable Opening Looks Like
</h2>

You do not need every town need filled before expanding. A good opening is one
where the first passenger line is actually carrying people, the town has at
least one useful connection beyond that local loop, and the company still has
enough room to react when demand changes.

Let the network run for a while before forcing the next expansion. Waiting
passengers, an unmet supply, a line that keeps losing money, or a growing queue
will give you a much better reason to spend than simply unlocking another
transport type.

Once the first town can operate without constant intervention, the opening is
doing its job. The next project should solve something you can already see on
the map.
