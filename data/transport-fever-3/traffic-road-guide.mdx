Traffic in Transport Fever 3 usually becomes a problem gradually.

One freight line crosses a town without trouble. Then another industry starts
using the same road, passenger vehicles join it, the town grows around the
junction, and the cargo station at the far end becomes busier. By the time the
queue looks serious, several different problems may be stacked on top of each
other.

That is why I do not begin with road width.

First work out **which vehicles belong on that road**, **where the queue really
ends**, and **which movement is blocking the others**. A wider road cannot fix
through-trucks taking the wrong route, a one-platform cargo terminal, or vehicles
using a roundabout to make turns you never intended.

Once those movements make sense, adding road capacity becomes much more
effective.


<h2
  id="separate-through-traffic"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Move Through-Freight Around the Town Before Widening the Center
</h2>

Warwick reached the point where several cargo routes wanted the same central
streets. Fish, planks, and sand moving toward glass production all added traffic
to roads that also had to serve the town itself.

The useful change was an outside connection.

A road was extended around Warwick, past the weaving mill, and connected back
into another medium road. Cargo that did not need to stop in the center could
stay outside for most of the journey.

    The outside connection gives long freight routes another path while local streets remain available for buses and deliveries that actually need the town center.

This does not mean every settlement needs a perfect circular ring road.

The important distinction is between **through-freight** and **local movement**.
A truck crossing Warwick on its way somewhere else should not use every
downtown junction merely because that was the original shortest road.

Keep long movements outside for as much of the trip as practical, then let local
delivery traffic handle the final approach.

That also makes later traffic problems easier to read. If the center still
queues after the long-haul routes have moved out, the remaining bottleneck is
much more likely to be a local junction, stop, or road-capacity issue.


<h2
  id="waypoints"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Use a Waypoint When Trucks Refuse to Use the Better Road
</h2>

Building a bypass does not guarantee that every line will choose it.

A route can continue through the town because the original road still produces
a path the line prefers. In that situation, rebuilding the bypass again is not
the answer.

Add a waypoint on the corridor you want the trucks to use.

One furniture line heading toward Spalding was forced onto the outer route this
way. The waypoint did not describe every turn; it simply pulled the line onto
the correct road before the trucks reached the area where the two routes
diverged.

A single well-placed waypoint is often enough.

They are most useful when a line:

- keeps cutting through a town center;
- approaches a destination from a congested side;
- uses an awkward turnaround;
- or ignores a new road built specifically for that traffic.

Avoid chaining many waypoints together unless the route genuinely needs them.
Every forced point makes the line less flexible when the road network changes
later.


<h2
  id="road-hierarchy"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Give Local Streets and Freight Corridors Different Jobs
</h2>

Traffic becomes much easier to manage when every road is not expected to handle
every kind of movement.

Small streets are good for building access and short local trips. Busier roads
can collect that traffic and connect districts. Long cargo movements benefit
from larger roads that avoid unnecessary intersections.

The useful hierarchy is simple:

**local streets** handle access to homes, businesses, and nearby stops;

**main town roads** collect those local movements;

**outer or higher-capacity corridors** handle longer travel between industries,
towns, and major terminals.

You do not need to rebuild an entire town to create that structure.

Start with the road that already carries repeated heavy traffic. Spalding, for
example, gradually received more cargo deliveries on its main streets. Improving
the important corridor before several more lines arrived was more useful than
upgrading quiet side roads.

Road width still has limits. A large road feeding one badly designed junction
can move a bigger queue toward the same bottleneck without increasing the
number of vehicles that actually get through.


<h2
  id="roundabouts-and-lanes"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A Roundabout Needs Clear Lane Movements, Not Just More Lanes
</h2>

Roundabouts are useful where several roads meet and no single movement should
dominate the whole junction.

Royston used a fairly large two-lane roundabout, with the circle itself upgraded
for better flow. That created the space for traffic to move, but the first
version still allowed movements that were not useful.

Lane management finished the job.

    width={1600}

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The roundabout provides the space; lane rules decide which approaches can use each exit and prevent unnecessary U-turn movements.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The key is to watch what vehicles actually do after the junction opens.

A multi-lane roundabout can still perform badly when every approach can reach
every exit and vehicles wait until the last moment to cross lanes. Removing an
unnecessary turn can improve flow without changing the road at all.

At Royston, lane rules were used to guide particular approaches toward specific
exits and stop vehicles from using the roundabout as a convenient U-turn.

When setting those rules, work from the purpose of each road.

An industrial approach may mainly need the freight exit. A local street may need
two town directions. A service road beside a station may not need access to the
entire roundabout.

Change one movement at a time and let routes recalculate. Aggressive lane
restrictions can solve one conflict and accidentally leave another line with no
usable path.


<h2
  id="traffic-lights"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Add Traffic Lights Only After the Junction Itself Makes Sense
</h2>

Traffic lights control conflicting movements. They do not manufacture road
capacity.

They become useful when several important approaches repeatedly compete for the
same space and the junction no longer clears cleanly on its own.

Before adding them, check what is happening immediately around the intersection.

A queue caused by a cargo station just beyond the junction will still be there
after the light changes. Through-trucks that should have taken a bypass will
still arrive. Poor lane choices will still force vehicles across one another.

Lights work best after those larger routing problems have been removed.

Then the signal can do what it is actually good at: give competing movements
clear turns through a busy junction instead of letting them all interfere at
once.


<h2
  id="follow-the-cargo-queue"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A Truck Queue Can Start at the Terminal Instead of the Road
</h2>

A long line of trucks outside a station looks like a traffic problem, but the
front of the queue tells you more than the length of it.

Chiswick eventually had **153 canned goods** in storage while vehicles were
also backing up around the delivery area. The road was busy, but widening it
would not have solved everything if the same trucks still had to wait for the
same loading position.

Follow the stationary vehicles all the way forward.

A queue ending at a junction points back toward road design. A queue ending at
a platform means the terminal itself is restricting how quickly vehicles can
move through the area.

At that point, the problem has crossed into cargo handling rather than road
capacity. Platform assignment, loading positions, storage, and terminal
throughput are covered in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry guide</Link>.

For the surrounding road, leave enough space for trucks to enter and leave the
terminal without blocking the next intersection.


<h2
  id="bus-lanes"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Protect Passenger Service When Cargo Traffic Starts Holding It Up
</h2>

Cargo congestion becomes more expensive when local passenger vehicles join the
same queue.

Chiswick eventually reached that point. The main road was carrying enough mixed
traffic that buses were being delayed along with the freight vehicles.

A section of the corridor was upgraded to a **medium street with bus lanes**.

That upgrade was not free.

Widening the road required removing buildings and temporarily hurt reputation,
so it only made sense on a corridor where passenger service genuinely needed
protection.

A quiet street with two buses does not need the same treatment.

Also check the road type carefully before converting a long stretch. Some
priority layouts can be more restrictive to ordinary traffic than expected.
Test the important cargo and passenger lines on a short section first before
changing the whole town.


<h2
  id="vehicle-count"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Reduce Vehicle Count Before You Assume the Road Is Too Small
</h2>

Traffic volume depends on how many vehicles the network needs to move a given
amount of cargo.

A later plank line showed why vehicle replacement can matter so much. The old
fleet produced a transport rate of **84**. After replacing those vehicles with
newer stake trucks, the rate rose to **155**.

Several new trucks could then be removed:

- after two were sold, the line still showed **124**;
- after two more were sold, it remained around **93**.

The final rate was still above the old **84**, but fewer vehicles needed road
space.

That is the traffic result worth paying attention to.

When a road is filled with an older low-capacity fleet, compare the transport
rate you actually need with what newer vehicles can provide before spending on
another lane.

Once the replacement has enough capacity, remove the vehicles it made
redundant. Keeping both generations running preserves the congestion the upgrade
could have removed.

Purchase cost, running cost, and replacement timing are handled in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">money and economy guide</Link>.
On the road itself, fewer vehicles doing the same work is a direct capacity
gain.


<h2
  id="road-maintenance"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Put Road Maintenance on Corridors Used by Several Lines
</h2>

The Road Maintenance Building automatically maintains trucks, buses, and trams
inside its coverage radius.

That makes location important.

A building beside one lightly used endpoint may spend most of its coverage on a
road that very few vehicles traverse. A shared industrial corridor can expose
the same maintenance area to several cargo and passenger lines throughout the
day.

One later industrial junction received a maintenance facility for exactly that
reason: many cargo trucks were already passing through the area.

Do not scatter maintenance buildings beside every route by default.

Watch where the fleet actually spends its time. Long lines that remain outside
existing coverage for extended periods may justify another maintenance point,
while several short local lines can often share the same facility.


<h2
  id="road-tools"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Road Upgrades Can Solve One Problem and Create Another
</h2>

Some road tools affect more than traffic flow.

**Locked roads** prevent the town from altering or extending the road. That is
useful for an important arterial whose junctions and geometry need to remain
stable, but the road becomes player-owned and brings its own upkeep. Locking
every side street is unnecessary.

Tree-lined road upgrades make another tradeoff. They reduce noise and pollution,
but vehicle top speed falls by **25%**.

That can be useful beside dense development where environmental effects have
become important. It is much less attractive on a high-volume freight road that
already struggles with travel time.

The town consequences of noise, pollution, and reputation are covered in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/city-growth-guide">city growth guide</Link>.

From the road side, the decision is whether the environmental benefit is worth
the loss in speed on that specific corridor.


<h2
  id="trams"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Replace Small Passenger Fleets When the Street Is Carrying Too Many Vehicles
</h2>

Passenger transport can become part of road congestion even when every vehicle
is doing useful work.

A busy local route may eventually need enough small coaches that the fleet
itself occupies a noticeable amount of street space. Trams can move more people
per vehicle and reduce how many separate vehicles are required for the same
corridor.

One transition replaced several stagecoaches after tram service began carrying
the local demand.

The important part was removing the capacity that had already been replaced.

Running the full bus fleet and the new tram service together would have kept
most of the original road traffic while adding another transport system on top
of it.

Use trams when passenger demand has actually outgrown the smaller local fleet,
not merely because tram technology has become available.


<h2
  id="underground-transit"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Move Passenger Transport Underground When Surface Space Is the Limiting Resource
</h2>

Underground passenger stops allow tram service to continue below the surface
while passengers access the line from above.

That is not an early-town requirement.

It becomes interesting when an already important surface corridor has no easy
room left: roads are heavily used, another large stop would require expensive
demolition, or passenger vehicles are competing with freight for the same
space.

Going underground preserves more of the surface network, but it also adds
construction to solve a problem that may still have a cheaper answer.

Before committing, check whether a better surface route, bus lane, or simpler
junction change can recover enough capacity.

Underground construction earns its cost when **space itself** has become the
bottleneck.


<h2
  id="when-road-is-not-enough"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Long-Haul Freight Eventually Stops Being a Road Problem
</h2>

There is no fixed truck count where a route suddenly needs rail.

The warning appears when the long-distance movement itself starts consuming too
much of the road network. A large fleet may still be moving the required cargo,
but town entrances, local buses, and other delivery routes now spend their time
waiting behind it.

At that point, adding another lane or another truck is treating the symptom.

The long-haul leg can move to rail while road vehicles continue doing what they
are good at: flexible collection and shorter delivery around the endpoints.

Do not replace a short industrial shuttle simply because it uses many trucks.
The change becomes worthwhile when the freight movement is repeatedly interfering
with the rest of the road network.

Once the question becomes how to build the railway, where to place the stations,
or how to keep several trains moving on shared track, continue with the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="traffic-troubleshooting"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Find the Front of the Queue Before You Change the Road
</h2>

A traffic jam can stretch far enough that the visible tail has almost nothing to
do with the original cause.

Follow the stationary vehicles toward the front and identify what the first
blocked vehicle is waiting for.

### The queue ends at a cargo terminal

The road may be fine. Check whether trucks are waiting for the same loading
position or entrance.

### The queue ends at a junction

Watch the actual turns. Look for crossing movements, unwanted U-turns, poor lane
choices, or several busy routes all competing for one exit.

### Long-haul trucks fill the center but rarely stop there

Give them an outside connection and use a waypoint if they continue choosing the
old route.

### Buses are trapped in the same queue as cargo trucks

Separate the movements where possible. A bus lane can protect an important
passenger corridor once the surrounding road layout is already sensible.

### The road is full of older trucks

Check whether newer vehicles can maintain the required transport rate with a
smaller fleet before adding road capacity.

### The junction was improved but the queue immediately returned

Look farther forward. A blocked station, another intersection, or an overflowing
exit may be storing the same traffic one block away.

### Traffic is flowing but the town is still unhappy

Congestion may no longer be the weak point. Check noise, pollution, and
reputation before rebuilding the road again.

A useful traffic fix changes the movement that created the queue. Widening a
road is valuable when road capacity is genuinely the limit; it is wasted when
the same vehicles are still being sent toward the same blocked point.
