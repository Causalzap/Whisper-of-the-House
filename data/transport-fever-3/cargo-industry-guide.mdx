The difficult cargo problems in Transport Fever 3 usually begin after the first
successful delivery.

A crop farm sending vegetables straight into town has very little to hide. A
furniture network can involve raw materials, intermediate industries, several
transport rates, shared storage, transfer lines, finished goods, and local
delivery. One broken link can make vehicles elsewhere in the chain look like
the problem even when they are doing exactly what they were asked to do.

Adding vehicles is therefore one of the last things I do when cargo stops
moving.

I first want to know **what the destination consumes, what is missing there, and
where that missing cargo was last seen**. That usually narrows a large network
to one terminal, one vehicle type, one production input, or one transfer.


<h2
  id="trace-chain-backward"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Start With the Building That Should Receive the Cargo
</h2>

When a factory or town is not getting what it needs, begin at that destination
rather than at the nearest producer.

A town can request a finished good in only part of the settlement. A factory may
require several inputs at the same time. One material can be overflowing while a
second shortage prevents the entire building from using it.

A simple vegetable chain is easy to read:

**town demand → delivery stop → crop farm → suitable vehicle**

Furniture is different:

**furniture demand → furniture factory → planks + glass → upstream industries**

If furniture production is low because glass is missing, buying more vehicles
for the finished furniture line changes nothing. The useful question is where
the glass stopped progressing toward the factory.

That backward approach scales well as the network grows. Find the missing item,
move one step upstream, and continue until you reach the first place where cargo
should exist but does not.


<h2
  id="industry-terminals"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Check the Industry's Own Terminals Before Building Another Station
</h2>

Some industries already provide the loading point you need.

The Chiswick Crop Farm has **two Passenger and Cargo road terminals** available
through the industry's Road Station options. Trucks can load directly there
without another cargo station being built beside the farm.

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Chiswick Crop Farm already provides two road terminals, allowing the vegetable line to load directly at the producer.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

This check is worth making before every new industry connection. An extra
terminal costs money and space, and it does not improve a route when the
producer already has usable loading positions.

The second terminal can also matter later. If several vehicles begin competing
for one loading position, assigning an alternative terminal can give them
another place to work without changing the production chain itself.

The delivery end needs a valid destination as well. Cargo entering a town is not
automatically useful to every building in that town. The stop still has to serve
the area that consumes the good.

If the issue is basic stop coverage rather than cargo flow, fix the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">local stop placement</Link>
before expanding the freight fleet.


<h2
  id="cargo-compatibility"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Prove the Route With the Correct Vehicle Before Buying the Fleet
</h2>

A connected line can remain empty when the selected vehicle cannot carry the
cargo assigned to it.

Check compatibility before committing to a large purchase. This becomes easy to
miss in multi-stage chains because the vehicle used for a raw material may not
be suitable for the processed product that leaves the next industry.

Rail freight follows the same rule: locomotive performance is irrelevant when
the attached wagons cannot carry the item the line is meant to move.

For a new connection, I prefer to send one vehicle or a small test fleet first.
That first trip answers several questions at once:

- does cargo appear at the loading terminal;
- can the vehicle load it;
- does the destination accept it;
- and does the vehicle return empty or still carrying something it could not
  unload?

Once those answers are correct, adding capacity is straightforward. Buying ten
vehicles before the first one completes the trip only makes configuration
mistakes more expensive.


<h2
  id="production-ratios"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Size Each Incoming Line Around the Industry Recipe
</h2>

Cargo starts moving long before the network is properly balanced.

Furniture production gives a clear example. The factory used here consumes:

**4 planks + 1 glass → 2 furniture**

That 4:1 input ratio should be visible in the transport feeding it.

With the glass route running at **37**, four times that amount gives a plank
target of roughly **148**. After vehicles were adjusted, the plank route reached
**146**, close enough to keep the two inputs arriving in useful proportion.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/transport-fever-3/transport-fever-3-cargo-rate-matching.webp"
    alt="Transport Fever 3 furniture chain with plank and glass transport rates matched to a four-to-one production ratio"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Furniture consumes four planks for every one glass, so equal-sized incoming fleets would oversupply one side of the recipe.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

This is why a factory surrounded by cargo can still produce slowly.

If glass is short while planks are already piling up, increasing the plank rate
does not create more furniture. It creates more stored planks, more trucks, and
more running cost while the same glass shortage remains.

The same calculation continues upstream. One glass chain used a **2:1
sand-to-glass** relationship. A sand flow around **160** therefore supported a
glass flow around **80**.

Do not treat those exact rates as permanent targets. Production can change,
vehicles can improve, and industries can gain additional output. The useful
method is the relationship:

**read the recipe → see what the building is consuming → size the incoming
lines around those proportions**

Whenever production rises, check the lines again. A route that was correctly
sized for the previous output can become the new bottleneck without anything
else being wrong.


<h2
  id="worker-transport"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Passenger Service Can Increase Production at Some Industries
</h2>

Cargo is not the only thing that can strengthen an industry.

Some industries can receive workers from nearby settlements, and transporting
those workers can improve production. That makes an industry passenger
connection different from an ordinary town bus route: the passengers are part
of the production setup.

A crop farm later received passenger service while another production input was
also being improved. With those conditions in place, its output increased and
the cargo network suddenly had more material to move.

The important consequence appears after the production change.

A freight line that previously matched the industry's output may now be too
small. Storage begins filling, cargo waits longer, and adding production no
longer produces the expected downstream result because transport has become the
new limit.

Worker service therefore belongs in the same calculation as other production
improvements:

**increase production → recheck output → compare the existing cargo rate with the
new amount available**

Do not add workers and immediately duplicate every cargo vehicle. Let the
production change become visible first, then increase only the lines that are
actually falling behind.


<h2
  id="warehouses"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Add a Warehouse When the Network Needs a Buffer Between Transport Stages
</h2>

A warehouse is most useful when cargo cannot or should not travel directly from
producer to final consumer in one line.

That can happen when a ship brings goods to shore before trucks distribute
them, several producers feed one shared area, finished products need to wait for
different town deliveries, or a factory line needs somewhere to leave its
return cargo.

The warehouse then becomes a buffer between transport stages.

What it should not become is an automatic building placed beside every
industry. A nearby farm supplying one town directly has no need for an extra
transfer simply because storage is available.

Every transfer introduces another place where the network can stop working.
Cargo has to reach the storage area, remain available to the next line, and
leave again toward a destination that can actually consume it.

Use the warehouse when that extra step simplifies several routes or separates
transport modes. Skip it when the direct connection is already simple and
working.


<h2
  id="distribution-center"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Use a Shared Distribution Center When Several Cargo Flows Need the Same Place
</h2>

The furniture network became easier to manage once planks, glass, and finished
furniture could pass through one shared transfer area.

The warehouse was configured for **planks, glass, and furniture**, with a
universal cargo station beside it handling the vehicles moving those goods.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/transport-fever-3/transport-fever-3-distribution-center-layout.webp"
    alt="Transport Fever 3 distribution center storing planks glass and furniture beside a universal cargo station"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Planks and glass arrive at shared storage, a factory line takes the required inputs onward, and finished furniture returns before local distribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The useful part is the separation of jobs.

One route can bring planks into storage. Another delivers glass. A factory line
collects the required inputs, takes them to furniture production, and returns
with finished furniture. Town delivery vehicles then work from that finished
stock instead of driving all the way back through the production chain.

That produces a readable sequence:

**producer → shared storage → factory → shared storage → consumer**

The same setup can also keep long-haul freight out of a busy town center.

When several cargo flows already converge near the same settlement, let the
long routes finish at the shared storage area and use a shorter delivery line
for the final trip into town:

**long-distance cargo → outside storage → short delivery line → town**

This works best when several routes already need the same transfer point. Do not
add an extra handoff to a simple direct route only to shorten the last few
streets; every transfer creates another place where cargo can wait.

It also gives each storage state a meaning.

A warehouse full of planks but empty of glass tells you where to look. Plenty of
both inputs with no furniture returning moves the problem downstream toward the
factory or return line. Finished furniture sitting in storage proves that the
production loop works and shifts attention toward the final delivery routes.


<h2
  id="confirm-transfer"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Finished Goods Returning to Storage Prove More Than a Pile of Raw Materials
</h2>

A large stockpile can look reassuring even when half of the chain is broken.

Raw planks arriving at a warehouse prove only that the plank route works.
Glass arriving beside them proves that two inbound routes work. Neither proves
that the factory is receiving the right amounts or that the finished product can
make the return trip.

In the furniture network, the important moment was furniture appearing back at
the shared warehouse after the factory had received its inputs.

That is the point where the final town delivery can be judged on its own.

Before finished goods appear, expanding the last-mile fleet is premature. There
is nothing useful for that fleet to solve yet.


<h2
  id="cargo-not-unloading"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Cargo Coming Back With the Vehicle Usually Means the Destination Cannot Use It
</h2>

Cargo loading correctly and then returning on the same vehicle is not the same
failure as an empty producer.

One example appeared at Lisburn Brewery. The brewery could hold **200 panes of
glass**. Once that storage filled, incoming vehicles no longer had room to leave
all of the glass they were carrying.

The line settings include **Force unload all cargo on arrival**.

<figure className="my-8">
  <Image
    src="/images/transport-fever-3/transport-fever-3-force-unload-cargo.webp"
    alt="Transport Fever 3 line settings showing Force unload all cargo on arrival"
    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Force unload prevents leftovers from riding back with the vehicle, but it does not correct a line delivering more than the destination can use.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

I would not turn that setting on before understanding why the storage is full.

The brewery may simply be receiving glass faster than it consumes it. Another
required production input may be missing, preventing the glass from being used.
The cargo may also be expected to transfer onward without a working storage and
outbound connection.

In those situations, forced unloading changes the symptom rather than the
balance of the chain.

Use it when the route is intentionally one-way and returning cargo serves no
purpose. If the destination is chronically overfilled, reduce the incoming
rate or fix the production stage that should be consuming the material.


<h2
  id="production-too-slow"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  When an Industry Has Cargo but Output Still Looks Wrong, Compare the Whole Chain
</h2>

An industry can have visible inputs and still fail to produce at the rate the
rest of the network expects.

Four values tell the story better than the number of vehicles parked nearby:

**input available → input consumed → output produced → output transported**

A weaving chain showed how that imbalance moves from one stage to another.

The weaving factory reached production around **136**, while one relevant
transport line was brought to **126**. The fabric-to-clothes leg was also
adjusted to **126**. Dyes were needed at roughly half that level, around **63**,
which exposed the next shortage farther upstream in the chemicals and oil chain.

The useful response was not to enlarge every line.

Once one stage could move enough cargo, attention shifted backward to the next
input that could not keep up. That is how a long chain stays readable: improve
the first proven bottleneck, then watch whether the shortage moves somewhere
else.

If production itself increases because workers or another input were added,
repeat the comparison from the industry outward. The output line may now need
more capacity even though it was perfectly balanced a few years earlier.


<h2
  id="terminal-congestion"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  More Road Space Does Not Fix Trucks Waiting for One Loading Position
</h2>

Cargo networks can create queues that look like road congestion while the real
limit is inside the terminal.

When several lines converge on one loading or unloading position, trucks may
stack up outside even though the road beyond the entrance is clear. Another lane
outside the facility does little if every vehicle still waits for the same
place.

Watch the front of the queue.

If vehicles are waiting to enter the same cargo platform, use available
alternative terminals or separate heavily competing movements where the
facility allows it. If the queue clears the terminal but then stalls at a road
junction, the problem has moved outside the cargo system.

Road routing, lane management, and through-freight belong in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road setup</Link>.
Inside the cargo network, the question is whether loading positions and
assignments can process the vehicles already arriving.


<h2
  id="long-haul-cargo"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Trucks Can Prove a Cargo Chain Before the Long Leg Moves to Rail
</h2>

Trucks are flexible and cheap enough to test a new production chain without
committing to a railway.

That makes them useful even when the final network may eventually use trains.

As production grows, however, a long freight leg can begin requiring enough
vehicles that the mode itself deserves reconsideration. At that point I stop
trying to solve the question inside the production chain.

Keep short collection and delivery work on the road where it still makes sense.
If the long-distance leg is now carrying sustained high volume and the truck
fleet is interfering with the wider network, the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals setup</Link>
is where to evaluate the next transport step.

The factory recipe does not change because the vehicle changes. Whatever mode
handles the long leg still has to deliver the same required amount to the next
industry.


<h2
  id="cargo-troubleshooting"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Find the First Place Where the Expected Cargo Disappears
</h2>

A large freight network is easier to diagnose when you stop looking at it as one
system.

Choose the item that is missing and follow only that item.

### Nothing appears at the producer

Check whether the building is actually producing. A required input, worker
connection, or production condition may still be missing.

### Cargo exists but the vehicle stays empty

Confirm that the vehicle can carry the item and that the line reaches a valid
loading terminal.

### The vehicle loads but the destination receives nothing

Check the unloading terminal and whether it serves the factory or town buildings
that actually consume the cargo.

### Cargo arrives and returns with the same vehicle

Look at destination storage and consumption. A full destination may simply be
receiving more than it can use.

### One factory input is always full while another stays empty

Compare the production recipe with the incoming line rates. Equal fleets are
often wrong when the inputs are consumed at different ratios.

### Raw materials fill the warehouse but finished goods never come back

Follow the factory leg. Confirm that every required input leaves storage,
reaches the factory, and that the return line can carry the finished product.

### The chain worked until an industry increased production

Recheck output against transport rate. The network may still be sized for the
old production level.

### Trucks queue outside a cargo station

Go to the front of the queue. If the first trucks are waiting for a loading
position, inspect terminal use before changing the road.

The first broken handoff is the one worth fixing. Once cargo passes that point,
let the chain run again before changing the next line.
