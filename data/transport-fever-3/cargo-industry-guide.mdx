A truck arrives at a factory, waits through its loading stop, and
leaves empty. The factory has goods in storage, the roads are connected,
and the line has vehicles assigned. Buying more trucks seems reasonable
until the next five arrive and leave empty too.

Transport Fever 3 has several ways for this to happen. A station can
sit just outside a warehouse's usable range. A ship may be assigned
to goods it cannot carry. A line can have the correct stops but no
permitted cargo selected for loading. Farther along the network, goods
may accumulate because the receiving industry has filled its storage
or cannot consume them yet.

I start with the cargo that should be moving, rather than the vehicle
that happens to be waiting. The important question is where that
particular item was last available and why the next transport stage
did not take it.

<h2 id="trace-chain-backward" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Start at the Destination and Follow the Missing Cargo Backward
</h2>

Suppose a furniture factory has a large stock of planks but produces
very little furniture. The plank deliveries may be working perfectly.
If glass is missing, additional plank trucks will only increase
storage and operating costs.

The same mistake occurs with town deliveries. A town might request
fuel in a specific area, while the delivery stop serves different
buildings. Fuel can reach the town without reaching the buildings
that actually need it.

To locate the interruption, examine the destination first:

- **Town:** Does the delivery stop serve buildings requesting that cargo?
- **Factory:** Which input is missing, and which inputs are already available?
- **Warehouse:** Is the incoming item accepted into storage, and is there a usable outbound connection?
- **Transport line:** Is cargo accumulating at the pickup point, remaining inside the vehicle, or disappearing before reaching the next stop?

Work backward through the affected item until you find the first
failed handoff.

For example, furniture travels through a chain involving planks and
glass. If finished furniture never appears, check whether both
materials reached the factory. If the factory has produced furniture
but none reaches the town, follow the finished product instead of
rechecking the raw-material suppliers.

The location of the missing goods determines which part of the
network needs attention.

<h2 id="industry-terminals" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Check Built-In Industry Terminals and Station Coverage
</h2>

Not every producer needs a separate cargo station.

The **Chiswick Crop Farm** has two Passenger and Cargo road terminals
available through its own Road Station options. Trucks can collect
vegetables directly from those terminals without constructing another
cargo facility beside the farm.

Using an existing terminal saves construction costs and avoids
introducing another transfer point. The second terminal becomes
useful when more vehicles begin serving the same producer.

The connection at the destination is just as important as the
loading point.

### Why a nearby warehouse receives nothing

A warehouse can appear almost adjacent to a station and still be
outside its usable coverage.

One distribution route encountered exactly this problem. A vehicle
was approaching the warehouse with goods, but the station did not
actually reach the storage building. Adjusting the stop and its
connection brought the warehouse into range.

The useful check is not whether the two structures look close on
the map. Select the relevant station and confirm that the intended
warehouse or industry is included in its connection area.

If the connection is missing, reposition the stop or correct the
access between the terminal and the building before adding more
vehicles.

For a town delivery, apply the same idea to the consuming buildings.
A stop serving the wrong part of town cannot satisfy demand merely
because it lies inside the settlement.

When the problem concerns ordinary local passenger or road-stop
placement, the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/beginner-guide">beginner guide</Link> covers those early connections.

<h2 id="cargo-compatibility" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Does a Vehicle Arrive but Load No Cargo?
</h2>

An empty vehicle does not always mean the producer has stopped
working. The cargo may be available, but the transport line has
not been set up to collect it.

This is especially easy to miss after adding another product to
an established distribution network.

### Check the permitted cargo at the pickup stop

A vehicle delivery route connected to the Warick distribution
center continued losing money without moving its intended goods.
The problem was in the line configuration: the loading stop was
not set to collect vehicles.

Opening the line settings and selecting the appropriate cargo
corrected that configuration.

When a stop has stock available but your trucks remain empty,
open the Line Manager and examine the cargo settings for that
specific stop. Confirm that the intended item is allowed to load
there and that the destination is configured to receive it.

Do not assume a newly created line automatically has the same
cargo permissions as another route using the same station.

This matters even more when a vehicle carries several cargo types.
A factory might receive two different inputs and produce a third
item, so the outgoing goods are not necessarily the same as the
incoming ones.

### Check vehicle compatibility

The vehicle must be able to carry the cargo selected for the line.

A route delivering Tools to the Warick fishing grounds once had
a ship traveling without collecting its intended goods. The
operator checked the vessel assignment, reconsidered the available
vehicle types, and changed the ship. On its subsequent visit to
the port, the vessel successfully loaded Tools.

That sequence is a useful reminder to inspect the vehicle before
assuming the warehouse or producer is broken.

For freight trains, inspect the wagons rather than only the
locomotive. A train may have plenty of pulling power but lack
suitable wagons for the cargo.

For ships, trucks, and cargo trams, check the supported goods
when purchasing or replacing the vehicle.

### Test a new line before filling it with vehicles

I prefer to run one suitable vehicle through the complete
connection before buying a large fleet.

Watch the first loading stop, inspect what enters the vehicle,
then follow it to the destination. If the item cannot be delivered,
the first unsuccessful trip usually reveals more than several
additional vehicles repeating the same mistake.

After that journey works, expand capacity according to the
actual amount of cargo waiting.

<h2 id="production-ratios" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Match Cargo Line Rates to What the Factory Consumes
</h2>

A functioning route can still deliver the wrong amount.

The furniture factory in one network consumed:

**4 planks + 1 glass → 2 furniture**

That unequal input requirement changes how much transport each
material needs.

When the glass line operated at a rate of **37**, the corresponding
plank requirement was approximately **148**. Adjusting the plank
vehicles brought their displayed transport rate to **146**,
close to the required proportion.

If glass supply stays at 37 while the plank route increases to
250, the extra plank capacity does not create additional furniture.
It mainly increases the amount of material waiting in storage.

The ratio is useful for sizing transport, but it is not a fixed
vehicle requirement. A faster truck, a larger train, or another
industry upgrade can change how many vehicles are needed to
deliver the same amount.

I compare three values before expanding an industrial line:

- How much of the item the destination can consume.
- How much is currently available from its supplier.
- How much the transport line can deliver over the same period.

The smallest of those amounts identifies the present limit.
Increasing capacity elsewhere will not remove it.

For all factory recipes, their required inputs and production
conditions, continue with the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/production-chains">production chains guide</Link>.

<h2 id="worker-transport" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Recheck Freight Capacity When an Industry Gains Workers
</h2>

Some industries benefit from passenger connections that bring
workers to their facilities.

That can change how much cargo becomes available. A freight line
built for the previous production level may no longer collect
everything the industry produces.

One crop farm gained a passenger service while other production
conditions were also improving. After output increased, the
surrounding freight routes needed more attention because the
amount of cargo available had changed.

Do not immediately double every freight line when adding worker
transport. Let the industry operate long enough to establish its
new output, then check whether cargo is accumulating at its
terminals.

If production rises but the destination still receives the same
amount, the freight network may now be limiting delivery.

If the industry itself has not increased output, examine its
production conditions before purchasing more cargo vehicles.

<h2 id="warehouses" className="scroll-mt-24">
  When Does a Warehouse Actually Help?
</h2>

A warehouse is useful when several transport stages need somewhere
to exchange or temporarily hold cargo.

A ship might deliver goods to a port before trucks distribute them
to towns. Multiple factories could supply one shared storage
area, or a production line might return with finished goods
that another service needs to collect.

Those are meaningful reasons to introduce storage.

A short vegetable route from one farm to one nearby town is
different. If the farm and consumer already have a reliable
direct connection, inserting a warehouse adds another terminal,
another transfer, and another possible failure.

Before building one, decide which job it will perform:

- **Storage:** Hold incoming cargo until the next service collects it.
- **Mode transfer:** Move freight between ships, trains, and road vehicles.
- **Distribution:** Allow several local delivery routes to collect goods from one location.
- **Production supply:** Combine cargo arriving from different suppliers before it travels to a factory.

A warehouse does not replace the destination's demand, nor
does it automatically make every nearby station a usable
connection.

### Configure the goods the warehouse needs to handle

The storage configuration must support the products entering
the warehouse.

If a distribution center is intended to receive planks, glass,
and furniture, confirm that those items can be handled there.
Also make sure its connected terminals and vehicles can move
each item through the next stage.

If one cargo type arrives successfully while another does not,
inspect the missing item's storage and line permissions rather
than rebuilding the entire facility.

A full warehouse also needs attention. Cargo waiting in storage
does not mean the next service is collecting it quickly enough.

Check whether the outbound line exists, whether it carries the
correct goods, and whether its destination can currently receive
them.


### Why a Warehouse Transfer Shows No Immediate Revenue

Cargo delivered to an intermediate warehouse does not generate
an immediate payment. Revenue is settled when the goods reach
their final consuming destination.

If the cargo is moving correctly but the transfer line still
shows a loss, compare the full transport chain's income and
operating costs in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">money and economy guide</Link>.

<h2 id="distribution-center" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How to Set Up a Shared Cargo Distribution Center
</h2>

A distribution center makes the most sense when several
cargo flows already need to meet in the same area.

The furniture network used shared storage for **planks,
glass, and furniture**, with a universal cargo station
handling the connecting vehicles.

    Planks and glass arrive at the transfer area, while finished
    furniture later returns for delivery to consumers.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The arrangement separates three transport jobs.

Raw-material vehicles bring planks and glass into the shared
area. Another service takes the required inputs to the
furniture factory and can return with finished furniture.
Local delivery trucks then collect the finished goods for
town consumption.

The network can be understood as:

**Suppliers → Shared storage → Furniture factory → Shared storage → Town**

This can be more convenient than sending every long-distance
vehicle into the town center. It can also reduce unnecessary
cross-map journeys by smaller delivery trucks.

But convenience is not the same as profitability.

### Why several distribution centers can lose money

A large network once developed multiple distribution routes
that were consuming substantial operating expenses. Several
of the largest losses came from vehicles transporting products
toward distribution centers rather than directly to final
consumers.

One vehicle delivery connection showed losses of roughly
**$2 million**.

Part of the difficulty was that goods were moving between
storage locations without completing the final delivery,
while the vehicles continued accumulating costs.

Before adding another center, consider whether it removes
a genuine transport problem or simply creates a second
handoff for the same goods.

A central storage location serving several useful routes
can make sense. Moving an item from one warehouse to
another and then to a third location needs a stronger
reason, especially when a direct connection is available.

I would keep the chain as short as practical unless
the extra transfer solves a clear capacity, distance,
or mode-connection problem.

<h2 id="confirm-transfer" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How Do You Know the Complete Cargo Transfer Is Working?
</h2>

Raw materials appearing in storage confirm that their
inbound vehicles can reach the warehouse. They do not
prove that the factory is receiving those inputs or that
the final product can travel back.

In the furniture network, the decisive moment came when
finished furniture appeared at the shared warehouse after
the factory received its planks and glass.

At that point, the remaining question is whether the town
delivery service can collect the furniture and deliver it
to buildings that consume it.

When checking a multi-stage network, use the cargo's
location to isolate the failed step.

If the warehouse has planks and glass but no furniture,
inspect the connection between storage and the factory.
If furniture is present but never leaves, investigate
the distribution vehicles and their loading permissions.
If loaded furniture reaches town but demand remains
unsatisfied, inspect the final unloading stop and its
coverage.

The same approach works for transfers between trains and
ships. Confirm that the item enters storage, becomes available
to the next transport mode, and eventually reaches a
consumer rather than remaining indefinitely at an
intermediate terminal.

<h2 id="cargo-not-unloading" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Won't Trucks, Trains, or Ships Unload Cargo?
</h2>

A vehicle returning with goods still on board has a different
problem from one that never loaded anything.

The item reached the vehicle successfully, but the receiving
stop could not accept or transfer all of it.

At **Lisburn Brewery**, incoming glass eventually filled the
building's storage capacity of **200 panes**. Vehicles continued
bringing glass, but the brewery no longer had sufficient
available storage to accept every delivery.

The line provided an option called **Force unload all cargo on
arrival**.

    to consume glass.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The distinction is important. Removing leftover cargo from
a vehicle does not automatically increase demand, factory
consumption, or production capacity.

A full destination can result from excessive deliveries.
It can also indicate that another production input is missing,
preventing the existing goods from being consumed.

### What to check before forcing an unload

If a vehicle is returning with its cargo, examine the
destination's current storage and consumption first.

- If the receiving building is full, compare inbound transport rate with actual consumption.
- If another input is missing, correct that shortage rather than increasing deliveries of the item already in storage.
- If goods are meant for a warehouse, check its accepted cargo types and connection to the unloading terminal.
- If the intended destination is not in station coverage, correct the physical access or stop location.
- If the vehicle is stopping at the correct terminal but unloading is restricted, inspect the line's cargo settings.

Force unloading is appropriate only when the resulting handling
of leftover cargo matches what you want that service to do.
It should not be used as a substitute for making the destination
accept and use its intended goods.

For lines designed to carry cargo in both directions, pay
particular attention to the load and unload selections at
each stop. An incorrectly configured stop can interfere
with the intended return load.

<h2 id="production-too-slow" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Cargo Arrives, but the Factory Still Produces Too Slowly
</h2>

Once all required goods reach an industry, freight capacity
is no longer the first thing to investigate.

A factory may be limited by its current production conditions,
its available workers, another input, or the amount of finished
goods it can hold.

Check the industry's production and stock information.
If one input is missing, transport may still be the reason
the building cannot proceed. If the inputs are present but
output remains limited, look at the industry's own requirements.

The <Link href="/transport-fever-3/production-chains">production chains guide</Link> covers the recipes, workers,
production boosters, and stock conditions involved.

When output begins increasing again, return to the freight
lines only if finished goods accumulate faster than the
outbound vehicles can remove them.

<h2 id="terminal-congestion" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Trucks Queue Outside a Cargo Station: What Is Blocking Them?
</h2>

A line of trucks waiting outside a cargo terminal can
look like an ordinary road-traffic problem.

The cause may instead be one loading position used by
several freight services.

Watch the first vehicle in the queue. If the road ahead
is clear but trucks are waiting to enter the same
terminal, widening the approaching road will not
necessarily improve loading capacity.

Industries with more than one suitable terminal can
sometimes distribute vehicles across the available
positions. Configure alternative terminals where the
facility and line allow them, and make sure those
positions can serve the required cargo.

A different problem exists when vehicles load and unload
normally but cannot leave because traffic is backed up
at a nearby junction.

In that situation, the terminal is functioning. The
approach road or surrounding intersection is limiting
the flow.

For the road layout and traffic-routing changes, continue
with the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/traffic-road-guide">traffic and road guide</Link>.

<h2 id="long-haul-cargo" className="scroll-mt-24">
  When Should a Cargo Route Switch From Trucks to Trains?
</h2>

Trucks are useful for establishing a production connection.
They can reach factories and towns without the initial
cost of stations, rail corridors, and locomotive fleets.

That flexibility becomes less valuable when one long
route requires a growing number of vehicles.

A heavily used connection between distant industries may
eventually need enough trucks that the road network
becomes congested or the cost of running the fleet
rises too far.

Before replacing that connection with rail, look at the
amount of freight currently moving and whether the
planned railway can handle it at a reasonable cost.

Keep the short collection and distribution journeys
on roads when they are still working efficiently.
A train can carry the larger flow between transport
centers without replacing every local truck.

The <Link href="/transport-fever-3/rail-signals-guide">rail and signals guide</Link> covers station connections,
crossovers, depots, and signals when that long-distance
leg moves to rail.

Changing transport modes does not change the factory's
underlying input requirements. Whatever carries the
goods must still deliver enough material to the next
industry without creating an unnecessarily expensive
route.

<h2 id="cargo-troubleshooting" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Test a Broken Cargo Line Without Rebuilding the Network
</h2>

When several freight routes are failing together, isolate
one shipment before making changes across the entire
network.

Pause the game and choose an item that should be moving.
For a furniture network, that might be glass entering the
factory or finished furniture leaving it. Do not try
to diagnose every product at once.

Open the relevant supplier, line, and destination, then
follow one vehicle through its next complete journey.

The quickest sequence is:

1. **Check the cargo stock at the pickup point.** If no goods are available, changing the vehicle fleet is premature.
2. **Inspect the station connection.** The producer or warehouse must be reachable from the terminal being used.
3. **Check vehicle and line settings.** Confirm that the vehicle supports the item and that the pickup stop allows it to load.
4. **Follow the shipment to its next stop.** Observe whether cargo leaves the vehicle and enters the expected building or storage area.
5. **Check what happens afterward.** For a transfer, confirm the next line can collect the goods. For a final delivery, confirm they reach the intended consumer.

After repairing that first failed movement, let the route
run again before adjusting vehicle counts.

A useful test changes one suspected cause and checks the
result. For example, moving a cargo stop into warehouse
coverage establishes whether the connection was the
problem. Correcting a line's selected loading item
establishes whether its cargo configuration was preventing
collection.

If one vehicle can complete the required movement but goods
continue accumulating, transport capacity becomes the
next question. Compare the supplier's available cargo
with the receiving industry's demand and the line's
displayed rate.

For a warehouse network, do not judge the first transfer
only by whether it shows an immediate payment. Make sure
the shipment can reach its final destination before
evaluating the complete transport chain's operating result.

A properly configured route should keep working when its
vehicles repeat the journey. Once that is established,
increase capacity where cargo is genuinely waiting,
rather than adding more vehicles to a connection that
has not yet moved its first successful shipment.
