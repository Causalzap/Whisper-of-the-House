A train refusing to leave its depot and two trains sitting opposite
each other at red signals may look like the same problem, but they
need different fixes. The first might have no valid route onto its
assigned track. The other two may each need a section of railway
that the opposite train has already reserved.

The distinction becomes especially important around station
approaches. Adding another signal might increase capacity on an
open double-track corridor, yet the same change can trap a long
freight train across a crossover if there is nowhere for its
wagons to clear the junction.

I usually follow the train's intended movement before touching the
tracks. Where does it need to go, which switches will it use, and
where can it safely wait? Those three questions identify most
signaling problems without sacrificing a working railway.

<h2 id="when-to-build-rail" className="scroll-mt-24">
  When Should You Replace a Road Route With Rail?
</h2>

A railway makes more sense after a road service has demonstrated
that passengers or freight actually need the connection.

On the early Spalding-to-Chiswick passenger route, approximately
**27 passengers** were waiting for Chiswick and the queue was
continuing to grow. The existing road service was carrying
passengers, but demand was beginning to exceed what it could
comfortably handle.

That was a reasonable point to consider a train. The two towns
already had a useful connection, local transport could feed the
stations, and a larger vehicle could carry more passengers per
departure.

Freight provides another reason to switch. If a long industrial
route requires an increasing number of trucks, rail can carry
the main flow while road vehicles continue handling short
connections around towns and factories.

Before committing to construction, make sure:

- The existing route has sustained passenger or cargo demand.
- The planned stations will connect to that demand.
- There is enough money to finish both stations and the railway.
- The railway offers a meaningful capacity improvement over the current service.

Do not replace every short truck route simply because trains have
become available. Rail construction and vehicle operation can be
expensive, especially when the line spends its first years carrying
very little.

For choosing affordable transport services and controlling
operating expenses, use the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">economy and money guide</Link>.

<h2 id="station-placement" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Place Stations Where Passengers Can Reach Them
</h2>

The first Spalding railway station was built beside existing
local bus stops. That mattered more than finding the easiest
piece of land for track construction: passengers already using
the buses could transfer onto the new intercity service.

The available two-track station configurations also differed.
One option displayed **296 passenger capacity and +20 comfort**,
compared with **276 capacity and +10 comfort** for another.

I would still favor a well-connected station with slightly
lower capacity over an isolated one with better specifications.

Leave room beyond the platforms as well. A short, cramped station
throat makes it difficult to add crossovers, directional tracks,
and extra platforms when traffic increases.

If two trains eventually need different platforms, both must
have a usable route into the station. Extra platforms do little
good when every service is forced through one narrow approach
or cannot reach the available track.

<h2 id="build-track-first" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Build the Physical Route Before Placing Signals
</h2>

Signals control train movements; they cannot create a missing
railway connection.

For a straightforward intercity double-track route, I normally
begin with one running track in each direction. Crossovers
near the station approaches then allow trains to change tracks
where their assigned platforms require it.

The construction order matters:

1. Connect the stations with the intended running tracks.
2. Add crossovers where trains must switch between tracks.
3. Check that each service can enter and leave its assigned platform.
4. Connect the depot to a usable direction of travel.
5. Add signals once the physical routes are complete.

A crossover must actually connect the track the train approaches
on to the track leading into its platform. A nearby switch
that only connects the opposite direction will not help.

This distinction is useful when diagnosing a line that initially
works but fails after an expansion. If the tracks were usable
before new one-way signals were installed, inspect those signals
first. If no route existed even without the restrictions,
the physical connections need attention.

<h2 id="how-signals-work" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How Do Path-Based Signals Work in Transport Fever 3?
</h2>

Transport Fever 3 uses **path-based signaling**, not traditional
block-based signaling.

A signal turns green when an approaching train can reserve the
specific path it intends to travel along, up to the next signal.
The reservation concerns the train's actual route rather than
every piece of track inside a large section.

This distinction is most noticeable at junctions.

Two trains using separate paths through a complex junction do
not necessarily interfere with each other. If one train needs
a crossing or switch already reserved by another train, however,
it must wait until that conflicting movement clears.

### Why a train waits at a red signal

A red signal does not automatically mean that every track beyond
it contains a train.

The approaching service may be unable to reserve a particular
switch, shared rail segment, or platform approach. Another train
might be physically occupying the required track or holding a
conflicting reservation.

Watch the intended movement rather than treating the entire
junction as one occupied area.

For instance, a train entering a station and another leaving
on different tracks may move simultaneously when their paths
do not conflict. If both need the same crossover, one must wait.

### Path reservations continue beyond some station stops

Transport Fever 3 changed how far a train can reserve its route
compared with Transport Fever 2.

A train can reserve beyond its next scheduled stop, continuing
until a stop where it can change direction or the next signal
along its route.

This has a particularly important consequence for single-track
lines. Two trains approaching from opposite directions cannot
rely on an intermediate station automatically separating their
reservations.

Where trains are supposed to pass each other, place signals
that establish the required passing movements.

Reversal stops behave differently from ordinary intermediate
stops. When examining a long rail line, consider whether the
train continues through the station or turns around there.

### Normal path signals versus one-way signals

A normal path signal governs trains approaching it from the
relevant direction. Placing a signal on a track does not by
itself make that entire track one-way.

The **One-Way** setting explicitly prevents trains from passing
the signal in the opposite direction.

That option is useful for directional double-track systems,
but a wrongly oriented one-way signal can make an otherwise
connected railway unusable.

<h2 id="signal-placement" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Where Should You Place Signals to Prevent Deadlocks?
</h2>

The most useful place for a signal is somewhere a train can
wait without obstructing other movements.

Consider a junction with three approaching tracks. Putting
signals before the shared crossing allows trains to wait
outside the conflict. But adding another signal immediately
beyond the crossing can create a new problem.

A long train might pass through the junction and stop at
the next signal while its rear wagons still occupy the switch.
A second train approaching from another direction cannot
continue even though the first locomotive has already passed
the junction.

This happened while adjusting an established network whose
trains had grown from approximately **160 m to 320 m** long.
Spacing that had been adequate for shorter trains no longer
provided enough clearance.

When a junction keeps blocking traffic, inspect both its
approach and its exit:

- **Before the junction:** Can an arriving train wait without occupying the crossing?
- **Beyond the junction:** Is there enough room for the complete train to clear every conflicting switch before its next possible stop?
- **Along the approach:** Can another service use a separate, nonconflicting route?
- **At the next signal:** Will a stopped train obstruct the track another line needs?

A signal placed too close to the far side of a crossover can
reduce throughput rather than improve it. Moving that signal
farther away may keep the whole junction available while the
train waits.

There is no single spacing value suitable for every railway.
Judge the full length of the trains using the route, not
just the locomotive.

### How to build a single-track passing loop

A single-track railway is inexpensive, but opposing trains
need somewhere to pass.

The usual solution is a **passing loop**, where one shared
track separates into two parallel tracks before joining
together again.

Build the passing section long enough for the trains using
it. The important detail is that a waiting train must fit
inside its side of the loop without obstructing the switches
at either end.

A practical arrangement is:

1. Choose a location between the stations where opposing trains should meet.
2. Split the single track into two parallel tracks and reconnect them at the far end.
3. Place signals at both ends of the passing section so approaching trains can obtain the required separate paths.
4. Check each train's direction through its side of the loop. Use one-way restrictions only where they support the intended movements.
5. Run trains from both directions and watch them meet before increasing the number of vehicles.

Avoid putting extra signals along a shared single-track stretch
merely to shorten the spacing. A signal in the wrong place may
allow trains to approach each other without providing somewhere
for either to pass.

If both trains stop before reaching the loop, inspect their
reserved routes and the signals at the loop ends. If they
reach the loop but cannot pass, check the switches, signal
directions, and whether both routes can actually use
different tracks.

On a busy single-track corridor, additional passing sections
may reduce delays. Once traffic becomes heavy enough that
trains repeatedly wait between those sections, converting
the busiest stretch to double track is often a more useful
capacity improvement.

### Double-track signals and station crossovers

Two parallel running tracks are easier to manage when each
has a consistent travel direction.

Choose left-hand or right-hand running and apply the same
convention throughout the main corridor. One-way signals
can keep trains traveling in the intended direction on
each track.

At a station approach, provide crossovers before the platforms
that need them. Arriving trains should not have to travel
against the direction of a one-way mainline to reach
their stops.

A busy station throat also benefits from separating incoming
and outgoing movements wherever the available space allows it.
When that is impossible, signals should hold arriving trains
before they block a departure route.

Do not assume that placing more signals inside the crossover
will improve the flow. If a train can stop while still
occupying a switch, the additional signal may create another
queue.

<h2 id="signal-direction" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How to Fix No Path Caused by One-Way Signals
</h2>

A railway can look fully connected while the Line Manager
still reports **No path**.

On a later oil-and-fuel railway, the physical connection
existed, but the signal arrangement prevented trains from
following their intended route into the terminal.

    signal prevents the train from taking its required route.

Start at the train's current track and follow the route in the
direction it needs to travel.

At each junction, check whether the intended branch really
connects to the next part of the line. Then inspect the
signals along that movement, paying particular attention to
any set to **One-Way: Yes**.

If a one-way signal faces against the required movement,
change its direction or remove that restriction where
two-way running is necessary.

Continue the inspection all the way to the assigned station
platform. The mainline may be usable while the platform
approach is not.

A train also needs a usable return journey. A line that reaches
one terminal but cannot depart correctly may have a directional
restriction on the opposite side of the station.

Change one suspect connection or signal at a time, then
recheck the line. That makes it easier to identify the
actual obstruction without disrupting other services.

<h2 id="train-depot" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Can't a Train Leave Its Depot?
</h2>

A depot can sit directly beside the railway and still be
unusable for a particular line.

The connecting spur must provide a valid route from the
depot to the track and direction required by the new train.

On the first Spalding passenger railway, the initial depot
connection joined the wrong side of the route. Correcting
that connection allowed the train to reach the intended
running track.

Inspect the spur before demolishing the depot. If it joins
a one-way running track in the wrong direction, the train
may need a crossover or a different connection.

A similar failure can occur when the depot spur reaches
the correct track but a one-way signal farther along
prevents access to the first scheduled station.

Depot connections are easiest to manage when they are built
with the intended running directions in mind, particularly
on double-track networks.

<h2 id="train-consist" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Is Your Train Slower Than Its Locomotive?
</h2>

A locomotive's advertised top speed is not necessarily the
speed of the complete train.

The early Spalding-to-Chiswick service used a **2-6-0 Mogul**
with a top speed of **75 km/h**. Three compartment cars
provided **12 passenger spaces each**, giving the service
a total capacity of **36 passengers**.

However, those passenger cars were limited to **60 km/h**.
The complete consist therefore could not reach the
locomotive's advertised speed.

The same limitation applies to freight consists. A faster
locomotive will not improve the maximum speed if its wagons
have a lower limit.

On a crowded railway, even a correctly matched faster train
may gain little time if it repeatedly waits behind a slower
service. Check the vehicle consist before buying an upgrade,
then examine the shared track if the new train still cannot
maintain its speed.

<h2 id="mixed-speed-trains" className="scroll-mt-24">
  How Do You Keep Fast Trains Moving Behind Slower Services?
</h2>

An upgraded train can catch slower traffic long before
reaching its own maximum speed.

On one shared corridor, a faster service achieved a displayed
**rate of 413** with approximately a **6-minute frequency**,
but still spent time waiting behind another train.

The problem was no longer the locomotive. Both trains were
using track that could not accommodate their different
speeds without delays.

A few adjustments can help, depending on where the faster
train loses time.

If the queue forms at station entrances, examine whether
the services can enter different platforms without sharing
a single restrictive crossover.

If the faster train catches its slower counterpart along
the open mainline, the railway may need additional passing
capacity or separate tracks for different services.

When a shared junction is the actual problem, Transport
Fever 3 also provides **Line Priority**.

### When should you change Line Priority?

Services normally operate with equal priority. You can
give one service higher priority when several trains compete
for the same railway infrastructure.

Higher-priority trains can receive preference at conflicting
movements, while lower-priority services may wait at red
signals. They also benefit from longer reservation distances,
which can reduce unnecessary braking on busy lines.

This is useful when a fast passenger service regularly
loses time to slower freight trains at shared junctions.

Priority does have a cost: the trains given less preference
can experience longer waits. Increasing priority on
several competing services also reduces the practical
benefit of choosing which traffic matters most.

Use it selectively, and observe the effect on the entire
corridor rather than only the train receiving preference.

Priority cannot create a missing connection, make an
unreachable platform accessible, or allow two trains to
occupy a conflicting path simultaneously. Where the tracks
themselves lack capacity, the infrastructure still needs
to change.

<h2 id="station-bottlenecks" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Are Trains Waiting Outside an Empty Station?
</h2>

An empty platform is not necessarily reachable.

A train approaching the station may have no crossover to
the assigned platform. It may also need a junction already
reserved by a departing train.

When this happens, look at the station throat instead of
immediately purchasing more platforms.

If several incoming trains use the same station, another
possibility is to give them more than one permitted platform.

### Set a preferred platform and an alternative

Transport Fever 3 can consider configured alternative
terminals when a train cannot reserve a route to its
primary terminal.

For example, a station with two suitable platforms can
allow an arriving train to use the alternative when its
preferred platform remains occupied.

Configure the permitted terminals for the relevant stop
in the Line Manager. Both options need valid track
connections from the approaching route.

I would check the crossover layout before changing the
assignments. An alternative platform is not useful when
the train cannot physically reach it.

For stations served by directional double tracks, assign
the preferred platform according to the approach direction,
then allow an accessible alternative where appropriate.

On a later busy railway, services were adjusted to prefer
different platform pairs depending on their travel direction,
rather than sending every train toward the same platform.

This helps distribute arriving trains, but the approach
still determines whether the arrangement works.

A train choosing an alternative may need to cross the path
of another service. If the crossover itself remains congested,
the queue can persist even when another platform is available.

### When does a station actually need more platforms?

Watch how the available platforms are used.

When all reachable platforms are regularly occupied and
arriving trains queue outside, additional platforms may
increase station capacity.

When platforms remain empty but trains cannot enter, adding
another one is unlikely to remove the underlying track
or reservation problem.

Also consider the length of departing trains. If the rear
of a train remains across the junction while it waits at
the next signal, it can block access to platforms that
would otherwise be available.

<h2 id="electrification" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Why Won't an Electric Train Use Your Upgraded Tracks?
</h2>

An electric locomotive requires compatible electrification
along its intended route.

Upgrading the main corridor may leave small sections behind,
especially around crossovers, depot connections, station
approaches, and recently constructed branches.

A single incompatible section can prevent the new train
from using the route even when most of the railway has
already been electrified.

Before replacing the vehicles, follow the entire movement
from depot to station and through any required alternate
platform connections.

Check the traction system required by the selected train
and the infrastructure provided by the tracks. Complete
those connections before assigning the replacement.

There is no need to electrify unrelated branches merely
because a newer locomotive has become available. The
important part is a continuous usable route for the
services that need the upgrade.

<h2 id="train-not-moving" className="scroll-mt-24">
  Find the First Movement That Fails
</h2>

When several trains stop around the same station, it can
be tempting to rebuild the whole approach. That is
usually more disruptive than investigating the first
train that fails to proceed.

Pause the game and select one affected service. Open its
line information, inspect the intended route, and identify
the first point where the train cannot continue as expected.

A **No path** message calls for a connection and directional
access check. A red signal on an otherwise functioning
line calls for an inspection of the movement the train
is trying to reserve.

A train stopped behind another service may simply be
waiting for the shared route to clear. If that delay
never resolves, follow both trains to find the point
where their required movements conflict.

After changing the suspected signal or connection, allow
the trains to run long enough to see whether the same
situation occurs again. A junction that works for one
departure may still deadlock when trains approach from
both directions together.

There is also a difference between a train unable to move
and a train moving correctly but carrying nothing.

If the railway is operating yet the connected industry
does not have enough goods, examine its inputs, production,
and output in the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/production-chains">production chains guide</Link>. If the goods exist but
the vehicles cannot collect or deliver them, check the
station coverage, cargo settings, and destination
connections in the <Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry guide</Link>.

A working signal arrangement should let trains repeat their
movements safely as traffic grows. Test the junction with
the trains actually using it, including the longest consist
and services arriving from opposite directions, before
adding more vehicles to the route.
