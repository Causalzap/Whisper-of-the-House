Rail solves a capacity problem that road transport has already exposed.

A passenger connection with people repeatedly left behind can justify a train.
So can a long freight route that now needs enough trucks to create its own
traffic problem. What does not justify rail by itself is simply unlocking a new
locomotive.

Once the railway exists, the difficult part changes. A train can have enough
capacity and still spend half its trip waiting because the track, platform,
crossover, or signal layout does not let it move.

Most rail failures come back to four questions:

- can the train physically reach the assigned platform;
- can it cross onto the track it needs;
- is a signal blocking the intended direction;
- and is another train occupying the section ahead?

Those checks are more useful than rebuilding a long stretch of track every time
a line stops working.


<h2
  id="when-to-build-rail"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Move to Rail After the Road Route Proves the Demand
</h2>

A railway is easiest to justify when the existing connection is already
struggling with real demand.

On the early Spalding-to-Chiswick passenger route, roughly **27 passengers**
were waiting for Chiswick and the queue was still rising. The road service had
done its job: it had proven that people actually wanted to make the trip.

That is a much stronger starting point than building stations between two towns
and hoping demand appears later.

For passenger rail, I look for a combination of:

- people consistently left behind by the road service;
- useful local transport at both ends;
- enough distance that intercity rail has a real job;
- and enough cash to finish the connection rather than only place the first
  station.

Freight reaches the same decision from a different direction. A cargo route
starts looking like a rail candidate when a long intercity leg needs many road
vehicles and those vehicles are beginning to create traffic or operating
overhead of their own.

Do not move every short delivery onto rail. Trucks remain useful around
producers, towns, and transfer points even after trains take over the long haul.

The financing itself belongs in the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/economy-money-guide">money and economy decisions</Link>.
Here, the rail question is whether the demand has already earned a higher-
capacity mode.


<h2
  id="station-placement"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Put the Station Where Passengers Can Actually Feed Into It
</h2>

The first Spalding station was placed beside the bus stops already serving the
town rather than farther outside simply because track construction would have
been easier there.

That gave the railway immediate access to passengers who were already moving
through the local network.

The station options also showed meaningful differences. One two-track version
had **296 capacity and +20 comfort**, while another showed **276 capacity and
+10 comfort**.

Capacity matters, but a slightly larger station in the wrong place is still a
weak station.

Leave some room beyond the platform throat too. Crossovers, depot connections,
and later additional tracks become much harder to fit when the station is
boxed tightly between roads and buildings.


<h2
  id="build-track-first"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Build the Physical Path Before You Start Signaling It
</h2>

Signals cannot create a route that the rails do not provide.

For a straightforward two-track intercity corridor, one track in each direction
keeps the movement easy to understand. Crossovers near station approaches then
allow trains to reach the platform or track they actually need.

The useful build order is:

1. finish the running tracks;
2. connect the station approaches;
3. add the necessary crossovers;
4. confirm trains can physically reach the intended platforms;
5. place signals after that path works.

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    Crossovers create the usable path; signals control when trains are allowed to enter each section of it.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

This order makes debugging much easier.

When a train cannot reach a platform before any signals exist, the problem is
track access. When the same route stops working after signals are added, you
know where to look next.


<h2
  id="how-signals-work"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Think of Every Signal as the Entrance to the Section Ahead
</h2>

A signal controls whether a train can enter the section after it.

When another train is already occupying that controlled section, the following
train waits at the previous signal until the track ahead clears.

That means signals do two jobs at once:

- they prevent trains from entering the same section unsafely;
- and they decide where the following train will wait.

A railway carrying only one train does not need signals every few meters.
Signal spacing begins to matter once multiple services are sharing the corridor.

Long sections make trains wait farther apart. Shorter sections let services
follow one another more closely, provided each section still has enough room
for the trains using it.

The station approach deserves the most attention because several movements can
meet there: one train leaving, another entering, a crossover movement, or a
vehicle joining from a depot.


<h2
  id="signal-placement"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Put Signals Where a Waiting Train Will Not Block Something Else
</h2>

A good signal location is a good waiting location.

Place the controlling signal before the part of the railway you do not want a
stopped train occupying.

That commonly means:

- before a station throat;
- before a crossover or busy junction;
- before a road crossing;
- and at useful intervals along a shared corridor.

A freight railway later needed exactly this kind of adjustment so waiting trains
would not stop across a road used by local transport.

Train length matters too.

A signal can appear safely clear of the junction while the rear half of a long
freight train still sits across it. When deciding where the train should wait,
judge the full consist rather than only the locomotive.


<h2
  id="signal-direction"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Wrong Signal Direction Can Create a No-Path Error
</h2>

One of the most confusing rail failures is a line that looks completely
connected but still reports **No path** or cannot connect its stations.

Do not demolish the track first.

Inspect the signals and the crossover access.

A later oil-and-fuel railway had a continuous physical connection, but part of
the signaling directed traffic in a way that prevented the new service from
reaching its assigned terminal.

Follow the line as the train would:

**Which track does it leave on?**

**Where can it change tracks?**

**Can it reach the assigned platform?**

**Can it return in the opposite direction?**

A single incorrectly directed signal can invalidate that chain even when every
piece of rail looks connected.


<h2
  id="train-depot"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Make Sure the Depot Connects to the Track the Train Actually Uses
</h2>

A depot placed beside the railway is not automatically usable.

Its spur still has to join a running track that gives the new train a valid path
onto its assigned line.

On the first Spalding railway, the depot connection initially joined the wrong
side for the intended movement. The connection had to be corrected before the
train could enter service cleanly.

  <Image

    width={1600}
    height={900}
    className="rounded-xl"
  />

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The depot spur needs access to the running track and direction the assigned train line actually uses.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

When a newly purchased train refuses to join its line, check this sequence:

1. follow the depot spur to the main track;
2. confirm the train can reach the correct direction;
3. add a crossover if it must reach the opposite track;
4. inspect the first signal it encounters;
5. then try assigning it to the line again.

That is much faster than rebuilding the station.


<h2
  id="train-consist"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  The Slowest Vehicle in the Consist Sets the Train's Useful Speed
</h2>

Locomotive speed is only part of the train.

The first Spalding-to-Chiswick passenger service used a **2-6-0 Mogul** with a
top speed of **75 km/h**.

The selected compartment cars carried **12 passengers each**. Three cars gave
the train a total passenger capacity of **36**.

Those cars were limited to **60 km/h**, so the whole train ran at 60 rather than
the locomotive's 75.

  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    The 75 km/h Mogul is limited to 60 km/h by its passenger cars, so always check the complete consist rather than only the locomotive.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The same rule applies to freight.

Buying a faster engine does not create a faster service when the wagons impose a
lower speed limit.

Before replacing a locomotive, compare:

**locomotive speed → wagon speed → capacity → actual line demand**

That tells you whether the upgrade will change the service or only replace one
expensive vehicle with another.


<h2
  id="mixed-speed-trains"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Faster Trains Can Lose Their Advantage Behind Slower Services
</h2>

Vehicle upgrades do not increase the capacity of the track itself.

On a later shared corridor, a faster service began catching the slower train
ahead and eventually stopped at a signal while the occupied section cleared.

The faster service still performed well: its displayed rate reached **413**
with roughly a **6-minute frequency**.

But the full speed of the new train mattered only while there was open track in
front of it.

When different-speed services share the same two tracks, watch where the faster
one repeatedly catches the slower one.

A small speed difference may be harmless when station stops naturally separate
them. Persistent catching is different.

Possible fixes include:

- improving signal spacing so the trains can follow more efficiently;
- upgrading the slower service as well;
- changing the stopping pattern;
- or separating the busiest service onto its own track when the corridor has
  genuinely reached that level of traffic.

Adding another fast train to a corridor where the first fast train already
waits behind slower traffic usually adds another train to the same queue.


<h2
  id="station-bottlenecks"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Diagnose the Station Approach Before Adding Platforms
</h2>

A train waiting outside a station does not always mean the station needs another
platform.

Watch where the conflict starts.

**The platform looks empty but the train will not enter.**  
Check whether the assigned track has crossover access to that platform.

**A departing train blocks an arriving train.**  
The shared crossover or station throat may be the limiting section.

**A train waits across a road crossing.**  
Move the controlling signal farther back so its stopping position clears the
road.

**Several trains queue behind the same occupied platform.**  
Another platform may help, but only if the approach tracks can actually reach
it.

Platform count and track access have to grow together.

A large station with several unusable platforms is still a single-platform
station in practice.


<h2
  id="electrification"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Electrify the Entire Path Before Assigning an Electric Train
</h2>

An electric train needs an electric route.

That sounds obvious until the main line is upgraded while a short crossover,
station approach, or connecting branch is left unchanged.

Follow the exact path the train will use from one end to the other and make sure
every required segment supports the new traction before replacing the vehicle.

This is particularly important around:

- station throats;
- crossovers;
- depot connections;
- short branch sections;
- and any alternate platform path the line may use.

Do the infrastructure work first, then introduce the electric train.

There is no need to electrify unrelated track simply because the technology is
available. Upgrade the corridor where the new train is actually going to run.


<h2
  id="train-not-moving"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Find the First Place Where the Train Stops Doing What You Expect
</h2>

Most rail failures are local even when the line crosses the entire map.

Start at the train and inspect the first point where its movement differs from
the route you intended.

### The line says No path

Follow the route through signal direction, crossovers, and the assigned
platform. Connected rails do not guarantee a valid directional path.

### The train is waiting at a signal

Look ahead to the next controlled section. Another train is likely occupying
it, or a junction conflict is preventing that section from clearing.

### The train cannot leave the depot

Check whether the depot spur connects to the correct running track and whether
the train can reach the first crossover it needs.

### A fast train repeatedly catches a slow one

The bottleneck is now corridor capacity rather than vehicle speed. Adding more
fast trains will not create more clear track.

### The train waits outside an apparently free platform

Check the station approach and platform assignment. The platform may be empty
but unreachable from the train's current track.

### The new train is slower than its locomotive specification

Inspect the wagon speed limit. The whole consist runs at the speed of its
slowest required component.

Rail troubleshooting becomes much easier when you resist rebuilding the whole
route. Find the first blocked movement, fix that block, and let the trains run
again.

When the railway itself is flowing but freight production still falls short,
the next bottleneck is no longer rail traffic. Check the
<Link href="/transport-fever-3/cargo-industry-guide">cargo and industry chain</Link>
for production ratios, loading, storage, and transfer problems.
