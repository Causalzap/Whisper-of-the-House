<section
  id="quick-answer"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  <GuideQuickAnswer title="Use the Campaign as a Readiness Check, Not a Race to Level 30">

  BOMBANANA has **30 Campaign levels**. I move forward when the current level is failing for different, ordinary reasons; I replay when the **same module or same team mistake** is still deciding the run.

  The useful checkpoints are:

  **1–7:** make the basic modules reliable  
  **8–15:** absorb Medium modules without rebuilding the whole plan  
  **16–20:** survive larger states from Switch and Maze  
  **21–24:** keep sequence-heavy modules intact  
  **25–30:** handle Random Hard pools while Chaos consumes more attention

  A lucky clear does not tell me much. A clear that I can explain usually does.

  </GuideQuickAnswer>
</section>


<h2
  id="all-levels"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  The 30 levels show where the Campaign actually changes shape
</h2>

<GuideTable
  caption="BOMBANANA Campaign levels, timers, defusal slots, and Chaos load."
  columns={levelColumns}
  rows={levelRows}
/>

The opening timer is **2:30**.

Later bombs often give **4:00 or 5:00**, but the extra time arrives with more modules, larger states, random pools, and heavier Chaos.

Level 1 contains one Cable.

Level 20 gives **5:00** for:

**Random Easy + Maze + Switch**

while **Pressure + Alarm** are also active.

Level 29 also gives **5:00**, but the defusal side has become:

**3 × Random Hard**

and the Chaos side contains:

**Slider + 2 × Pressure + Alarm**

The clock therefore stops being a useful difficulty measure on its own.

I read the level by asking what has been added to the team's workload.

Levels 1 and 2 allow **one mistake**. From Level 3 onward, the allowance rises to **three**, which helps with recovery but does not make repeated guesses a viable way through later modules.


<h2
  id="levels-1-7"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Levels 1–7 turn isolated answers into a repeatable team routine
</h2>

The first three levels stay deliberately narrow:

**Level 1 — Cable**

**Level 2 — Color Slider**

**Level 3 — Cable + Color Slider**

This is where I want basic physical language to stop costing time.

A Cable answer that still needs three rounds of “which one?” is not ready for a bomb containing four modules. If Cable itself is the delay, I go back to the <Link href="/bombanana/wire">Cable / Wire Guide</Link> before asking a later level to hide the problem inside a larger run.

Level 4 is the first real change of pace.

It brings **Direction** together with the first **Slider Chaos**.

Now a correct answer can still fail if left and right are being interpreted from different viewpoints. The Direction lookup belongs in the <Link href="/bombanana/direction">Direction Guide</Link>; at the Campaign level, the question is whether the team can settle that perspective once and keep playing.

Level 5 adds **Calculator**.

Level 6 then stacks:

**Calculator + Cable + Color Slider**

under **Pressure**.

That combination exposes hesitation quickly. Ten seconds spent rediscovering how Calculator advances is also ten seconds in which another part of the bomb keeps running.

The full Calculator sequence stays in the <Link href="/bombanana/calculator">Calculator Guide & Solver</Link>.

Level 7 removes the safety of a memorized composition:

**4 × Random Easy**

with **Slider**.

That is my first useful graduation test.

If the Easy pool changes and the team still sounds roughly the same, the early procedures are becoming automatic.

<Image
  src="/images/bombanana/bombanana-chaos-modules.webp"
  alt="BOMBANANA manual explaining that Chaos Modules remain active while the bomb is being defused"
  width={1600}

I stay in this opening block when one of these is still happening regularly:

- Cable descriptions lose the physical position;
- Direction turns into a left-versus-right argument;
- Calculator pauses because nobody knows which state comes next;
- one active Chaos problem pulls everybody away from the defusal modules.

When the module rules are fine but information keeps dying between players, the better fix is the <Link href="/bombanana/roles">Roles & Communication Guide</Link>.


<h2
  id="levels-8-15"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Levels 8–15 ask whether new modules can join the run without stopping it
</h2>

The next stretch adds three different kinds of friction in quick succession.

**Level 8** brings Symbol.

**Level 9** brings Piano.

**Level 11** brings Soundboard.

The important change is not simply that the manual gets longer.

Each new module asks the team to hold information in a different form.

A Symbol description needs to stay recognizable long enough for the manual side to find the same shape.

Piano carries a short ordered sequence.

Soundboard punishes time spent describing the wrong target.

<Image
  src="/images/bombanana/bombanana-piano-yellow-light.webp"
  alt="BOMBANANA Piano module active with a colored light and Braille information"
  width={1600}
  height={900}
  className="my-8 rounded-xl"
/>

Level 10 already combines **Symbol + Piano** under Alarm.

Then Level 12 shifts the Campaign toward mixed difficulty pools:

**Random Easy + Soundboard + Random Medium**

with:

**Pressure + Slider**

By Level 15 the bomb has reached:

**2 × Random Easy + 2 × Random Medium**

while **Slider + Alarm** keep running.

At that point, “we are stuck on Level 15” is not specific enough to help me.

I look at which part of the run repeatedly consumes the clock.

If Symbol is slow every time it appears, that is the thing to practice.

If Piano works until the sequence reaches the player who must execute it, that is a different weakness.

If the fixed modules are fine and only one Random Medium roll keeps derailing the attempt, another run may be more useful than stepping backward.

/>

I leave this block once Medium modules feel like part of the bomb rather than an event that makes everybody stop and reorganize.


<h2
  id="levels-16-20"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Levels 16–20 make incomplete information much more expensive
</h2>

Level 16 gives **Switch** its first listed Campaign slot:

**2 × Random Easy + Switch**

with:

**Pressure + Alarm**

The difficulty spike is easy to underestimate because Switch is only one new module.

What changes is the amount of state that has to survive before anybody can safely submit.

By Level 17, a Random Medium module is sharing that same four-minute run.

If Switch is still ending with somebody asking which number belonged to which light, I would rather repair it on the <Link href="/bombanana/switch">BOMBANANA Switch Guide</Link> than keep feeding the same error into the next level.

Level 18 introduces **Maze**.

That creates a different test: a route only helps after the team agrees on where the move begins and what point it is trying to reach.

Level 19 mixes Maze with Easy and Medium random slots.

Then Level 20 puts both new burdens together:

**Random Easy + Maze + Switch**

**Pressure + Alarm**

**5:00**

This is the checkpoint I care about.

A Level 20 clear caused by an unusually friendly Random Easy module can still be progress, but I want to know what happened on the fixed parts.

If Switch was clean and Maze was clean, I move on.

If one of them consumed nearly the entire five minutes and the clear came from a last-second guess, Level 21 is unlikely to make that weakness disappear.


<h2
  id="levels-21-24"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Levels 21–24 add information that has to survive longer than one call
</h2>

Level 21 moves to:

**2 × Random Easy + Morse**

with:

**Slider + Alarm**

inside **5:00**.

Morse changes the run because four decoded results still have to remain attached to four positions before the module is finished.

The dot-and-dash rules, five-signal digits, slot timing, rereads, and Enter decision stay in the <Link href="/bombanana/morse">BOMBANANA Morse Code Guide</Link>.

Here I care about what Morse does to the Campaign.

If one slot needs another cycle, the bomb is still running around it.

If another module interrupts halfway through a pattern, the team has to know which Morse progress is already safe to keep.

Level 22 removes even more breathing room:

**Random Easy + Random Medium + Morse**

with:

**Alarm + Pressure**

A team that only solved Morse on Level 21 because everybody stared at it continuously now has to prove that the same module can coexist with the rest of the bomb.

Level 23 replaces that new pressure with **Monkey-Says**.

Level 24 combines:

**Random Easy + Monkey-Says + Maze**

under:

**Pressure + Slider**

The common thread across this four-level stretch is memory.

Earlier in the Campaign, a solved Cable state can disappear from everyone's head immediately.

Here, throwing away information too soon can damage the unfinished module.

My readiness test becomes simple: can the team return from an interruption and still say exactly what part of the sequence remains?

If the answer is yes, the late Campaign becomes much more manageable.


<h2
  id="levels-25-30"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Levels 25–30 stop rewarding a memorized level solution
</h2>

The final six levels lean heavily into **Random Hard** pools.

Level 25 opens with:

**Random Medium + 2 × Random Hard**

Level 26 contains one Easy, one Medium, and one Hard slot, but all three main Chaos types are active:

**Alarm + Pressure + Slider**

Level 27 returns to:

**Random Medium + 2 × Random Hard**

Then the composition gets harsher.

Level 28:

**3 × Random Hard**

with all three Chaos types.

Level 29:

**3 × Random Hard**

with:

**Slider + 2 × Pressure + Alarm**

Level 30:

**4 × Random Hard**

with:

**2 × Alarm**

There is no stable button sequence to memorize for that stretch because the random slots can change which modules appear.

What remains stable is the team's response to whatever the pool produces.

A Hard module that forces everybody to reopen the manual mentally from zero is the obvious practice target.

A bomb where the modules are understood but Chaos repeatedly steals all available attention points somewhere else; the <Link href="/bombanana/chaos">Chaos Guide</Link> is where I fix that problem.

The late game becomes much easier to diagnose once I separate those two failures.

**“We don't know this module.”**

and:

**“We know it, but the rest of the bomb gave us no room to execute it.”**

Those are different reasons to lose, so I do not practice them the same way.

  The first Campaign appearance gives me a clean place to isolate a weakness
</h2>

I use these introduction points as practice anchors rather than as a second walkthrough table.

If Switch is the problem, Level 16 gives me a predictable Switch run before the later Maze combination.

If Morse is still unfamiliar, Level 21 isolates it more cleanly than a late Random Hard bomb.

The point is to move backward only far enough to expose the weak skill again.

Returning all the way to Level 1 because Level 22 failed usually teaches me nothing about the thing that actually ended the run.


<h2
  id="replay-or-push"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A clear only counts as readiness when I can explain why it worked
</h2>

One of the easiest traps in Campaign is winning a level by accident and treating the result as proof that the team learned it.

I have had runs where a new puzzle cleared, everyone celebrated, and then the immediate post-run conversation revealed that nobody could explain what had actually solved it.

That is a replay for me.

The result was good.

The knowledge was not stable.

A messy win can still be enough to advance when the team can reconstruct it afterward:

- the correct rule was understood;
- the right player performed the intended action;
- the failure risk is identifiable;
- another attempt would use the same reasoning rather than the same guess.

I usually keep moving after a loss caused by one isolated accident, one unusual random roll, or a single obvious misclick.

I go back when the same cause survives the retry.

Three explosions with three different causes may simply mean the level is demanding.

Three explosions because Morse position two keeps getting attached to position three means the weakness has already identified itself.

The same applies to a lucky clear.

If nobody can explain what changed the module from active to solved, I would rather repeat one level now than discover five levels later that we never learned it.

That is the Campaign rhythm I use through all 30 levels:

**advance when the problem changes; replay when the same problem follows us forward.**
