<h2
  id="two-inputs"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Two values decide the next Direction press
</h2>

Direction gives me a very small input packet:

**LED color + Braille number**

That pair returns exactly one action:

**Up, Down, Left, or Right**

There is no cable order to preserve and no second Calculator value waiting behind the first one.

The trap is that those two pieces of information may reach different players at different times.

A call such as:

**“Green.”**

is incomplete.

So is:

**“Two.”**

Together:

**Green + 2**

has a definite answer:

**Up**

Once both values are known, there is nothing useful to gain from adding descriptions of the other arrow buttons or repeating older Direction states.


<h2
  id="direction-table"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  36 valid states collapse into five rule patterns
</h2>

Direction uses:

**4 LED colors × 9 Braille numbers = 36 LED-number combinations**

The table becomes much easier to scan because four number pairs share the same four-color pattern:

**1 and 4**

**2 and 7**

**3 and 5**

**6 and 8**

Braille **9** keeps its own pattern.

That gives me five patterns instead of 36 unrelated answers.

For example, **2 and 7** both return **Up** with a Green LED.

Likewise, **6 and 8** share the same four-color mapping, while **9** must be read from its own row.

The table owns the complete mapping. The rest of the page can stay focused on what causes mistakes during an actual solve.

<h2
  id="number-patterns"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Braille 9 Is the Only Number Without a Paired Pattern
</h2>

Most Direction numbers have a partner that behaves exactly the same across all four LED colors:

**1 matches 4**

**2 matches 7**

**3 matches 5**

**6 matches 8**

Braille **9** is the exception.

Its answers are:

**Red → Down**

**Blue → Up**

**Green → Left**

**Yellow → Right**

That makes 9 the number I am least willing to answer from memory based on a nearby pattern. If the panel shows 9, I use its own row.

<h2
  id="state-changes"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  One correct press makes the old answer disposable
</h2>

Direction can move quickly through several states.

A sequence can look like:

**Green + 2**

then later:

**Red + 3**

then:

**Blue + 1**

then:

**Red + 6**

Those are separate lookups.

The earlier direction does not become a default just because the same four arrow buttons are still sitting on the panel.

My checkpoint is the press itself.

Once a correct direction has been entered, the old LED-number pair is finished.

If the panel remains active, I want two new values before another button is touched:

**new LED**

and:

**new Braille number**

This is where fast teams can make a surprisingly expensive mistake. The lookup was right; the answer simply survived longer than the state it belonged to.

Repeating the previous direction from memory is riskier than spending another second reading the panel.


<h2
  id="left-right"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Left and Right can be correct in the table and wrong at the bomb
</h2>

Up and Down are usually easy to execute.

Left and Right introduce a different failure.

The manual can return:

**Left**

while the team immediately asks:

**“Whose left?”**

At that point the Direction rule has already been solved.

The remaining problem is physical perspective.

I keep one perspective fixed for the entire session rather than translating left and right differently depending on who is speaking.

If this argument keeps happening after the table answer is known, the useful fix is in the <Link href="/bombanana/roles">BOMBANANA Roles Guide</Link>, where the team can settle the physical convention and relay instead of repeatedly checking the Direction table.

That separation matters:

**wrong LED or Braille → Direction problem**

**correct answer, disputed physical Left/Right → communication problem**


<h2
  id="wrong-answer"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  A strange result usually points back to one of three reads
</h2>

Because Direction has only two inputs, troubleshooting can stay short.

When an answer does not fit what the team expected, I check three things.

### The LED

Was the current light actually Red, Blue, Green, or Yellow?

An older color from the previous stage should not stay in the call after the panel advances.

### The Braille number

Is the number confirmed?

Direction accepts **1 through 9**, so I solve the number that is actually showing rather than trying to force it into a remembered pair.

### The current stage

Did somebody already press a direction?

If yes, the LED and number from before that press are no longer safe inputs.

That is enough diagnosis for most Direction failures.

Adding more people to the lookup rarely helps when the two source values themselves are still disputed.


<h2
  id="level-four"
  className="scroll-mt-24"
>
  Level 4 is the clean place to practice Direction
</h2>

Direction first appears as a fixed Campaign module on **Level 4**.

That bomb gives the team:

**3:00**

with:

**Color Slider + Direction**

and:

**Slider Chaos**

This makes Level 4 more useful than a later random encounter when I specifically want to check whether Direction is ready.

I would replay Level 4 when any of these still happens repeatedly:

- the LED and Braille number arrive as separate, unlabeled calls;
- the Braille number is guessed instead of confirmed;
- Left or Right starts a perspective argument;
- an old direction gets reused after the panel changes;
- one Direction state takes several rereads before anybody presses.
The goal is not to memorize every table cell before leaving Level 4.

What should become cheap is the procedure:

**read two values → get one direction → press once → clear the old state**

Once that happens without a discussion, Direction is no longer the reason to stay in the early Campaign.
