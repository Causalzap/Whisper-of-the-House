<h2 id="infection-cure">Experimental Gene Therapy is the actual cure</h2>

The first thing I separate is **slowing infection down** from **removing it**.

Phalanx Pills help with the first problem.

**Experimental Gene Therapy** solves the second.

Its inventory description makes the role unusually clear: it is an
experimental cure synthesized specifically for the Responder's DNA and is
designed for a single use.

That is why I stop thinking of it as generic medical loot.

It also weighs **1.00 kg**, so picking it up can force a real inventory
decision.

If I am healthy and still early in a run, I weigh that slot against my current
ammo, healing, and mission items.

If I am already infected, the decision becomes much easier.

I make room.

A spare weapon, ammunition for a caliber I am not using, or an optional
explosive is replaceable.

A developed Responder is not.

<h2 id="how-infection-starts">How I know infection is becoming a real problem</h2>

I do not wait for the screen to become almost unreadable before reacting.

The progression I watch for starts much earlier.

The first obvious warning is usually **coughing**.

As the infection develops, visual effects start appearing across the screen.
Veins become more noticeable, and eventually the run can reach a much more
aggressive hallucination stage.

My practical warning sequence is:

**coughing**

→ **visible veins / screen effects**

→ **hallucinations become harder to ignore**

→ **turning risk**

The exact moment I change priorities depends on what I am carrying.

If Gene Therapy is already in my inventory, I start preparing the cure.

If I only have Phalanx Pills, I start thinking about how many doses remain and
how far I am from a safe exit.

What I do not do anymore is continue looting as though nothing changed.

<h2 id="phalanx-pills">
  Phalanx Pills buy time, but I do not waste all three doses at once
</h2>

Phalanx Pills are common enough that I actively look for them in medical
supplies.

They are useful.

They are also easy to misunderstand.

The bottle demonstrated in this infection run had **three doses**.

I do not consume all three immediately.

My better rhythm is:

1. Let the infection progress until the warning effects are becoming a problem.
2. Take one dose.
3. Use the time it gives me to keep moving, search medical locations, or reach
   extraction.
4. Wait until the infection starts building again before using another dose.

Burning all three doses together wastes the main advantage of the item.

I want three separate opportunities to push the infection back, not one panic
button that empties the bottle.

The important distinction remains:

> **Phalanx Pills extend the clock. They do not finish the problem.**

That is why I am still looking for Gene Therapy after taking them.

<h2 id="when-to-start-gene-therapy">
  I start Gene Therapy before the hallucinations become severe
</h2>

Once I have Experimental Gene Therapy and know I am infected, I do not wait
until I am visibly close to turning.

The cure still has to be prepared.

In the dedicated infection test, the synthesis itself took about **30 seconds**.

Thirty seconds is not long while I am standing in an empty room.

It feels much longer when:

- zombies are pushing through a doorway;
- my stamina is empty;
- teammates are separated;
- I am halfway through a timed objective;
- the route ahead is still several rooms long.

So I prefer starting it somewhere defensible.

If I am playing with a squad, this is one of the few times I explicitly ask
somebody to cover me instead of assuming everyone knows what I am doing.

If I can close a door, stand behind a barricade, or use a temporarily secure
room, I do that first.

I do not begin the cure process in the middle of the largest fight just because
the infection finally looks scary enough.

<h2 id="use-gene-therapy">
  Use the treatment when it is ready instead of carrying it into another fight
</h2>

The final step is still an active decision.

I use the completed treatment.

I do not save a finished cure because:

> maybe I will need it more later.

If I am already infected, **this is later**.

Once the treatment finishes and the infection clears, I can return to normal
mission decisions instead of constantly measuring every room against the risk
of losing the character.

<h2 id="infection-inventory">
  What I drop when Gene Therapy does not fit
</h2>

Gene Therapy taking **1.00 kg** makes the inventory decision real.

I have had plenty of runs where another useful item appeared while my loadout
was already full.

When infection is active, I rank the slot differently.

I normally give up these first:

- ammunition for a caliber I am not using;
- a duplicate weapon I was carrying only because it looked better;
- an optional grenade or throwable;
- excess utility gear that does not solve the current problem.

I hesitate much more before dropping:

- the firearm I actually have ammunition for;
- my only healing;
- mandatory mission equipment.

The exact item changes from run to run.

The principle does not:

**if I am protecting a high-level Responder, Gene Therapy is worth more than
replaceable loot.**

<h2 id="when-to-stop-objectives">
  When infection changes my decision from progress to survival
</h2>

This is the part that matters more to me than memorizing item descriptions.

Once infected, I keep asking:

> **Can I realistically finish what comes next before my remaining infection
> control runs out?**

If I have:

- Gene Therapy already preparing;
- multiple Phalanx doses;
- a short objective ahead;
- nearby teammates;

I am comfortable continuing.

If I have:

- no Gene Therapy;
- one dose or no pills left;
- worsening visual effects;
- a long defense or bunker route ahead;

I stop pretending the objective is still the most important thing on the map.

At that point I want a way out.

giving up the better full-mission rewards. I cover that tradeoff separately in

<h2 id="infection-death">
  Letting the infection win can cost the entire Responder
</h2>

I learned this the expensive way.

In one failed run, I started feeling sick late in the mission.

Then the symptoms became harder to ignore.

I used the pills I had.

Eventually I ran out.

The infection continued into severe visual effects, and I did not make it out
alive.

The result was not just losing the loot from that match.

I received **no character XP** because the Responder died.

The Responder itself was also gone, so I had to continue with a different
character.

That changed the value calculation completely.

For a fresh low-level Responder, I may be willing to take more risk.

For a character I have already spent several successful runs developing, the
question becomes:

> Is one more optional objective really worth losing the entire Responder?

When infection is already advanced and I have no cure plan, the answer is
usually no.

<h2 id="infection-mistakes">The infection mistakes I avoid now</h2>

### Treating Phalanx Pills as the cure

They give me time.

Experimental Gene Therapy is what I use to remove the infection.

### Taking every Phalanx dose immediately

The bottle gives me multiple chances to push the infection back.

I space the doses out instead of wasting them together.

### Waiting for severe hallucinations before starting Gene Therapy

The cure still needs preparation time.

I start while I still have enough control over the situation to protect myself.

### Keeping unnecessary loot because Gene Therapy weighs 1.00 kg

When I am infected, I would rather drop replaceable gear than risk losing the
Responder.

### Continuing a long objective with no cure and almost no pills

That is the point where I stop thinking about maximum mission rewards and start
thinking about extraction.

### Using health as my only infection check

Healing normal damage does not mean the infection is solved.

I watch coughing and the visual infection effects separately.

### Holding a ready treatment while entering another dangerous section

If I am infected and the cure is ready, I use it.

There is no advantage in carrying the answer while taking another unnecessary
risk.
