<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
The biggest combat mistake I made in *No More Room in Hell 2* was treating
every zombie like something I had to kill.

My survival loop is:

**arrive with stamina**

→ **kick to create space, not to spam damage**

→ **use melee on controlled targets**

→ **spend firearm ammo on threats that can actually break the run**

→ **carry ammo for the guns the squad is using**

→ **heal and reload before a holdout locks me in**

→ **use doors and barricades to reduce the number of approaches**

→ **retreat before the room becomes unrecoverable**

Once extraction is active, I stop trying to win the last fight and concentrate
on keeping the route to the exit open.

I also do not spend Credits on a stronger starting loadout just because I can.
If Normal runs are already stable, I save the money. I spend more readily when
moving into Hard or Nightmare, where mistakes and ammunition shortages become
much more expensive.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="combat-priority">
  I fight for the objective and the route out, not for an empty room
</h2>

The longer I played, the less useful it became to ask:

> How do I kill everything here?

What matters more is:

> **Which zombies actually stop me from finishing the objective or leaving?**

If a pair of walkers is blocking the only doorway, I deal with them.

If a crowd is wandering behind me while the objective is already open in the
opposite direction, I leave.

If a runner is about to reach the teammate using an objective terminal, that
target is worth ammunition.

If a slow zombie is twenty meters away and not blocking anything, I usually
have a better use for the bullet.

This matters because every fight consumes more than ammo.

It can also cost:

- stamina;
- health;
- healing items;
- inventory space;
- time;
- the Responder itself.

I have taken a longer route around a dangerous group rather than force a short
path while the squad was already exhausted.

The route looked less efficient.

Finishing the run made it the better decision.

<h2 id="stamina-and-kicks">
  I keep enough stamina to leave the fight I just entered
</h2>

Stamina caused some of my most avoidable failures.

Running, kicking, and repeated melee actions all draw from the same ability to
keep moving when the fight turns bad.

The dangerous pattern is:

**sprint into the objective**

→ **kick repeatedly**

→ **stamina empties**

→ **another zombie arrives**

→ **now I need to retreat with nothing left**

I stopped sprinting all the way to an objective when I could already hear
zombies near the destination.

I slow down before I arrive.

That leaves enough stamina to:

- push one zombie off me;
- reposition around a doorway;
- escape a runner;
- retreat through the route I just used;
- help a teammate without immediately exhausting myself.

The same rule changed how I use kicks.

A kick is primarily a **space-making tool**.

I use it to interrupt pressure, open a path, or give another player a safe
attack.

I do not treat it as free damage.

<h3 id="kick-melee-pairing">One player kicks, another player finishes</h3>

In narrow rooms, the cleanest co-op rhythm I found is:

**one responder controls distance**

while

**another responder deals the damage.**

The first player kicks when a zombie enters the dangerous space.

The second uses melee while that target is off balance or pushed back.

That is much safer than everyone crowding the same doorway and burning stamina
at the same time.

Four players swinging at one zombie can actually make the situation worse:

- movement gets blocked;
- everybody spends stamina;
- nobody has a clean retreat;
- the next zombie reaches the group while everyone is recovering.

Two people can control a doorway.

The others can reload, heal, watch another approach, or work the objective.

<h2 id="firearms-and-ammo">
  I spend bullets on dangerous positions, then build the loadout around available ammo
</h2>

I do not save every round until extraction.

A bullet that prevents a runner from reaching the team is doing its job.

I normally spend firearm ammo on:

- runners that are closing too quickly for safe melee;
- armored or shielded enemies holding a choke point;
- dangerous enemies approaching a player who is interacting with an objective;
- crowds blocking the only escape route;
- situations where melee would leave me trapped.

I am much less interested in firing at isolated slow zombies that can be
avoided or handled safely with melee.

I have seen a player reach the end of a run with only **11 rounds** remaining.

That is still enough ammunition to solve several critical threats.

It is not enough ammunition to turn every corridor between the squad and
extraction into an empty hallway.

That is the distinction I try to preserve.

<h3 id="ammo-by-caliber">I only carry calibers somebody is actually using</h3>

My inventory improved immediately when I stopped picking up every ammo type.

If I am carrying a 5.56 rifle and a .22 sidearm, stacks of .45 or .357 are not
automatically useful just because I have room for them.

If a teammate uses that caliber, I hand it over.

If nobody does, I leave it unless we are actively planning to change weapons.

Late in one run, with **two objectives still remaining**, we deliberately
started dropping ammunition nobody could use and redistributing it to the
players whose guns actually matched it.

That created both ammunition and inventory space where the team needed it.

<h3 id="when-to-switch-weapons">
  A stronger weapon with no reserve ammo is usually not an upgrade
</h3>

I judge a weapon in this order:

**available ammunition**

→ **whether it solves my current combat role**

→ **reliability**

→ **weapon quality**

I do not reverse that order just because a higher-tier gun appears.

If my current weapon is loaded and I have enough reserves to finish the next
major objective, I need a real reason to replace it.

A better gun with only one magazine can leave me worse off ten minutes later.

I switch when the new weapon gives me something I actually need:

- a caliber the squad has much more of;
- better control over runners or armored enemies;
- a useful close-range option I currently lack;
- a more reliable weapon for the remaining mission phase.

I leave it when the upgrade only looks better on paper.

<h2 id="squad-inventory">
  I divide utility items instead of giving everyone the same backpack
</h2>

The squad does not need four copies of every utility item.

I pay attention to what other players are already carrying.

In one run I was deciding whether to keep additional barricade boards and found
that another responder already had **eight**.

That solved the decision immediately.

I freed the slot.

The same logic applies to:

- healing;
- flashlight batteries;
- grenades;
- barricades;
- spare ammunition.

I do not need perfectly equal inventories.

I need the squad to avoid being full of the same thing while missing something
important.

If one responder already has enough boards for the next holdout, another player
can carry extra medical supplies.

If one player has plenty of healing, another can keep the ammunition the team
will need later.

The question is:

> **What capability does the squad still lack?**

Not:

> What item can I personally squeeze into this slot?

<h2 id="carrier-and-cover">
  Carry objectives work better when the carrier is not also the main fighter
</h2>

Carryable mission items change how I move.

The responder holding the object often has worse mobility, slower reactions, or
less access to normal combat options.

I use a simple split:

**carrier**

+ **cover**

The carrier moves the objective item along the direct route.

The covering player keeps that route usable.

The carrier's priorities are:

- move;
- avoid unnecessary detours;
- call the destination;
- drop the object only if combat makes continuing unsafe.

The cover player's priorities are:

- remove the enemies directly blocking the path;
- call runners and dangerous targets;
- reload before the carrier starts moving;
- stay with the route instead of searching side rooms.

The mistake is letting both players become distracted.

If both stop to loot, the objective stops moving.

If both stop to fight everything, the objective also stops moving.

I want the combat to support progression, not replace it.

<h2 id="doors-and-barricades">
  Doors buy seconds; barricades buy a controlled approach
</h2>

A closed door does not need to hold forever to be valuable.

Sometimes I only need enough time to:

- reload;
- recover stamina;
- heal;
- use an objective terminal;
- move toward another exit.

That makes ordinary doors useful crowd control.

If zombies are feeding through the hallway behind me, I close the door.

Even a short delay can turn several enemies arriving together into separate
problems.

Barricades are the stronger version of the same idea.

I use boards when:

- the objective is going to keep us in one room;
- one doorway is feeding most of the pressure;
- somebody needs uninterrupted interaction time;
- the group is going to defend the area long enough for the boards to matter.

I keep them when:

- we are only passing through;
- the next objective moves outdoors;
- the door would block a retreat route I may still need;
- another responder is already carrying enough boards.

I do not barricade a room simply because the game allows it.

The barrier needs to solve an actual approach.

<h2 id="healing">
  I heal before the objective removes my safe window
</h2>

I used to hold medical supplies because I wanted maximum value from them.

That often meant waiting until the worst possible moment.

If I already know the next interaction will trigger a defense, scanner, upload,
carry section, or extraction push, I check my health **before** activating it.

I have entered a major fight with a teammate at roughly **25% health** while
another player was around half.

That is not a good time to save the bandage for later.

Later may be after the room has already filled.

I usually heal before:

- a timed holdout;
- a long interaction;
- moving a heavy objective item through a dangerous route;
- entering an area with limited exits;
- the final extraction push.

I delay healing when the current position itself is unsafe.

Standing still with a medkit in the middle of a crowd is not preparation.

I create space first.

Then I heal.

<h2 id="retreat">
  I leave while the room is still recoverable
</h2>

The hardest retreat is the one that feels premature.

If the squad has already killed twenty zombies, it is easy to think:

> We have come this far. We might as well finish the rest.

The zombies do not care how much ammunition I already spent.

I retreat when several warning signs start stacking:

- stamina is nearly gone;
- more zombies are entering than the team is removing;
- two approaches are becoming unsafe;
- a teammate is down or separated;
- nobody has a clean reload window;
- the objective can be reached from another direction;
- the room contains nothing worth the resources being spent.

Once I decide to leave, I create distance first.

I do not retreat five meters, shoot again, retreat another five meters, and
repeat until the same group catches me.

I get out.

Then I:

**reload**

→ **recover stamina**

→ **heal**

→ **decide whether returning is still necessary**

A route that takes an extra minute is often cheaper than a fight that drains
the rest of the run.

<h2 id="extraction-combat">
  When extraction starts, my definition of a useful kill changes
</h2>

Final extraction is not the time to prove that the area can be cleared.

My priorities become:

**keep the route open**

→ **keep teammates moving**

→ **board**

During one final push, the squad was already low on health and ammunition.

The useful call was not to hold the lower floor.

It was to stop fighting everything and move toward the exit.

On a crowded staircase, kicks created enough space to keep climbing without
trying to kill every zombie behind us.

That is how I use my remaining resources at extraction.

I shoot when something prevents boarding.

I kick when I need a second of space.

I heal when a safe pause exists.

I do not:

- return for another crate;
- chase a zombie away from the route;
- test a new weapon;
- clear rooms the extraction path does not use.

Once the helicopter, train, boat, or rope is ready, the last objective is
leaving alive.

<h2 id="when-to-buy-loadout">
  I spend Credits when the starting loadout fixes a real failure point
</h2>

A more expensive starting weapon is not automatically a better purchase.

During early progression I had enough currency to buy stronger gear while I
was still completing runs reliably with basic equipment.

I saved the Credits.

The reason was simple:

**my loadout was not what was making me fail.**

If I am still learning the map, a stronger rifle does not tell me which
staircase leads to the next objective.

If I keep running out of stamina in bad rooms, a more expensive pistol does not
fix the decision that put me there.

### I usually save Credits when

- Normal runs are already stable;
- I am still learning objective routes;
- the starting weapon is doing its job;
- the Responder is still early enough that I am comfortable experimenting.

### I spend more readily when

- moving into Hard or Nightmare;
- protecting a developed Responder;
- ammunition pressure is consistently causing failures;
- a reliable starting weapon prevents dependence on the first random loot room.

I buy to remove a known weakness.

I do not buy because the next item in the menu has a larger number.

  Nightmare punishes every inefficient combat habit harder
</h2>

The difficulty screen lists several changes that directly affect how I fight:

- **Nightmare mode reduced UI**
- zombies deal more damage;
- plenty of Prime zombies can appear;
- more enemies wear body armor or carry shields;
- found firearms may contain much less ammunition;
- there is less ammunition to find.

That makes inefficient habits much more expensive.

### I shoot fewer low-value targets

A wasted bullet matters more when replacement ammunition is scarce.

### I reach the objective with stamina

Higher incoming damage makes one exhausted mistake harder to recover from.

### I control entrances sooner

Doors and barricades become more valuable when the squad cannot erase every
crowd with ammunition.

### I care more about the starting loadout

Depending completely on the first random firearm is much less attractive when
the difficulty is already restricting ammunition.

### I do not learn the map here

Reduced UI and heavier combat pressure make navigation errors much more
expensive.

If I still need to learn which objective opens which route, I do that on Normal
or Hard.

Nightmare is where I want route knowledge to be solved already.

<h2 id="infection">
  Infection is the point where combat stops being the main problem
</h2>

Once infection starts getting out of control, winning the next fight more
efficiently does not solve the real threat.

If symptoms are controlled and I have a treatment plan, I keep progressing.

If infection is worsening and I have no reliable cure plan, I stop taking
optional fights.
