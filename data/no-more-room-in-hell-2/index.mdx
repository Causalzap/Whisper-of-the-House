<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
*No More Room in Hell 2* is not a chapter-by-chapter campaign.

The 1.0 game is built around two mission loops:

**Objective Mode** — larger maps with changing mission chains that end in extraction.

**Survival Mode** — smaller maps where I prepare a position, survive timed waves, regroup, and move to the next defense.

For a new player, my progression is:

**Tutorial**

→ **Solo Mode to learn unfamiliar routes**

→ **Normal multiplayer for real Responder progression**

→ **Hard once the map feels reliable**

→ **Nightmare when route knowledge and resource management are already stable**

The rule that saves me the most time across every map is:

**trust the current objective before trusting the route I remember.**

Objective branches, carryable locations, access states, and some extraction routes can change between runs.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="how-the-game-works">
  How I think about No More Room in Hell 2 progression
</h2>

There are two different kinds of progress happening at the same time.

Inside a match, the loop is:

**complete the current objective**

→ **unlock the next phase**

→ **keep the Responder alive**

→ **extract**

Between matches, I am building a character and account that can survive harder runs.

That includes:

- Responder levels and skills;
- better starting loadouts;
- Credits;
- Account progression;
- weekly Assignments and Merits;
- higher difficulties;
- achievement progress.

This distinction matters because a successful run is not only about reaching the end of one map.

A developed Responder becomes something I increasingly want to protect.

That changes decisions inside the mission.

With a fresh character, I am more willing to test a risky route.

With a developed character, I start asking whether another optional building, weapon crate, or unnecessary fight is worth the chance of losing the Responder.

The current game has **nine maps** split between:

**6 Objective maps**

and

**3 Survival maps**.

The Objective maps reward route knowledge.

Survival rewards preparation and defensive decisions.

<h2 id="where-to-start">Where I start as a new player</h2>

I get better results by separating **learning the game** from **progressing a valuable Responder**.

### Tutorial first

I do the Tutorial once before trying to learn the larger maps.

The useful parts are not just shooting.

I want to understand:

- inventory management;
- melee;
- kicks;
- basic medical items;
- objective interactions;
- carryable mission items;
- extraction.

Once those controls make sense, repeating the Tutorial gives me less value than learning actual mission routes.

### Solo Mode when I am learning

Solo Mode is where I am comfortable making navigation mistakes.

I use it to learn things such as:

- which building connects to the next objective;
- where stairs and vertical transitions lead;
- what an unfamiliar interaction does;
- where I tend to get trapped;
- how a weapon feels before relying on it in multiplayer.

Solo does not give me the normal character-progression loop, but it also removes the usual permadeath concern.

That makes it useful for reconnaissance.

I do **not** use Solo when my goal is to develop a Responder.

### Normal when I want the run to count

Once I understand enough of the map to stop wandering blindly, I move to Normal.

That is where I want to learn the real rhythm of:

**route**

→ **resource use**

→ **holdout**

→ **extraction**

while also making meaningful character progress.

Normal or higher is also where the map-completion achievements start mattering, so I prefer learning enough to finish these maps there rather than clearing everything repeatedly on Beginner first.

<h2 id="route-variation">
  The HUD matters more than the route I remember
</h2>

The most transferable navigation lesson I have from the Objective maps is simple:

**a familiar map does not guarantee a familiar mission state.**

Broadway can change branches.

Lewiston can change extraction.

Hospital can open different routes through the building.

Carryables can appear in different places.

Some interactions remain inactive until an earlier step is complete.

Because of that, I use four checks whenever a run feels stuck:

### What is still written on the HUD?

If the objective still says I need another research drive, fuse, security interaction, or sample, I am not finished with that phase.

### Did the last interaction change the route?

A completed security panel, power objective, upload, ventilation system, or lockdown can open something that was unusable a few minutes earlier.

I recheck the route after major state changes.

### Am I trying to do a later objective early?

A door that will not activate is not automatically broken.

Several maps gate later interactions behind an earlier objective.

### Has the objective already moved on?

Once the old objective disappears, I stop searching for optional leftovers from that phase.

This is especially useful with carryables.

If the power objective is complete, I do not keep searching for another fuse because I remember finding one in a previous run.

I move with the mission.

<h2 id="survival-mode">How Survival Mode works</h2>

Survival is the part of *No More Room in Hell 2* where route knowledge matters less and preparation matters more.

The three current Survival maps are:

- **Flooded**
- **Lighthouse**
- **Night of the Living Dead**

Instead of crossing a large map through a long mission chain, the squad prepares around objectives that attract waves of zombies.

A wave lasts **five minutes**.

After surviving one, I get another preparation period and access to fresh supplies before the next defense.

Surviving **three waves** leads to the final helicopter extraction.

If the team fails **two waves**, the run can shift toward Early Extraction instead.

That makes the decision loop very different from Objective Mode.

Before an Objective interaction, I usually ask:

> Where does this send me next?

Before a Survival wave, I ask:

> What will become difficult once I cannot safely reorganize?

My preparation check is:

- health is stable;
- weapons are loaded;
- ammunition matches the weapons actually being used;
- melee backups are available;
- useful supplies have been collected from the latest drop;
- teammates are back at the defense;
- we understand which approaches need the most attention.

I do not start another wave just because the interaction is available.

If someone is still returning from a supply drop or reorganizing an empty gun, those extra seconds before activation are valuable.

### Flooded

I treat **Flooded** as a positioning problem first.

Water, routes between usable areas, and where I choose to stand can matter as much as raw weapon strength.

I want to know where I can retreat before the wave becomes crowded.

### Lighthouse

On **Lighthouse**, I pay more attention to how the team moves between the preparation area and the active defense.

A supply trip is only useful if everybody can return before the next wave begins.

### Night of the Living Dead

**Night of the Living Dead** rewards the same basic discipline:

prepare before activation, control useful approaches, and avoid turning the quiet period into another unstructured loot search.

All three Survival maps also have completion achievements requiring a Normal-or-higher clear, so I eventually want successful runs on each rather than treating Survival as an optional side mode.

<h2 id="early-extraction">
  When I choose Early Extraction instead of forcing the mission
</h2>

Early Extraction is not the same reward state as finishing the mission.

That is why I do not leave just because the option appears.

But there are runs where continuing is a worse decision than preserving the Responder.

I start considering the early exit when several problems stack together:

- infection is worsening without a reliable cure;
- the squad has lost too many resources;
- the next objective is a major defense;
- a developed Responder is now at serious risk;
- the run no longer has enough upside to justify that risk.

Early Extraction can still produce account-level rewards, while Final Extraction is the route I want when character progression and the full mission payout still look achievable.

<h2 id="weapons-loadouts">
  I choose weapons around the run, not a permanent tier list
</h2>

The weapon question I care about most during a mission is usually not:

> What is the strongest gun in the game?

It is:

> Is this weapon better for the situation I am actually in?

A weapon becomes much less attractive when switching to it means abandoning a gun with a healthy reserve of matching ammunition.

My quick check is:

**usable weapon**

+ **matching ammunition**

+ **a role I actually need**

+ **enough inventory space for the rest of the mission**

That often beats chasing a theoretical upgrade.

### Ammunition can decide the weapon

If I already have a reliable firearm with plenty of ammunition, I rarely replace it with a more exciting weapon that has almost no reserves.

I also stop carrying large amounts of ammunition for weapons nobody in the squad uses.

### A flashlight can matter more than extra damage

Dark interiors change what I consider a usable weapon.

A gun I can actually navigate and fight with can be more valuable than another weapon that looks stronger on paper but makes the current route harder to control.

### Melee stays relevant

Melee is not something I discard once firearms appear.

It lets me solve low-pressure enemies without turning every corridor into an ammunition problem.

That becomes more important as difficulty rises and replacement ammunition becomes less generous.

### I buy a starting loadout to solve a failure point

If Normal is already stable, I am comfortable saving Credits.

If Hard or Nightmare runs repeatedly begin with weak equipment and that is genuinely causing the failure, paying for a more dependable start makes more sense.

A stronger loadout is useful when it fixes the run.

It is not a substitute for learning the map.

<h2 id="responder-progression">
  Responder progression changes how much risk I am willing to take
</h2>

The campsite can hold up to **three Responders**.

Each character begins with a background and starting Skill, then becomes more valuable as successful runs add progression.

That creates a natural change in how I play.

A fresh Responder is where I experiment.

A developed Responder is where I become much more selective about unnecessary risk.

### I choose skills around the problem I keep having

I do not try to build every Responder from one universal template.

The useful question is:

> What keeps ending this character's runs?

If the problem is surviving contact, I value skills that help keep the character alive.

If the run is stable but objective pressure is the issue, utility becomes more attractive.

If ammunition and combat are already comfortable, another combat-oriented choice may matter less than something that improves reliability elsewhere.

The build only has value if I can bring the Responder home.

### Credits are a resource, not a requirement to shop

Having Credits does not mean I need to spend them before every mission.

I save when the current starting gear already supports the difficulty I am playing.

I spend more willingly when:

- moving into a harder difficulty;
- protecting a developed character;
- the opening equipment is repeatedly causing failed runs;
- reducing early loot dependence makes the mission more reliable.

### Rescue Beacon is insurance

A **Rescue Beacon** is something I consider for a Responder I particularly care about preserving.

It occupies a loadout slot and is consumable, so I do not treat it as free protection.

For a fresh disposable character, that slot may be more useful elsewhere.

For a developed Responder whose loss would erase a lot of successful progression, the trade changes.

<h2 id="difficulty">
  I move up in difficulty when the route is already under control
</h2>

Difficulty is not only a reward multiplier.

It changes how expensive mistakes become.

As I move from Beginner toward Normal, Hard, and Nightmare, I have less room for:

- bad stamina use;
- wasted ammunition;
- route confusion;
- poor positioning;
- careless Responder loss.

Nightmare is where I expect those weaknesses to be exposed.

The current difficulty screen recommends a **level 40+ Responder**.

My real readiness test is:

**Do I know where the major progression gates are?**

**Can I reach the difficult holdouts with resources left?**

**Do I know when to retreat instead of trying to clear the room?**

**Can I recognize when extraction has become more important than loot?**

If the answer is yes, increasing difficulty makes sense.

If I am still getting lost on Normal, a higher-level Responder does not solve that problem.

<h2 id="assignments-merits">
  I stack weekly Assignments onto runs I already want to play
</h2>

The weekly Assignment system gives me **four Assignments** to work around.

I check them before choosing secondary goals for the next set of runs.

The best tasks are the ones that naturally overlap with something I already need.

For example, if I need another map completion and an Assignment wants a weapon or barricade action I can reasonably perform there, I combine them.

I do not turn a healthy mission into a bad run just to finish one counter earlier.

The order stays:

**keep the Responder alive**

→ **finish the map objective**

→ **take Assignment progress when it fits**

Assignments also feed **Merits**, so they remain useful even after I am comfortable with the basic maps.

Merits can support selected purchases, cosmetics, and items such as the Rescue Beacon.

That makes weekly progress another part of the broader account loop rather than a separate activity I need to grind in isolation.

<h2 id="achievements">
  My route for all 35 achievements
</h2>

There are currently **35 base-game achievements**.

Most of them overlap with ordinary play.

Map completions, kills, healing, revives, sharing, Credits, Account levels, and Responder progression all move while I am learning the game.

The achievements I deliberately plan around are the ones with restrictive conditions or long-term setup, including:

- extracting without firing a gun;
- completing a Pacifist run;
- the Model 13 headshot condition;
- Cowboy Action Shooter;
- General Contractor;
- keeping three level-50 Responders at the same time;
- 10 successful Nightmare completions.

I do not bend early learning runs around all of those restrictions.

First I learn the maps.

Then I build reliable Responders.

After that, an achievement run only needs to solve the unusual condition instead of solving the condition and learning the entire game at the same time.
