<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Get the **Prototype Train Key** from the tunnel containing Alice's journal and unlock the train. Check its Whale Oil and Water tanks, start the firebox, and let steam pressure rise above **25 PSI**. Use **Slow gear** for ordinary travel and save **Fast gear** for obstacles that require a harder impact.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

The Prototype Train can have fuel in its tank, water in its boiler, and a clear track ahead without being ready to move. The missing ingredient is often steam pressure. Once the engine is burning, the pressure gauge determines whether you can drive and how long you can maintain a higher gear.

Driving is only half the job. Damaged rails can destroy the wheels, sustained braking wears down the Brake Shoes, and collisions eat away at the Cowcatcher. Allowing those parts to deteriorate makes subsequent travel more expensive and less controllable.

In Iron Road's initial v2.58 configuration, there are several important thresholds: **25 PSI to move, speed 7 for a medium impact, speed 12 for a heavy impact, and specific repair-cost increases once component condition falls too low**. Knowing when to slow down, accelerate, or stop for repairs makes a much greater difference than running at maximum speed everywhere.

## Starting the Prototype Train: Key, Fuel, Water, and Pressure

The Prototype Train is hidden inside a tunnel on Anna Island. Alice's journal and the **Prototype Train Key** are found in the same tunnel, giving you access to the locomotive.

If you have not found the key, follow Alice's [lighthouse and church clues](/the-long-dark-iron-road/anna-island-walkthrough) before attempting to unlock the locomotive.

Alice has already spent months recovering the old locomotive, so reaching it does not mean constructing a train from individual components. Your immediate job is to gain access, check the existing machinery, and prepare it for travel.

### Check the two tanks before lighting the engine

The locomotive uses **Whale Oil**, not ordinary wood or coal, as its main engine fuel.

It has two reservoirs:

- **Whale Oil Tank — 500 L maximum capacity**
- **Water Tank — 600 L maximum capacity**

The starting fill amounts can vary. The initial configuration generally gives the Whale Oil Tank more than 90% of its capacity and the Water Tank approximately 75–90%.

That is a substantial amount of fuel and water, but it is not a reason to ignore the gauges. Both resources are consumed while the locomotive's firebox is operating.

You can replenish them using carried supplies or the dedicated Whale Oil and Water facilities distributed along the railway. The train's refilling interactions handle quantities in **0.5 L increments**.

When choosing what to carry away from a refill location, prioritize the reservoir that is closer to empty. Adding more oil will not compensate for an exhausted water supply.

### Build enough steam pressure to move

Once the firebox is burning, the train begins generating steam.

The important pressure figures are:

- **Below 25 PSI:** Insufficient pressure to drive.
- **25 PSI and above:** Enough pressure for the locomotive to move.
- **300 PSI:** Normal upper pressure limit.
- **Up to 400 PSI:** Overpressure range with additional boiler risks.

The engine generates approximately **7 PSI per second** while burning normally.

Wait for pressure to build before attempting to accelerate. A train with an active firebox may still refuse to move when the gauge has not yet reached the minimum requirement.

Getting just above 25 PSI is enough to begin driving, but it leaves very little pressure available for extended Fast-gear operation. Allowing the gauge to climb first gives you more freedom to deal with an obstacle farther down the track.

<section id="pressure-gears">

## Slow Gear vs. Fast Gear: How Steam Pressure Changes

There is a substantial difference between the two operating gears.

**Slow gear uses 5 PSI per second**, while the burning engine generates 7 PSI per second. That leaves a positive pressure balance of roughly **2 PSI per second** until the normal operating limit is reached.

**Fast gear uses 14 PSI per second**. With only 7 PSI being generated, the gauge loses approximately **7 PSI per second** while Fast gear is engaged.

Starting from 300 PSI, the difference between 300 and the 25-PSI minimum is 275 PSI. At a net loss of 7 PSI per second, Fast gear has roughly **39 seconds** of usable pressure before falling below the driving threshold.

That calculation assumes normal pressure generation and consumption throughout the interval. It is not a guaranteed travel distance.

### Why Slow gear is better for routine travel

Slow gear has a maximum speed of **8** in the train's speed system.

That is sufficient for ordinary movement and many railway obstacles, and its positive steam balance makes it easier to preserve operating pressure during a long journey.

The main advantage is not simply reduced pressure consumption. It also avoids repeatedly draining the gauge and stopping to rebuild steam before every difficult section.

### When Fast gear is worth using

Fast gear is necessary when the obstacle ahead requires an impact at **speed 12 or higher**.

Running Fast gear continuously is a poor choice. The locomotive spends steam more quickly than it generates it, so the pressure reserve falls even while the engine is burning.

For a difficult obstacle, the useful sequence is:

1. Build a substantial pressure reserve before approaching it.
2. Use the available straight track to accelerate toward the required impact speed.
3. Engage Fast gear when the obstacle requires a high-speed collision.
4. Return to Slow gear after clearing it so pressure can recover.

The pressure gauge should guide the decision. A nearly depleted gauge is a reason to recover steam before another heavy impact, not to continue demanding Fast-gear performance.

<section id="obstacles">

## Breaking Railway Obstacles Without Wasting Pressure

Not every obstruction requires maximum speed.

The train uses three important impact categories, and applying the wrong one either wastes steam or stops the locomotive outright.

### Snow drifts

Ordinary snow drifts can be pushed through at any speed.

The impact reduces train speed by approximately **1**.

There is little benefit to spending a full pressure reserve accelerating toward a simple snow obstruction. Keep moving and allow the locomotive to recover its normal speed afterward.

### Medium-impact obstacles

A medium impact requires a train speed of at least **7**.

Slow gear reaches a maximum of 8, so it can handle many of these obstructions without switching to Fast.

An ordinary solid obstruction that can be broken at medium speed causes the train to lose approximately **2–3 speed units** after impact.

The Cowcatcher also suffers **2 condition points** from a medium collision.

The efficient approach is to reach the required speed before contact rather than approaching the obstacle slowly and expecting the locomotive to push through from a standstill.

### Heavy-impact obstacles

Some obstructions require **speed 12 or higher**.

Because Slow gear tops out at 8, a high-speed collision requires Fast gear.

The Cowcatcher loses **4 condition points** from a heavy impact, twice the wear of a medium one.

Using Fast gear against every obstacle therefore creates two avoidable costs: greater pressure consumption and additional Cowcatcher damage.

Reserve it for the obstacles that will not break under a medium-speed impact.

### What to do after the train hits an obstacle and stops

A failed collision leaves the obstruction in place because the train did not meet the required speed.

Repeating the same low-speed approach will not change the impact category.

Check the steam pressure and the space available for acceleration. A harder obstruction requires enough approach distance to reach the appropriate speed before contact.

If the collision causes unusually severe speed loss, inspect the Cowcatcher's condition before making another attempt. A badly damaged Cowcatcher increases the slowdown suffered during impacts.

Track switches, gated routes, and other environmental mechanisms are not necessarily solved by ramming them. Follow the corresponding railway objective rather than treating every blocked route as a breakable obstacle.

</section>

<section id="broken-track">

## Slow Down on Damaged Track or Repair It First

Broken railway sections cause a different kind of damage from obstacles.

The primary risk is to the train's **Wheels**.

In the initial train rules, damaged track accumulates wheel-damage risk while the locomotive travels at higher speeds:

- **Below speed 7:** The damage-risk counter does not increase.
- **Medium-speed travel:** Risk accumulates at approximately 0.7 points per second.
- **High-speed travel:** Risk accumulates at approximately 2 points per second.
- **Once the risk counter reaches 1:** Wheel condition can fall by 5 points per second while the damaging condition continues.

Fast travel through a broken rail section can therefore consume wheel condition extremely quickly.

Slow down **below speed 7 before entering the damaged section**. Merely selecting Slow gear is not sufficient if the locomotive is still traveling too quickly.

This is an important distinction from an ordinary snow drift. You can push through snow without requiring a high-speed impact, while broken track can punish you for entering too quickly.

### Repairing the track

Damaged track can also be repaired.

The initial repair rule requires a **Hammer**, takes **2 in-game hours**, and does not consume additional repair material.

This makes track repair particularly attractive when compared with repeatedly damaging your train's wheels and then spending Scrap Metal on locomotive repairs.

The decision depends on your immediate situation:

- With a Hammer and enough safe time available, repairing the damaged section avoids continued wheel deterioration.
- Without the opportunity to repair, crossing at low speed protects the train better than rushing.
- With already damaged wheels, another high-speed crossing adds to an existing speed and fuel-efficiency penalty.

A broken rail section should not be treated as a challenge to accelerate through. It is a reason to slow down or stop and repair the track.

## Train Repairs: Wheels, Brake Shoes, Cowcatcher, and Boiler

**Four train components require condition management.** Their damage affects different aspects of driving, so the next repair should be chosen based on the part's condition and the risk immediately ahead.

Carry a **Hammer** for train repairs. The metal components use Scrap Metal, while worn Brake Shoes require their own replacement item.

Repairing the Wheels or Cowcatcher before they fall below 50% condition, or the Boiler before it falls below 25%, avoids the higher material costs shown in the table.

Low-condition repairs take longer. Although some restore more condition points, the increased Scrap Metal requirement can still make repeated late repairs more expensive.

### Wheels: repair before speed and fuel penalties accumulate

Wheel damage can make the locomotive slower and more expensive to operate.

The initial performance thresholds are:

- **Below 75% condition:** Maximum speed falls to 90% and fuel consumption increases by 10%.
- **Below 50%:** Maximum speed falls to 75% and fuel consumption increases by 15%.
- **At 0%:** Maximum speed falls to 50% and fuel consumption increases by 25%.

A normal Wheel repair restores 20 condition points. Once condition falls below 50%, a repair restores 25 points but requires twice the Scrap Metal and more time. Repair earlier when metal supplies are limited, especially before crossing damaged track.

### Brake Shoes: replace them before stopping becomes unreliable

The Brake Shoes are worn down by braking.

The wear rate is approximately:

- **0.25 condition points per second** while braking at ordinary speed.
- **0.5 condition points per second** while braking above speed 8.

As long as Brake Shoe condition remains above zero, braking retains its normal effectiveness.

Once the component is completely worn out, braking force can fall to **25% of normal**.

That produces a much longer stopping distance at precisely the time you may need to stop before a dangerous stretch of railway.

A replacement **Brake Shoe weighs 0.5 kg** and restores the component to full condition. Keep a spare in the train's storage so you can replace worn brakes before stopping performance deteriorates.

### Cowcatcher: frequent collisions can become an expensive habit

The Cowcatcher takes damage when the train strikes obstacles.

Medium impacts cost **2 condition points**, while heavy impacts cost **4**.

A broken Cowcatcher makes collisions more disruptive. Its damaged-state slowdown modifier reaches **1.75 times** the normal impact slowdown.

After several collisions, inspect the Cowcatcher before approaching another heavy obstruction. Repairing it above 50% condition avoids the doubled Scrap Metal cost shown in the table.

### Boiler: protect it before experimenting with overpressure

The Boiler is the most dangerous component to neglect because its failure can destroy the train.

A normal Boiler repair restores 20 condition points. Below 25% condition, repairs restore 25 points but require more Scrap Metal and time. Repairing earlier is generally cheaper, particularly before using Fast gear at elevated steam pressure.

## Is Going Above 300 PSI Worth the Risk?

Normal pressure management and overpressure are two different driving decisions.

The train can reach an overpressure ceiling of approximately **400 PSI**, above its normal **300-PSI** limit.

This provides a potential **15% increase in maximum speed**, but the extra performance comes with Boiler damage when the locomotive is operating above 300 PSI in Fast gear.

In the initial v2.58 rules, the Boiler can lose condition at a rate equivalent to **100 condition points per in-game hour** during this combination.

That may sound manageable at high Boiler condition, but the additional explosion checks are much more dangerous.

While operating above 300 PSI in Fast gear:

- At Boiler condition below **50%**, an explosion check can occur every 10 seconds, with a 50% chance.
- At condition below **25%**, that chance increases to 75%.
- At **0% Boiler condition**, the Boiler explodes.

These are the launch-version overpressure thresholds, not a reason to operate continuously near them. A brief gain in maximum speed is not worth destroying a valuable Survival run.

Overpressure damage is associated with the combination of **pressure above 300 PSI and Fast gear**, not simply having a working firebox.

For ordinary travel, use normal pressure and Slow gear. A high-speed obstacle already demands enough planning around acceleration and available steam; adding an overpressure risk should not be your default solution.

The practical rule is to protect Boiler condition first and treat overpressure as an exceptional maneuver rather than a routine driving mode.

## How Long Do Whale Oil and Water Last?

The firebox consumes approximately:

- **0.5 L of Whale Oil per minute**
- **0.5 L of Water per minute**

At that burn rate, a completely full **500-L Whale Oil Tank** supplies approximately **1,000 minutes**, or **16 hours and 40 minutes**, of continuous burning.

A full **600-L Water Tank** would last approximately **1,200 minutes**, or **20 hours**.

These are theoretical durations based on full reservoirs and the stated burn rate. They are not promises of how far the train can travel, and worn Wheels can impose additional fuel-efficiency penalties.

The initially available supply will also be lower than those maximums when the tanks are not completely filled.

### Fuel and water stations matter more than carried cans

Train-scale consumption is large compared with portable Whale Oil containers.

A standard Whale Oil can holds up to **5 L**, a small fraction of the train's 500-L capacity.

Carry portable reserves when useful, but plan longer journeys around the railway's dedicated refill facilities instead of assuming a few carried cans can replace a nearly empty locomotive tank.

Alice marks some fuel and water stations on the train map, and other stations may exist along the route.

Before leaving a refill location, inspect both reservoirs. A full oil tank is not useful when the train has too little water to keep its steam engine operating.

### When to turn the firebox off

The burn rate applies while the firebox is running, not only while the locomotive is covering ground.

Extended stops with the engine burning therefore consume supplies without providing additional distance.

The tradeoff is cabin warmth and immediate access to steam pressure. Keeping the engine hot may be useful when you are sheltering inside or expect to move again shortly. A prolonged stationary stay consumes resources that could otherwise support travel.

Use the train's interior warmth and available shelter deliberately rather than leaving the engine burning without considering its fuel and water cost.

<section id="mobile-shelter">

## Using the Prototype Train as a Mobile Shelter

The Prototype Train is also a shelter with its own heating, sleeping, cooking, and storage functions.

This makes it useful for more than transporting a survivor between railway sections.

### Cabin warmth and retained heat

With the firebox fully heated, the cabin can receive approximately **+20°C of warmth**.

The train also accumulates residual warmth during sustained heating.

For each hour spent at full operating heat, it can store approximately **12 minutes of residual warmth**, up to a maximum of **3 hours**.

After the firebox is extinguished, that stored warmth can keep the interior warmer until the reserve is exhausted.

The decision to shut the engine down therefore depends partly on how much heat has accumulated. A brief warm-up does not create the same residual shelter time as prolonged operation.

### Bed, cooking, and storage

Inside the train, the initial equipment includes:

- **A bed:** Approximately +6°C sleeping warmth and 2% Condition recovery per hour.
- **A cooking position:** Space for a cooking pot, allowing food and water preparation.
- **A storage cabinet:** **45 kg capacity** for equipment and provisions.

These functions are particularly valuable when your survival inventory is already heavy with food, tools, and repair materials.

Store the things you expect to use repeatedly rather than carrying them through every nearby exploration stop. A spare Brake Shoe, Hammer, and Scrap Metal are worth keeping available when your next objective is farther along the railway.

There are restrictions:

- You cannot freely drop items onto the train's floor as ordinary ground storage.
- Sleeping and passing time are unavailable while the train is running.

The cabinet's 45-kg limit therefore matters. The train is not an unlimited warehouse merely because it is large enough to enter.

Stop the locomotive before planning to rest, organize supplies, or recover Condition. Use the cabin's warmth and equipment while accounting for the resources still being consumed by an active firebox.

</section>
