<h2 id="take-human-to-exodus">
  Take the grown human southeast from Lazarus
</h2>

The first Exodus run starts immediately after Lazarus finishes growing a human. The objective changes to **Time to Let Go Soon** and points southeast, telling me to escort the grown human to Exodus Station and bring them safely to the departure hall.

At that point I stop changing the human.

The first story launch does not need another round of profession optimization just because Lazarus still lets me work with food and Memories. If the human is not viable yet, the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/human-growth-professions-guide">Human Growth & Professions Guide</Link> covers that system. Once this quest appears, my problem has changed from making a human to transporting one.

Carrying them immediately reveals a practical problem: humans are **heavy**.

On my first trip, picking up the grown human made jumping effectively impossible. That matters around Lazarus because stairs, deck edges, cables, machinery, and the boat itself suddenly become much more awkward while a full person is attached to me.

I carry the human only as far as necessary, put them somewhere stable on the boat, and finish the departure setup with my hands free. A flat section of deck away from the edge is much safer than leaving them in a walkway where turning the boat or grabbing a cable can knock them around.

The objective says southeast, but blindly sailing toward the largest structure I can see is not the fastest way to solve Exodus.

There is a relay stop first.

<h2 id="navigation-beacon-32">
  Navigation Beacon 32 comes before the rocket launch pad
</h2>

The first major structure I reached on the southeast route was **Navigation Beacon 32**.

That is where the route can become confusing, because it looks important enough to be the destination. It has industrial structures, power equipment, enemies, fuel, and the vertical scale I expected from a launch facility.

It is **not** the rocket launch pad.

I know I am on the correct path when the relay becomes the immediate job. Its purpose is to restore enough navigation and communications infrastructure to expose what is nearby, including the route I actually need for Exodus.

So I leave the human on the boat while I work.

Dragging a grown human through the relay structure adds weight and movement restrictions without helping the repair. The departure hall is farther along; the relay only needs me, power, repair materials, and enough combat capability to reach its upper sections.

This is also a good place to save.

The relay is large enough that a failed power setup or an accidental fall can cost more time than the save itself.

<h2 id="power-exodus-relay">
  Power the relay before stripping the station for materials
</h2>

The relay has plenty to loot, which is exactly why I avoid turning the first visit into a salvage run.

On the early setup I used, two batteries were available inside the relay route, but they were not enough for what I was trying to power. I ended up bringing **two more**, working around a four-battery setup while restoring the station.

I would not treat “four batteries” as a universal recipe for every future build. What matters is the lesson behind it: the relay can require more stored power than the first obvious battery room provides.

Repairable wind generation around the facility helped stabilize the setup as well. Once the renewable side was producing consistently, carrying electrical power higher into the structure became much less painful than relying on whatever charge happened to be left in one portable battery.

The real enemy here turned out to be inventory weight.

During that first relay run I reached:

**230 kg carried / 200 kg capacity**

That is **30 kg overweight**.

At that point movement through the vertical structure became much worse, and I was still trying to power a relay rather than haul the entire building home.

<GuideCallout title="Leave the heavy salvage for later">
Keep repair parts, batteries, cable, and enough free capacity to move comfortably. Exodus progression is worth more than another load of chairs, screens, petrol containers, and scrap while the relay is still offline.
</GuideCallout>

I do take useful materials that are directly beside the route, especially anything that solves a current repair. Everything else can stay.

One detail is worth planning before climbing too far: **keep some repair material with you**. Reaching an upper objective and discovering the useful fabric, plastic, or components are back on the boat turns a vertical station into a commute.

For general ship-side battery and routing behavior, I keep the larger electrical rebuild in the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/power-fuel-wall-outlets">Power, Fuel & Wall Outlets Guide</Link>. At Exodus I only build enough network to make the station do its job.

The upper relay route has enemies and enough elevation that I keep a ranged weapon available instead of hauling a character full of scrap. The launch pad can already be visible from high sections of the facility, but I finish the array job before abandoning the relay.

This is one of the places where the game rewards finishing the active state rather than following whatever looks interesting in the distance.

After the communications route came online, the surrounding facilities became much easier to identify. From the upper structure I could finally pick out the place that matched what I had been looking for: the rocket facility.

That is my cue to return to the boat.

The human has been waiting safely there while the dangerous, vertical repair work was happening. Now there is an actual destination worth carrying them into.

<h2 id="reach-exodus-launch-facility">
  Move the human only after the launch route is open
</h2>

The quest wording matters here: the grown human needs to reach the **exit station departure hall**.

I keep that distinction in mind because Exodus is larger than a single rocket platform. The relay, communications structure, landing-pad side, elevators, fuel infrastructure, and rocket-side systems can all look like separate destinations when I first arrive.

For the first human, I want the station route active before I take them off the boat.

That keeps the awkward part of the journey short.

Carrying a human around an unpowered facility while searching for the right door makes every staircase and platform slower. Once the relay and communications work has already revealed the launch area, the human only needs to travel through the part of Exodus that is actually relevant to departure.

I also resist another temptation: there is a lot of petrol and useful industrial material around Exodus.

It can wait.

The first human has already consumed a long Lazarus chain, a southeast voyage, relay repair, and communications work. Finishing that progression is worth more than filling my inventory before the final handoff.

Once Exodus becomes a repeat-launch base, salvage and infrastructure upgrades make much more sense because I know which parts of the site I will revisit.

<h2 id="move-pods-to-rocket">
  For later launches, move pods from the landing pad to the rocket in small batches
</h2>

Repeat launches are where Exodus logistics get much easier.

On a mature 5.5 setup, I stopped using the front entrance as the default cargo route. Instead, I dock near the **landing pad and the stairs**, where the trip from boat to rocket is much shorter.

The method that worked reliably for me was:

1. sail the pods to Exodus;
2. dock beside the landing-pad side;
3. move pods up to the deck;
4. use the gyrocopter for **two pods at a time**;
5. drop them near the base of the rocket where the elevator stops;
6. put the pods onto the elevator and take them up.

Two at a time is a good practical limit with the gyrocopter. The pods already affect how the aircraft handles, and a damaged gyro makes the trip noticeably worse. Slow movements work better than aggressive banking while cargo is hanging underneath.

The boat is slower for pure transport, but it has one advantage: I can keep crafting and organizing while sailing.

That gives me a real choice.

For a short, known route with a healthy gyrocopter, flying the cargo can save time. When I need to move several pods, replenish Exodus supplies, bring oil, or fabricate other equipment on the journey, the boat earns the longer travel time.

I choose the vehicle based on the whole job rather than the distance alone.

<h2 id="launch-the-rocket">
  Do not start the countdown until the rocket is green across the board
</h2>

Once the payload is in position, I look at the rocket state rather than assuming that being physically beside the rocket means it is ready.

On the working launch setup, there was still a payload state to satisfy and a disconnected connection could hold things up. After the doors and support state were behaving correctly, the important confirmation was simple:

**green across the board.**

That is the point where I am comfortable committing the launch.

The countdown then moves quickly. In the working launch I watched the final sequence move through the last seconds, the guidance system took over, ignition started, and the rocket lifted off.

That visual confirmation matters.

A pod sitting at the rocket is not the same thing as a completed launch. A door being open is not the same thing as the whole system being ready. The status check before countdown removes a lot of ambiguity.

For the first human, this is the payoff to the whole Lazarus-to-Exodus chain.

For later humans, it turns Exodus into infrastructure rather than a mystery. The route is known, the elevator is known, the cargo staging point is known, and the launch status gives me a clear final check.

<h2 id="rocket-support">
  Keep Exodus supplied if you plan to launch more humans
</h2>

A single successful launch does not mean the station will remain ready forever.

On a developed Exodus setup, I keep support storage there because repeat launches use local infrastructure. Oil is especially useful in my arrangement because the gas-generation side lives at Exodus rather than on the boat.

After a launch, the rocket may also need time and resources to recover for the next payload. During one repeat cycle I was already thinking about reconnecting the rocket so it could start recharging again while waiting for the next pod.

That is when permanent local storage starts to make sense.

It is also why I would not dismantle every battery, tank, generator, and connection at Exodus after the first launch just because the immediate quest has completed. A small working station saves a huge amount of setup on the second and third human.

The exact size of that infrastructure depends on how often I launch.

One story launch does not justify turning Exodus into a giant refinery. Repeated Council or human launches do.

<h2 id="exodus-launch-stuck">
  If the Exodus rocket will not launch, check the state that stopped changing
</h2>

**The human is ready, but I cannot find Exodus**

Follow the quest southeast from Lazarus. Navigation Beacon 32 is part of the progression route; it is not the final launch pad.

**I reached Navigation Beacon 32 and nothing points to the rocket**

Power the relay first. Once the relay advances, repair the communications array. The surrounding launch facilities become much easier to identify after that state is restored.

**The relay still will not power up**

Look for the existing batteries and generation before carrying the entire boat's electrical system inside. On the early run, two batteries were found on site and two more had to be brought into the working setup.

**I can barely move around the relay**

Check inventory weight before blaming the station. I hit **230 / 200 kg**, which turned the vertical repair route into unnecessary work. Store common salvage and keep only what the current repair needs.

**I can see the launch facility, but the human is still on the boat**

That is fine while the relay work is happening. Once the departure route is active, return for the human and bring them to the exit station departure hall rather than carrying them through every repair room.

**The pod is near the rocket, but launch will not start**

Read the launch states. Check payload position, rocket doors, and any disconnected support connection before assuming the pod itself is broken. The working state reached **green across the board** before countdown.

**The rocket worked before, but the next launch is not filling**

This can become a support-network problem rather than a payload problem. After a later launch, one setup stopped transferring correctly even though the tanks were not empty.

I checked the route between the tanks and rockets, replaced suspect connections, and eventually saw **methane and oxygen begin filling again**, reaching **23%** when the network resumed.

**The tanks show resources but nothing moves**

Confirm the physical tank-to-rocket route before rebuilding every tank. In the failing setup the problem appeared to sit between storage and the rocket rather than at the source itself.

**Connections look correct but the state still seems stale**

A save and reload is worth trying after the obvious physical checks. On the later setup, reloading was used when tank state and the visible resource state no longer agreed.

If the problem is broader than Exodus — cable mode, socket direction, tank Fill/Equalize, or a mixed power/fuel network — I fix the underlying network separately rather than making the launch facility carry the whole diagnosis.

<h2 id="when-to-leave-exodus">
  Leave after the launch is confirmed, not after every shelf is empty
</h2>

Exodus can absorb a lot of time because nearly every visit exposes another useful resource, storage opportunity, or piece of infrastructure I could improve.

The launch gives me a better stopping point.

After liftoff, I check whether the next rocket needs to recharge, make sure any infrastructure I want to keep is not being left in a broken state, and collect only the equipment I actually need back on the boat.

Then I go.

There is no reason to turn the first successful launch into a complete demolition of the relay and launch station.

Later visits are cheaper because the route is already understood. I know where to dock, where the elevator ends, where the pods can be staged, which support systems matter, and how the rocket looks when it is genuinely ready.

The first Exodus run is about opening the path to space.

After that, Exodus becomes a repeatable logistics base — and each new human is much easier to send up than the first one.
