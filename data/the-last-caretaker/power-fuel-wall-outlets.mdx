<h2 id="fix-the-bottleneck">
  Fix the network that is actually blocking the boat
</h2>

A messy boat and a broken boat are not the same problem. I can tolerate cables that look ugly if every machine starts when I need it, the batteries recover after a heavy load, and fuel reaches the right destination. Rebuilding that setup just because wall outlets exist gives me more chances to break a system that was already doing its job.

I start changing the network when there is a measurable cost to leaving it alone.

If machines shut down while generation should be available, electricity gets attention first. If there is fuel in storage but the engine, refinery, or another consumer cannot receive it, I follow that fuel route instead. A cable that repeatedly runs out of range is a placement problem, while a ship where I simply cannot remember which connector belongs to which machine has reached the point where wall outlets begin paying for themselves.

That distinction becomes more important after New Horizons. Project Eden, Oil Whale work, more tanks, larger battery banks, and gas systems can all be added to the same boat. What was readable with four cables can become difficult to diagnose after twenty.

For a returning save where several Update 5 routes have opened at once, I choose the next job in the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/new-horizons-guide">New Horizons Guide</Link> before tearing apart the whole ship.

<h2 id="what-wall-outlets-do">
  What wall outlets change — and what they do not
</h2>

Update 5 wall outlets are **buildable connection points**. They are different from the older fixed sockets that are already part of the ship, because I can decide where a new outlet belongs instead of designing every future machine around a socket the boat gave me.

There are separate outlet types for the networks that matter:

- **power wall outlets** for electricity;
- **fuel wall outlets** for oil, diesel, and petrol;
- **gas wall outlets** for oxygen and other gas routes;
- **electric cable extenders** for the gaps an outlet cannot cover by itself;
- **Grid Scan** for checking a network that looks connected but is behaving incorrectly.

What they do **not** provide is wireless routing.

A wall outlet still needs a valid source, the correct network type, usable range, a clear connection side, and a destination that can accept what the outlet is sending. Putting a socket on a wall does not bypass those rules; it gives me a better place to organize them.

That is why I judge an outlet by how quickly I can diagnose the network later. If a machine dies and I can immediately trace it to one nearby power outlet, the layout has improved. If I still have to follow six extenders through the cabin, moving the original outlet would have been more useful.

<h2 id="build-from-the-load">
  Build the route from the machine backward
</h2>

The safest retrofit starts at the thing that needs the resource.

Suppose I want a machine on the rear deck to have a cleaner electrical feed. I place its power outlet close to that section first, connect the outlet back toward stable generation or storage, and run the machine before touching the old cable. Only after the new route survives that test do I remove the temporary line it replaced.

That sequence leaves a working fallback throughout the rebuild.

It also prevents a problem I ran into often on larger layouts: removing three old connections at once, adding the new sockets, then discovering I no longer know which change killed the machine.

Electric extenders still have a place here.

<h2 id="range-blocked-output">
  Range and blocked output cause most outlet failures
</h2>

Cable distance becomes deceptive around floors, walls, and the irregular geometry of the boat. Two points can look close on screen while the connection behaves as though it is traveling much farther through the structure.

When a connection dies sooner than expected, I look at the geometry before assuming the cable itself is bugged. Moving an outlet around an obstruction can save more distance than another extender.

A second failure is even easier to miss: **blocked output**.

A tank, machine, wall edge, floor, or another piece of equipment can leave enough room for the socket model while blocking the side that actually needs to connect. Rotating or shifting the socket slightly is often enough.

For a dead outlet, my checks run in this order: clear the output side, confirm its direction, verify the network type, check range, then confirm the source and destination. Rebuilding the whole grid comes much later.

<h2 id="bulk-storage-update-55">
  Update 5.5 Bulk Battery and fuel tank capacities
</h2>

The storage calculation changed shortly after Deck Drop launched.

The current 5.5 values I use are:

- **Bulk Battery: 20 kWh / 20,000 Wh**
- **Medium Battery: 6 kWh / 6,000 Wh**
- **Bulk Petrol Tank: 10,000L**
- **Bulk Diesel Tank: 10,000L**
- **standard Petrol / Diesel Tank: 2,000L**

That gives one Bulk Battery about **3.3 times the nominal capacity of one Medium Battery**, while a single Bulk Fuel Tank holds the same fuel as **five 2,000L tanks**.

The original Deck Drop build briefly showed lower bulk values — 18 kWh for the battery and 6,000L for the large fuel tanks — before the follow-up adjustment. I use the 20 kWh and 10,000L numbers when deciding whether a rebuild is worth the space.

These larger pieces are not unlocked simply by reaching the appropriate skill level. The **MOSES Field Reserve Program** is the progression gate for the new bulk-storage equipment.

There is also a smaller Battery Barrel in the same generation of storage tools. At roughly **1 kWh**, it solves a different problem: portable or temporary electricity. It is not a serious replacement for a 6 kWh Medium Battery or 20 kWh Bulk Battery in a permanent bank.

<h2 id="bulk-battery-fill-equalize">
  Why a Bulk Battery can look half-empty even when the grid is full
</h2>

The biggest trap with the Bulk Battery is assuming that a larger capacity automatically behaves like several Medium Batteries connected together.

In a mixed bank, **Equalize** can prevent that.

During a 5.5 setup with Medium Batteries sitting around **5.3 kWh**, connecting the larger battery through Equalize pulled it toward the same 5.3 level instead of filling its much larger capacity. A 20 kWh battery sitting near 5.3 kWh is only using roughly a quarter of what was built.

The workaround is to give the Bulk Battery a route set to **Fill** when I actually want to charge the large container past the smaller-bank level.

Once the large battery has been charged, Equalize can reconnect it to the shared reserve so the network can draw from it. The catch is that Equalize can also pull the large battery back toward the levels of the smaller batteries.

That leaves me with two practical layouts.

For a boat that keeps its existing Medium Batteries, I use the Bulk Battery more deliberately: charge it through Fill and treat it as a reserve rather than expecting the mixed bank to manage all 20 kWh automatically.

At a fixed facility where I am prepared to redesign the bank, replacing more of the small batteries with Bulk Batteries removes much of that mismatch. Large storage starts making more sense when the network is built around it instead of adding one tall battery to a mature Medium Battery wall.

This behavior is worth rechecking after major Early Access patches. The capacity is straightforward; the Fill and Equalize logic is the part most likely to change.

<h2 id="bulk-tanks">
  A 10,000L tank is valuable only if the route can fill it
</h2>

Five-to-one capacity makes the Bulk Petrol and Diesel Tanks easier to justify than the battery in some boat layouts. Replacing five standard 2,000L tanks with one 10,000L tank can remove a lot of separate connections, provided the larger model fits the space I actually have available.

The same Fill / Equalize lesson matters for fluids.

Early 5.5 behavior showed large containers in mixed networks failing to rise above the level imposed by smaller tanks unless the large container was explicitly filled. When a 10,000L tank appears to stop around the capacity of the small tanks, I first change how the large tank is being filled rather than assuming its displayed maximum is wrong.

A dedicated fill route is especially useful when I want to stock the large tank before a long trip. Once the reserve is where I want it, I can connect it back into the normal fuel network.

I also avoid turning every type of fluid into one dense socket wall. Petrol, diesel, and oil become much easier to diagnose when each route has an obvious source and an obvious destination.

<h2 id="boat-or-fixed-base">
  Bulk storage belongs where space and access both make sense
</h2>

The Bulk Battery is not an automatic boat upgrade.

A row of Medium Batteries can be packed along walls and around existing equipment. The Bulk Battery stores more per unit, but its height and shape may force a larger redesign than the capacity gain justifies. If fitting one means moving grinders, crates, or a walkway that already works, I keep the smaller bank until there is another reason to rebuild.

Fixed locations are a different calculation. At Lazarus or Exodus, floor space is less sensitive than on a working boat and I care more about keeping a large reserve available for repeated use. That is where the tall battery becomes easier to recommend.

Bulk fuel tanks can be more attractive on the ship because **10,000L replaces five normal tanks by capacity**. If I can put one where those five separate tanks and their connections used to live, the simplification is immediately useful. Under-deck space can also work for large storage when I already use that area, though I still keep anything that needs frequent manual interaction somewhere reachable.

The build earns its footprint when it removes other hardware or prevents a real capacity problem. A larger number on the inspect screen is not enough by itself.

<h2 id="fuel-gas-routing">
  Keep fuel and gas routes readable before adding more capacity
</h2>

Fuel and gas wall outlets follow the same connection rules as power, but the consequences of mixing routes are harder to spot because several tanks can sit beside each other while carrying completely different resources.

For fuel, I keep oil, diesel, and petrol identifiable from source to destination. An Oil Whale transfer, for example, needs a route that actually ends in oil storage on the boat; the Whale-specific charging and transfer behavior stays in the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/oil-whale-guide">Oil Whale Guide</Link>.

Gas routes become important around oxygen systems and larger construction chains. If Project Eden is the reason the boat suddenly needs more support infrastructure, I keep its build requirements and progression in the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/project-eden-guide">Project Eden Guide</Link> and use wall outlets here only to keep the supporting network understandable.

A route is in good shape when I can stand in front of an outlet and answer three questions without tracing every hose: what feeds it, where it sends the resource, and which machine or tank depends on it.

When one of those answers is unclear, I fix the routing before expanding storage.


<h2 id="grid-scan">
  Grid Scan is most useful when the cables look right
</h2>

Some failures are obvious: a disconnected cable is lying on the deck or a socket shows blocked output. Grid Scan earns its place when everything looks connected and the machine still refuses to behave.

I use it after moving batteries or generation, after converting a direct cable route into wall outlets, and when power seems to disappear into a section of the boat with no obvious cause. It is also useful before removing an old backup line; confirming that the new network is live first is faster than reconnecting the old system after the machine dies.

Grid Scan does not replace a clean layout. If I need it every time I want to understand one simple machine, the underlying network is still too complicated.

<h2 id="power-fuel-troubleshooting">
  Match the fix to what the network is doing
</h2>

**The wall outlet will not connect**

Give its output side more physical space first. Then check orientation, network type, and distance. A socket that fits on the wall is not necessarily placed where a cable or hose can leave it.

**The outlet connects, but the machine has no power**

Follow the route back to actual generation or charged storage. Confirm that the new outlet is part of the same electrical network, then use Grid Scan if the physical connections all appear intact.

**The cable runs out even though the target looks close**

Treat the wall or floor between the two points as part of the route. Moving the outlet closer usually recovers more range than adding several extenders.

**A Bulk Battery refuses to use most of its 20 kWh capacity**

Check the route mode. In a mixed Medium / Bulk bank, Equalize can hold the large battery near the smaller batteries' level. Charge the Bulk Battery with Fill before deciding the battery itself is broken.

**A 10,000L Bulk Fuel Tank appears stuck near the smaller-tank level**

Give the large tank an explicit fill route. Mixed-container Equalize behavior can stop a bulk container from taking advantage of its full capacity.

**Fuel exists, but the consumer is not receiving it**

Verify the resource type and direction at both ends. Petrol, diesel, and oil sharing the same part of the boat makes a wrong connection surprisingly easy to miss.

**Everything worked before the rebuild**

Reconnect the last known-good route and change one section at a time. This is exactly why I keep the old connection alive until its replacement has passed a real test.

<h2 id="when-to-stop-rebuilding">
  Stop once the boat is easy to diagnose
</h2>

I consider a routing rebuild finished when a failure no longer requires detective work.

The machine has one obvious local connection. The source can be identified without crawling through the ship. Large storage is actually reaching its rated capacity instead of being constrained by a mixed-bank mode. Fuel types are visually separated enough that I know what each tank is doing, and there is still room to walk through the deck and reach the parts I may need to change later.

Beyond that point, cleaner routing becomes decoration.

The boat will keep changing as Early Access adds larger batteries, tanks, facilities, and new objectives. Leaving some physical and electrical room for the next system has been more useful to me than trying to make the current layout permanent.
