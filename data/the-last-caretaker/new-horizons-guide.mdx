<h2 id="start-with-the-boat">
  Start New Horizons by checking the boat
</h2>

New Horizons does not give me a reason to throw away a working save and rebuild from zero. A stable returning boat is an advantage because most of the new routes immediately ask for some combination of power, storage, fuel, free deck space, and materials.

I spend a few minutes testing what I already have before touching the new markers.

I run the machines I normally depend on, look at whether the battery bank is actually recovering, check how much room is left in storage, and make sure I can still walk through the parts of the deck where I connect equipment.

There are three outcomes.

**The boat loses power while sitting still.**  
I deal with the power problem before committing to Project Eden, the Oil Whale, or a long underwater run. Adding another machine to a grid that is already declining only makes the failure harder to trace. The <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/power-fuel-wall-outlets">Power, Fuel & Wall Outlets Guide</Link> owns the actual routing and troubleshooting.

**Power is stable, but the deck is packed.**  
I clear one working area and enough storage for the next objective. I do not rebuild the entire boat just because New Horizons adds better organization tools.

**The boat already works.**  
I leave it alone and pick a route.

That last case matters. Wall outlets and cleaner layouts are useful, but a functioning ugly boat is still functioning. Rebuilding before every new expedition can consume more time than the expedition itself.

<GuideCallout title="Returning-save rule">
A New Horizons upgrade is worth doing when it removes a problem you are already experiencing. If power, storage, and movement around the deck are fine, spend the next trip on progression instead.
</GuideCallout>


<h2 id="project-eden">
  Choose Project Eden when you are ready for a construction route
</h2>

Project Eden is the route I pick when the boat is stable and I want a larger build objective rather than another scavenging stop.

The first important destination is **Pishon**.

I make the first Pishon visit with free inventory and deck space rather than trying to predict every material in advance. Once the build site shows what it needs, I have a real shopping list instead of a boat loaded with parts I may not use yet.

That also gives me a clean decision point.

If I can support the build without tearing apart the rest of the ship, I continue. If the project exposes a power, storage, or material shortage, I return prepared instead of stretching one trip until the boat becomes the problem.

<h2 id="oil-whale">
  Take the Oil Whale route when oil has become the bottleneck
</h2>

The Oil Whale is not the route I choose merely because it is new.

It makes sense when oil supply is becoming an actual constraint and I am ready to dedicate power and storage to solving it. If I already have enough oil for the work in front of me, Project Jonah can wait.

This distinction saves a lot of unnecessary setup.

A Whale trip is more involved than stopping beside a normal source, filling a container, and leaving. I want spare storage, a healthy electrical system, and room to work around the boat before I commit to it.

Once those conditions are met, the Whale becomes a useful long-term option rather than another device competing for power.

<h2 id="theta-9">
  Save Theta 9 for a properly prepared expedition
</h2>

Research Outpost Theta 9 is the route I choose when I want to spend a trip on exploration rather than construction.

It is also one of the places where I care more about what I am carrying than how much empty build space is left on the boat.

Before leaving, I want comfortable underwater movement, weapons I trust, enough ammunition to avoid turning the return trip into a retreat, and free inventory for whatever comes out of the station.

If one of those is missing, Theta 9 is easy to postpone. Nothing is gained by reaching a deep station and immediately discovering that I prepared for a normal surface ruin.

There is also a real progression blocker inside the station. If I reach the authorization stop, I treat that as a route problem rather than repeatedly testing the same locked access.

The authorization path, station entry, enemy rooms, and deeper route stay in the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/research-outpost-theta-9-guide">Research Outpost Theta 9 Guide</Link>.

<h2 id="wall-outlets">
  Rebuild around wall outlets only when the old routing is costing time
</h2>

Wall outlets are easiest to appreciate on an old boat.

By the time New Horizons starts adding more machines and destinations, an early layout can have cables crossing walkways, connectors hidden behind storage, and lines that made sense twenty hours ago but no longer have an obvious purpose.

That does not mean every returning save needs an immediate refit.

I move wall-outlet work forward when I am doing any of these repeatedly:

* following cables by hand to find the wrong connection;
* disconnecting working equipment just to reach another port;
* adding temporary lines across the deck for every new machine;
* losing track of which battery, tank, or system is feeding a route.

If none of that is happening, I keep playing.

A cosmetic rebuild can wait until I have a functional reason to open the deck up. Once routing itself becomes the bottleneck, the cleanup pays for the time it takes.

<h2 id="samples-memories">
  Collect Samples and Memories on trips you were already going to make
</h2>

Samples and Holo Memories change how I explore, but not where I sail first.

The efficient way to handle them is to attach them to another objective.

If I am already inside a station, cave, ruin, or abandoned habitat, I slow down before leaving and scan the areas that look deliberately preserved or historically important. If I am halfway across the map preparing Project Eden, I do not abandon the build because I remembered a collectible on the opposite side of the world.

That makes the extra exploration almost free.

It also avoids the trap of turning New Horizons into a checklist before the larger systems are even running.

**Samples** are worth collecting when I encounter them because they give exploration a longer tail. I leave completion cleanup for a later pass.

**Holo Memories and cultural discoveries** get the same treatment. I scan them while the route already has me in the area, especially because Memories have value beyond being simple decorations.

**Music cartridges** are pure opportunity pickups for me. I take them when I see them; I never prepare a whole expedition around one while a real progression objective is waiting.

<GuideCallout title="When I turn around">
I backtrack for a missed collectible when I am already close or when I have finished the major objective in that region. During a material-heavy or underwater run, the main job gets finished first.
</GuideCallout>

<h2 id="water-jet">
  Buy better underwater movement before the trip that actually needs it
</h2>

The Water Jet tools are another New Horizons upgrade that makes more sense when tied to a destination.

For short dives around the boat, I can live without turning underwater mobility into the next major purchase.

A long approach changes the calculation.

The standard Water Jet helps with movement, while the stronger Tide Ripper / Twin Jet option is the one I want before committing to repeated deep-water travel. Faster corrections matter more when the boat is far away, visibility is poor, or I am leaving a hostile station with a full inventory.

That makes Theta 9 an obvious point where the upgrade starts paying for itself.

Buying it earlier is not wrong. I simply get more value by spending the materials when I can name the trip that needs it.

<h2 id="what-can-wait">
  Leave low-pressure New Horizons work for the return trip
</h2>

The update becomes easier to manage once every new feature stops feeling urgent.

I usually postpone these until they solve a current problem:

* a complete boat rebuild when the existing grid still works;
* collectible-only trips while a major route is unfinished;
* extra underwater upgrades before I have a long dive planned;
* deeper Project Jonah work when oil supply is already comfortable;
* expanding Project Eden beyond the part I can currently support;
* unrelated exploration after I have already filled the boat with materials for another objective.

The useful question is not “have I done every Update 5 activity?”

It is **“what is stopping the next trip?”**

If the answer is power, I fix power.

If the answer is construction, I go to Pishon.

If oil is limiting the next build, the Whale route becomes valuable.

If the boat is ready and I want a contained expedition, Theta 9 makes sense.

Once I started choosing New Horizons routes that way, the update stopped feeling like a pile of new markers and started behaving like several tools I could pull forward only when I needed them.
