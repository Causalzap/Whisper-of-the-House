<h2 id="where-to-go-next">
  What should you do next in The Last Caretaker?
</h2>

The Last Caretaker becomes much easier to navigate once I stop treating every marker, signal, update feature, and unfinished mystery as equally urgent.

There are really three situations.

If I am on a **new save**, progression comes first. I stabilize the boat, learn the basic electrical and crafting systems, reach Lazarus, recover what is needed to grow the first human, and keep following that human's journey until Exodus. The <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/walkthrough">complete progression walkthrough</Link> is the clean route when I do not yet know which major location should come next.

If I already have **Lazarus and the core boat working**, the useful question changes from “where does the story go?” to “which system is actually blocking me?” A human that will not match a profession is a Human Growth problem. A finished human waiting for space is an Exodus problem. A boat full of cables that I can no longer trace is an infrastructure problem.

A **returning save** is different again. Update 5 and 5.5 can expose several new routes at once, but opening all of them does not make the save progress faster. Pishon, Project Jonah, Theta 9, MOSES, Aaron Voss, Transposium Security, and later Council work can all compete for the same inventory, power, fuel, and attention.

The state already visible on screen is usually a better guide than the update number.

<GuideCallout title="The rule I use when several routes are open">
Finish the blocker that is already giving you a concrete objective before starting another expensive system. A working boat with one completed route is more useful than three half-built destinations and an inventory full of parts meant for different jobs.
</GuideCallout>

<h2 id="core-progression">
  Follow one progression line until the first human reaches space
</h2>

The early game has a natural backbone:

**boat basics → Lazarus → Seed Vault → Human Growth → Exodus**

That is the route I protect from side-content drift.

The opening hours teach systems that keep returning later: power has direction, cables have practical limits, batteries can be drained from the wrong place, the Recycler turns junk into useful material, and the Fabricator converts that material into the parts the boat actually needs. Those lessons matter because later stations are not separate from the survival layer; they are larger versions of it.

Once Lazarus becomes the active destination, the game starts shifting away from simple survival and toward rebuilding humanity. Seed recovery, growth infrastructure, fresh water, food, and the first viable human belong to that transition.

Profession tuning does not need to overwhelm the first story run. Later, when Council roles matter, the system becomes much more deliberate: physical requirements, Memories, mental traits, Growth Prediction, and profession percentage all become resources worth planning. The <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/human-growth-professions-guide">Human Growth & Professions Guide</Link> is where I work through those decisions instead of repeating them during every journey.

The clearest sign that Lazarus has handed the story off is **Time to Let Go Soon**.

Until that launch path is understood, I do not spend much time trying to perfect later Council production or deep Update 5 exploration. Opening the route to space gives the rest of the human system somewhere to go.

<h2 id="returning-new-horizons">
  Returning for New Horizons? Pick the route by what your save can support
</h2>

A developed save can make Update 5 look deceptively easy. The map is already open, storage is full, Lazarus exists, and the boat may have enough old infrastructure to reach almost anything.

That does not mean every new objective is ready.

The <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/new-horizons-guide">New Horizons Guide</Link> is the broad return route when I have not yet decided which Update 5 branch deserves the next trip. Once the blocker is obvious, I leave the update overview behind and work inside the corresponding system.

**Pishon / First Roots**

Pishon is worth scouting as soon as the route exposes it, but a real Project Eden build becomes a construction commitment. If the first frame is asking for materials my boat cannot comfortably supply, I use the visit to learn the site and return prepared. The detailed frame placement, Stainless Steel planning, Farming Platform, fresh water, power, soil, seeds, and First Roots failure states stay in the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/project-eden-guide">Project Eden Guide</Link>.

**Project Jonah / Oil Whale**

The Oil Whale becomes attractive when oil supply has turned into a real constraint and the save is mature enough to support the route. I do not treat it as mandatory just because it exists. If ordinary refinery supply still covers what I am building, adding another power-hungry logistics loop can make the boat harder to manage without solving a current problem.

When the Whale itself becomes the objective, the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/oil-whale-guide">Oil Whale Guide</Link> owns the Project Jonah route, Whale handling, transfer setup, storage planning, and the question that matters most: when the Whale is actually better than the alternatives.

**Research Outpost Theta 9**

Theta 9 is a different kind of commitment. It is a deep-station route with underwater travel, enclosed combat, terminals, locked states, and enough backtracking that poor preparation follows me all the way inside.

If the old survivor signal is active and I have movement, weapons, free inventory, and enough reserve for the return, I go. If the station has already stopped me at Ventilation 3.3 or authorization, the <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/research-outpost-theta-9-guide">Theta 9 Guide</Link> owns the route from entry through that blocker.

These three paths do not need to be completed in one expedition. In practice, each becomes much smoother when the boat is carrying parts for one job instead of parts for all three.

<h2 id="update-55">
  Update 5.5 adds routes that look familiar until they change underneath you
</h2>

Deck Drop is especially good at reusing places or systems I thought I already understood.

That makes an old save dangerous in a different way: familiarity can hide the new progression.

**MOSES Field Reserve Program** is the clearest example. The new storage equipment can become visible before it is buildable, which makes the Skills screen look like the answer. It is not. The progression goes back through **Central Warehouse Alpha**, where the old warehouse now contains a field-logistics route tied to the storage prototypes.

<h2 id="system-blockers">
  When the quest is fine but the boat or human system is not
</h2>

Some of the hardest-looking progression failures are not quest failures at all.

A station can be perfectly reachable while the boat supporting it is a mess. A human pod can be correctly powered while the profession build is wrong. A rocket can have resources nearby while the route between storage and the destination is broken.

I treat those as system blockers.

The boat is ready for another major project when I can answer basic questions without tracing half the deck:

- Which source is powering this machine?
- Which battery is supposed to receive charge?
- Where does this fuel line actually end?
- Is this tank being filled, drained, or equalized?
- Can I disconnect one machine without breaking three unrelated systems?
- Do I still have enough free deck space to add another large component?

If those answers are unclear, another Update 5 structure usually makes the problem worse. The <Link href="/the-last-caretaker/power-fuel-wall-outlets">Power, Fuel & Wall Outlets Guide</Link> is where I clean up that layer before blaming the next quest.

Human production has the same pattern. A pod that technically works can still create the wrong specialist if I am feeding it without tracking the profession first. Once Council work matters, food, Memories, traits, and lifespan have to serve a target rather than simply make numbers larger.

The common rule is simple: **fix the reusable system before repeating the journey that depends on it**.

One clean electrical network improves Pishon, Oil Whale, Exodus, and every later base. One reliable profession workflow improves every Council human that follows.

<h2 id="when-to-turn-back">
  Turn back when the trip has stopped producing new state
</h2>

I use “new state” as the test for whether an expedition is still worth continuing.

A useful trip changes something:

- a quest advances;
- a door gains authorization;
- a design becomes available;
- a new marker appears;
- a frame accepts the next construction stage;
- a profession reaches its target;
- a rocket begins accepting resources;
- a shortcut opens;
- a location becomes understandable enough to repeat safely.

When none of those things is happening, I check whether I am simply repeating the same failed action.

That is usually the moment to return to the boat.

Inventory is another strong signal. Heavy salvage is valuable after the route is solved; before that, it can turn navigation and combat into the harder problem. The same applies to ammunition, energy, and vehicle condition. A deep station with no ammo left or a damaged gyro carrying expensive cargo is no longer the trip I originally planned.

The boat is not just the place I came from. It is the reset point where I can change the problem.

I unload the material that is slowing me down, recharge, repair, fabricate the exact missing part, and return with a reason.

That rhythm is much faster than forcing every destination into a one-visit clear.

<h2 id="unfinished-searches">
  Some searches are worth keeping alive without letting them control the run
</h2>

Early Access creates a useful middle category between “solved” and “ignore it.”

Several current leads are interesting enough to remember but not strong enough to reorganize the whole save around.

**Salvage Gyro Lost Blueprints** is one of them until the entire route is mapped cleanly on the same standard as the larger journey guides. The quest itself is meaningful and points toward multiple old service platforms, so I progress it when those locations fit the trip. I would not invent exact paths for any platform I have not reproduced.

**Eden Cradle** clearly matters in later human and Council logistics, but I treat it as a destination tied to the objective currently sending me there rather than assuming every late-game human system begins at the Cradle.

**Rooms 994, 995, and 997** belong to the wider 99X breadcrumb trail. A number attached to a location is worth recording; it is not automatically a playable room with a finished access chain.

**Committee chains** are another area where I avoid overbuilding ahead of the game. Filling a role that advances an active objective makes sense. Spending rare Memories on every possible profession merely because an empty Council slot exists is harder to justify while some chains remain incomplete.

**Achievements** are similar. I would rather wait for a stable, verifiable list than turn uncertain Early Access completion conditions into a collection of thin pages.

Samples, isolated Memories, one-off tools, and small location mysteries can stay useful without becoming separate projects. When one of them starts producing a repeatable route, a clear failure state, or a strong reason to leave the current objective, that is when it deserves more attention.

<h2 id="early-access-rule">
  In Early Access, trust the state you can reproduce
</h2>

The Last Caretaker rewards experimentation, but Early Access also makes old assumptions expensive.

Capacities change. New routes get inserted into old locations. A room can appear in Security before a normal entrance exists. A system that worked on an older save can behave differently after an update.

So I give the current game state more weight than memory.

If a quest says the next destination is North Deep Intake, I go there rather than following an unrelated theory. If a door says authorization is missing, I look for authorization rather than forcing power into the same door again. If a Skills entry says MOSES is required, I treat the quest as the gate instead of assuming the item is bugged.

The same rule protects me from outdated numbers.

A screenshot or older save can prove that a system existed and show how it behaved at that moment. Current build values are the ones I use for planning. When the game changes a capacity or transfer behavior, I update the decision around the new value rather than trying to preserve the old setup.

That does not make older routes useless. Continuous early-game runs are still excellent for understanding progression order; newer specialized runs are better for current mechanics.

The practical rule while playing is much simpler:

**follow the newest state the game can actually reproduce on your save.**

If a route gives me a real objective and a real state change, it is progression. If I am cycling the same camera, door, or container without anything changing, I stop treating repetition as discovery.

<h2 id="pick-one-job">
  Give the next trip one job
</h2>

Before leaving the boat, I try to describe the trip in one sentence.

“Get the first human to Exodus.”

“Scout Pishon and learn what the frame needs.”

“Recover the MOSES storage design.”

“Get Theta authorization.”

“Find out what Aaron Voss leads to.”

“Fix the ship network before I add another system.”

If I need three sentences connected by “and while I'm there,” the loadout is probably already drifting.

One-job trips make every decision easier. I know what deserves inventory space, what can stay on the floor, when a shortcut matters, when the current objective has actually finished, and when another pile of salvage is no longer worth the weight.

That is the way I keep The Last Caretaker readable even after the save becomes complicated.

The world can stay messy. The next objective does not have to be.
