<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Use the world belonging to the player whose **story and Sanctum you want to advance**. Your character can bring its level, equipment, inventory, spells, and personal progression into another world, but that world's story state, recruited allies, and buildings stay with that world. Dimraeth supports up to **8 players**, so joining a friend does not require abandoning the character you have already built.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="character-vs-world">
  Your character travels between worlds, but the world itself keeps its own progress
</h2>

The most important multiplayer distinction in Dimraeth is not host versus guest.

It is **character progress versus world progress**.

I can build a character, gain levels, collect equipment, fill the inventory, learn spells, and develop that character's personal progression. Those parts travel with the hero when the character moves into another world.

The world keeps a different set of records.

Story progress belongs to that world. Recruited allies belong to it. Buildings and the Sanctum setup belong to it as well.

That means joining a friend's save with an established character does not turn their new world into a copy of mine. I can arrive stronger, but I am entering **their version of Mirkea and its current progression state**.

For a regular group, that makes the first decision surprisingly important:

**Whose world are we actually progressing?**

The player who wants to keep the shared story, recruited NPCs, and developed home should be the one whose world the group uses most consistently.

<h2 id="hosting">
  I decide the main host before the group starts investing in the world
</h2>

The server setup allows a player to host a multiplayer world and invite other characters into it.

The setup I tested exposes multiplayer options directly and supports as many as **eight players in the same world**.

For a casual night, host choice barely matters.

For a group planning to play through the campaign together, it matters immediately because the buildings and story accomplishments are being attached to one world rather than copied into every player's personal save.

If four of us spend an evening recruiting allies and expanding one Sanctum, the practical result is a more developed **shared host world**. The guests still leave with the progress that belongs to their characters, but they should not expect their untouched personal worlds to inherit that same settlement.

So I pick a regular host before spending heavily on buildings.

That prevents the awkward situation where the group has several half-developed worlds and nobody has the complete shared base.

<h2 id="what-carries-over">
  Level, gear, inventory, and spells are safe investments for the character
</h2>

Anything that belongs directly to the hero is much easier to invest in when I know I will play both solo and with friends.

The important carry-over group is:

- character level;
- equipment;
- inventory;
- learned spells;
- personal character progression.

This is why I am comfortable spending time refining a build even if tonight's session is happening in somebody else's world.

The character is still mine.

A strong Shadow caster can arrive with the spells and equipment already assembled. A Brawler does not need to rebuild its Bleed setup every time it joins a different group's save.

The same logic makes material collection more interesting. If something is sitting in the character's inventory rather than existing as a world structure or recruited NPC, it remains part of the travelling hero.

For the deeper stat, spell, and equipment decisions, I keep that work in the <Link href="/dimraeth/builds">Dimraeth builds guide</Link> instead of duplicating it here.

<h2 id="what-stays-in-world">
  Story progress, recruited allies, and buildings belong to the world
</h2>

This is the side of multiplayer that can catch a group off guard.

Three major systems are world-bound:

**story progress, recruited allies, and buildings.**

If I clear a major story gate in my friend's world, that advances the state of that world.

If the group recruits an NPC there, that NPC becomes part of that world's progression.

If we build facilities and expand the Sanctum there, those structures do not become a universal upgrade attached to every character who helped pay for them.

This creates a useful division of responsibility.

My character build is portable.  
Our campaign home is not.

For players who alternate hosts constantly, that means repeating some world development by choice. Nothing is wrong with doing that, but it is much easier when the group realizes it before investing materials.

I keep one world as the group's main campaign world and use other saves when someone specifically wants solo progression or a separate playthrough.

<h2 id="joining-new-world">
  A powerful character can enter an early world without advancing its story automatically
</h2>

Because character and world progress are separated, an experienced character can enter a world that is much earlier in the campaign.

That creates an unusual situation: my hero can be far stronger than the story stage I am joining.

The old character does not need the host's world to be equally advanced before entering it.

For helping a new friend, that is useful. I can bring a character whose equipment and spell setup are already functional instead of making another level-one hero solely because the host is starting fresh.

The tradeoff is difficulty balance.

If I bring a developed character into the opening hours, normal encounters can stop representing what the new player would experience alone. When I actually want to learn the game together rather than carry somebody through it, making a fresh character can produce a better first run even though it is not technically required.

That is a group choice rather than a progression requirement.

<h2 id="host-offline">
  The multiplayer session depends on the host being online
</h2>

In the Early Access setup I tested, multiplayer was host-run rather than a permanently available shared world.

When the host leaves, the hosted session closes for the other players as well.

That makes the choice of main host practical, not just organizational.

If the person who owns the group's primary world is only available one night a week, the rest of the group cannot treat that world as a permanently running community server.

I plan around that in one of two ways.

A group that mostly plays together keeps one main world and accepts that it is available when the host is present.

Players with very different schedules keep developing their own worlds too, then bring the same characters together when the group is online.

Because character progression travels, the second option does not mean throwing away all the solo work done between sessions.

That becomes more noticeable when the fight lasts long enough for resource management to matter.

<h2 id="deeds-coop">
  Higher Deeds are where adding players starts solving a real problem
</h2>

The campaign can be played solo.

The first place I seriously start thinking about bringing more players is the repeatable boss loop.

The jump from an early Deed to the next difficulty can turn a familiar boss into a much less forgiving fight. More health stretches the encounter, more damage punishes a missed defense, and dangerous attacks get more opportunities to land.

At that point, another player is not just faster DPS.

They can split pressure, contribute a different status setup, cover healing or control, and prevent one mistake from ending the entire attempt.

I still do not use party size as a substitute for fixing a weak build. If Deed 1 is already exhausting every consumable, adding people only hides the underlying problem.

The actual Deed levels, rewards, efficient difficulty ceiling, and material-farming decisions belong in the <Link href="/dimraeth/deeds-bounties">Deeds and Bounties guide</Link>.

<h2 id="solo-and-coop">
  I use solo worlds for personal progress and one stable world for the group campaign
</h2>

The cleanest setup I have found is to stop treating solo and co-op as competing save choices.

They solve different things.

My character can continue gaining levels, finding equipment, learning spells, and improving between group sessions. Then that same hero can enter the group's world when everyone is online.

Meanwhile, the shared world's story, recruited allies, and buildings stay coherent because the group is not changing the main host every session.

That gives me two progress tracks without needing two completely separate characters:

**personal character progression wherever I play, and shared world progression when the group is together.**

Once that distinction clicks, Dimraeth's multiplayer structure becomes much easier to manage.
