<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Use the **Deed Board** when you can name what the next repeat is supposed to earn: a boss material, spellbook component, useful drop, or a harder test for a build that has already outgrown the previous tier. The board currently shows **Deed Levels 1–10**. Start at a level you can clear cleanly, spend what the run gives you, then move up. If the next tier suddenly burns through healing or turns one mistake into a death, that is a reason to upgrade, change the build, or bring other players—not a reason to keep raising the number.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="deed-board">
  The Deed Board solves the problem of bosses that are worth killing twice
</h2>

The value of Deeds became obvious before I cared about pushing difficulty.

Some of the materials I wanted came from bosses I had already killed. The Shadow build needed boss components for **Corruption spellbooks**, while another character wanted more **Alpha Teeth** after the first Alpha Wolf kill. Waiting around the original encounter location was not a useful farming plan.

After the Goblin King stage, the game begins surfacing **New Deeds available at the Deed Board**. From there, selected bosses and larger encounters can be repeated through the board instead of being treated as one-time story fights.

This does not turn every quest into repeatable endgame content. What matters are the encounters whose rewards still connect to character progression: boss materials, equipment, XP, skill progression, or a higher difficulty version worth testing.

That distinction is why I rarely open the board and simply choose the largest number available.

<h2 id="choose-deed">
  Pick the reward first, then pick the Deed
</h2>

The fastest way I found to waste time with Bounties was to farm before deciding what the character needed.

A better order is:

**next upgrade → required material or reward → matching boss → highest Deed I can clear efficiently.**

The **Alpha Wolf Bounty** is a good example. I returned to it because I specifically wanted more **Alpha Teeth**, not because the wolf happened to be an available repeat.

The board also makes it clear that different entries are not identical reward packages. On the screens I reached, an **Alpha Wolf Bounty** showed **143 XP**, while **Corrupted Beast Returns** showed **400 XP**. Both also exposed a **Potential Loot** section, but their displayed rewards were different.

That matters more than it first appears. “Higher Deed” and “better target” are not the same decision. A boss with the material I need can be more valuable than a harder encounter whose reward list does nothing for the current build.

The broader question of which attributes, spells, and equipment are worth funding stays in the <Link href="/dimraeth/builds">builds guide</Link>. Here, the important part is making the repeat serve that decision instead of replacing it.

Higher levels make familiar encounters tougher through more health, more damage, and greater pressure. What surprised me was how quickly that scaling became noticeable.

The first real wall in my run appeared between **Deed 1 and Deed 2**. This was not a subtle efficiency loss where the boss merely took a little longer. The same fight suddenly made missed defenses much more expensive, and one bad sequence could turn into a near-death or a full combo kill. That was enough to start thinking about another gear stage and whether bringing **two or three players** would make the repeat more practical.

I would not turn that observation into a rule that every player needs a group for Deed 2. A stronger solo character can push further, and different bosses create different problems. What Deed 2 demonstrated was how misleading the number can be: moving from 1 to 2 can feel much larger than a one-step increase suggests.

The previous clear gives me a better signal.

If I finish with healing left, the boss dies in a reasonable number of openings, and one mistake does not derail the whole run, I test the next level. A clear that consumes almost everything is technically a win, but it is poor evidence that the next tier will be a good farming route.

<h2 id="efficient-farming">
  Potion use tells me when a higher Deed has stopped paying for itself
</h2>

Repeatable content changes the meaning of a victory. During the story, beating a difficult boss once is enough. When I am farming the same encounter for materials, the cost of that clear matters almost as much as the reward.

I watch three things during a Deed:

- how much healing disappears before the boss is near death;
- whether a single failed dodge, parry, or positioning mistake can become a lethal combo;
- how long the run takes compared with the material or reward I came for.

A boss can still be killable after crossing the efficient ceiling. That does not make it a good farm.

If the previous Deed takes five controlled minutes and the next one becomes a long sequence of resets, potion refills, and narrow recoveries, I would rather use the lower tier until the character changes. One completed upgrade can alter that calculation immediately, which is why I spend useful rewards before starting another repeat instead of stacking several runs with the same weaker setup.

The board becomes much more productive when each clear feeds the next one:

**kill → spend → test → decide.**

Not:

**kill → kill → kill → eventually check whether any of it helped.**

<h2 id="boss-rewards">
  A Deed can move the build forward even when the ideal gear piece never appears
</h2>

Equipment is only one reason to repeat these fights.

One boss clear awarded a **skill point**, which immediately made the run useful even though the equipment drop was not the only thing I cared about. Boss materials can unlock spellbooks or other upgrades, XP continues moving the character, and the reward UI itself can differ noticeably from one bounty to another.

That broader reward mix is useful when deciding whether a repeat is worth doing again. If the boss supplies a material that finishes a spell and also gives meaningful character progression, I can tolerate several imperfect gear drops. If I am only chasing one hypothetical item with no other useful return, the same time investment is much harder to justify.

This is also why I avoid hoarding boss materials without a plan. Once the component I needed finally drops, I leave the loop long enough to craft or upgrade. The next fight should meet the new version of the character.

That confirms the drop itself. It does **not** confirm a rule such as:

**Deed 2+ always drops purple gear.**

The same caution applies to Legendary or Mythic hunting. Higher difficulty is a sensible place to look for stronger rewards, but I would not push a Deed that the character can barely finish solely because I expect a particular color at the end.

A repeatable system encourages pattern hunting, and one good drop is especially easy to overread. Until the same reward behavior holds across enough clears, I treat rarity as upside rather than a promise.

<h2 id="solo-coop">
  Co-op becomes attractive when the mistake budget disappears
</h2>

The normal campaign can be completed solo, and I do not use player count as a substitute for judging the build.

Higher Deeds are different because scaling magnifies the cost of every mistake. More boss health keeps dangerous mechanics active for longer, increased damage reduces the number of recoverable errors, and depleted healing makes a fight that was comfortable at the previous level much harder to stabilize.

That is the point where another player starts providing more than extra damage. A group can shorten the encounter, divide pressure, and make support tools such as healing or barriers more valuable.

My own Deed 1→2 jump was severe enough that **two or three players** immediately sounded reasonable. It was still an option, not a mechanical requirement.

For a solo character, I keep climbing while the fight remains controlled. Once repeated attempts are being decided by one mistake rather than by the overall quality of the build, I either strengthen the character or stop treating solo progression as something that has to be proved at that moment.

If the group is also deciding whose character and world are carrying the progression, the <Link href="/dimraeth/co-op-progression">co-op progression guide</Link> covers that separation.

<h2 id="after-forest-king">
  Deeds matter more once there is no main quest competing for the same time
</h2>

The Deed Board becomes available while there is still story left in Wildwood, which creates an awkward early choice: a repeat boss can be useful without being the best thing to do next.

Before the Forest King, I interrupt the campaign for Deeds when they solve something specific—a required boss material, a build that clearly needs more power, or an upgrade close enough to justify the detour. Otherwise, I continue the main route.

After **Aelwynor the Wildroot Father** falls, that opportunity cost largely disappears. The current linear Early Access story has reached its endpoint, while repeat bosses, side quests, NPC progression, Sanctum development, and build completion remain open.

If **Woldroot the Path Guardian** is missing after the Forest King fight, I would not keep searching the old story route and waiting for him to respawn. Players are getting him back by taking **Return to the Forest Throne** from the Deed Board, teleporting to **The Sealed Approach**, and then walking north through the route again. Reports place Woldroot just north of the **Lost Guardian** teleport point while the Forest King repeat is active.

I would walk the route rather than teleporting straight to Lost Guardian or the Forest Throne. Players who skipped ahead have reported the repeat getting stuck, so starting from The Sealed Approach is the safer approach until that behavior changes.

That is when Deeds become a progression loop rather than an interruption. Several completion goals also run through Deeds, Bounties, and mini-bosses, so if I am cleaning up the game rather than pushing the story, I use the <Link href="/dimraeth/achievements">Dimraeth achievements guide</Link> to track the exact requirements.

I choose the next improvement first, find the encounter that can supply it, clear the highest Deed that is still efficient, spend the result, and only then decide whether the character has earned a harder version of the same fight.
