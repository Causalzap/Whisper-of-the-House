My answer to **the best character in War in the North: Legacy Edition** depends
less on which hero can produce the biggest damage number and more on **what keeps
ending my fights**.

For a first solo playthrough, I start with **Eradan**.

He gives me the easiest combination of ranged pressure, melee flexibility,
Evasion, and exploration utility without forcing me into one narrow playstyle.

If my real problem is survival, revives, or recovering from bad positioning, I
prefer **Andriel**.

If I already understand the encounters and want to stay close to enemies, I use
**Farin**.

The important Legacy Edition difference is that I no longer treat this as a
permanent choice.

I choose one hero as my **primary build**, learn that combat loop properly, and
switch when another character gives me a better answer to the next section.

<h2 id="legacy-character-choice">
  Legacy Edition changes how I choose a character
</h2>

The old question is:

**Which character should I commit my entire save to?**

I do not approach Legacy Edition that way.

Eradan, Farin, and Andriel still have different skill trees, weapons, resources,
and exploration abilities, so the choice matters.

What changed for me is the consequence of that choice.

I am choosing **who receives my attention first**, not locking myself into one
hero for the entire campaign.

If Eradan is comfortably handling the opening chapters, I keep building Eradan.

If a later fight exposes a survival problem that Andriel solves more cleanly, I
have a reason to use Andriel.

If a dense melee section keeps forcing me into close range anyway, Farin can
turn that same situation into an advantage.

What I do **not** do is change characters every few minutes simply because the
option exists.

A build only becomes useful once I understand why I spent the points and what
the character is supposed to do when a fight starts going wrong.

<h2 id="switching-characters">
  How I use character switching in Legacy Edition
</h2>

I treat character switching as a **problem-solving tool**, not as a replacement
for making a build.

My default habit is to stay on my main character until I can explain why another
hero is better for the next problem.

Examples:

- I stay on **Eradan** when clean ranged lanes and Evasion are giving me control.
- I move toward **Andriel** when recovery and protection are the reason I am
  failing.
- I prefer **Farin** when the fight keeps collapsing into close-range pressure
  and I want to meet that pressure directly.

I also avoid rebuilding my entire plan around one bad encounter.

If Eradan struggles in one room, that does not automatically mean Eradan was a
bad first choice.

I first ask whether I used the character correctly.

Only when the same weakness keeps appearing do I seriously change the build or
make another hero my main answer.

I am deliberately not listing one universal controller shortcut here because I
want the actual input shown for the platform I am playing rather than copying an
unverified button combination.

<h2 id="character-comparison">
  Eradan vs Farin vs Andriel
</h2>

The opening numbers already tell me what direction each hero is being pushed
toward.

| Character | Opening profile | What I use them for first | Main weakness I plan around |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Eradan** | 100 Health, 70 Power, 12 Dexterity | Bow pressure, Evasion, flexible melee | Ammunition and weaker sustain than Andriel |
| **Farin** | 105 Health, 73 Power, 12 Strength | Melee pressure, War-cry, heavy weapons | Bad positioning is punished quickly |
| **Andriel** | 95 Health, 125 Power, 12 Will | Sanctuary, magic, staff pressure, support | Lowest starting Health |

I do not treat those starting values as an endgame tier list.

They tell me what each character is naturally prepared to do **before**
equipment and skill investment start changing the picture.

Eradan starts with the strongest Dexterity direction.

Farin starts with the strongest Strength direction and slightly more Health.

Andriel gives up some starting Health for a much larger **125 Power** pool.

That is why I never try to distribute points across all three characters using
one identical formula.

<h2 id="eradan-build">
  Eradan build — my best first-playthrough setup
</h2>

Eradan is the character I recommend when someone wants one answer and does not
yet know which playstyle they will enjoy.

His three important directions are:

- **Evasion** for stealth, escape, safer revives, and reveal attacks;
- **Heavy Shot** for bow damage, charged shots, stuns, and ranged pressure;
- **Ranger Strike** for heavier melee attacks, dual wielding, and two-handed
  weapon support.

I do not try to finish all three branches at once.

For a first run, I start with one question:

**Am I failing because enemies keep reaching me, or because I cannot remove
them fast enough from range?**

If enemies repeatedly collapse onto me, I value **Evasion** first.

If I already control distance well but enemies are surviving too long, I move
farther into **Heavy Shot**.

Ranger Strike becomes more important when I have deliberately decided that my
Eradan is becoming a melee-heavy or dual-wield character.

### My Eradan stat rule

For a bow-first Eradan, **Dexterity** is the offensive direction I care about
most.

I still refuse to dump every point into Dexterity while ignoring the reason I
am dying.

If ordinary mistakes are taking too much of my health bar, I add Stamina.

If I repeatedly exhaust Power before the dangerous part of a fight, I add Will.

If I have consciously moved toward a melee-heavy setup, that is when Strength
becomes a real priority.

My next point answers a problem I have actually experienced.

It does not follow a level-by-level spreadsheet that assumes my equipment and
playstyle are identical to somebody else's.

<h3 id="eradan-evasion">
  Why Evasion is better than it first looks
</h3>

Evasion is not only an offensive stealth tool.

Its real value becomes obvious when a fight starts going badly.

If a companion goes down while enemies are standing around the body, Evasion
can give me a much safer revive window.

If ranged enemies have pinned me into a bad position, Evasion gives me a way to
reset the engagement instead of trading another potion for another mistake.

That is why I value it more as difficulty rises.

More damage makes a good fight shorter.

Evasion can rescue a bad fight.

<h2 id="andriel-build">
  Andriel build — my safest solo and sustain setup
</h2>

Andriel is the character I move toward when my problem is no longer finding
enough damage.

It is **staying alive long enough to use that damage**.

Her three major directions are:

- **Sanctuary** for protection, healing, and defensive support;
- **Word of Command** for area control and energy attacks;
- **Empowered Staff** for stronger ranged staff pressure.

If I am building Andriel for a difficult solo section, Sanctuary is the branch
I refuse to neglect.

There are many encounters where one more offensive skill does not fix the
actual failure.

The real problem is:

- getting hit repeatedly while trying to revive;
- crossing open ground under ranged pressure;
- or using health potions faster than I can replace them.

Sanctuary changes that equation.

That is why I do not judge Sanctuary by damage-per-second.

Its value is the damage, interruption, and resource loss that never happens

<h3 id="andriel-stats">
  My Andriel stat rule
</h3>

Andriel starts with only **95 Health**, but **125 Power**.

That tells me immediately what not to do.

I do not build her as if she were Farin with a Staff.

If I am getting killed before Sanctuary can stabilize the encounter, I add
Stamina.

If a spell-heavy setup genuinely empties the Power pool too early, I add Will.

Only after the character is surviving the fights I am actually playing do I
become more aggressive with offensive investment.

Her ranged game also solves a different resource problem from Eradan and Farin.

She does not rely on arrows or crossbow bolts for her primary ranged pressure.

The tradeoff is that poor skill use wastes Power instead.

That means I can keep attacking from range, but I still need discipline about
which target deserves an expensive ability.

<h3 id="andriel-beyond-sanctuary">
  When I move beyond Sanctuary
</h3>

Once survival stops being the problem, I choose between two directions.

If groups are overwhelming me, I move farther toward **Word of Command**.

If I already control the battlefield but want stronger consistent ranged
pressure, I invest more heavily in **Empowered Staff**.

That is where Andriel stops feeling like "the healer" and becomes a full combat
character whose build simply has better recovery tools than the other two.

<h2 id="farin-build">
  Farin build — my aggressive melee choice
</h2>

Farin is the easiest character to misunderstand.

He starts with **105 Health** and **12 Strength**, which naturally encourages
melee.

That can create one bad habit:

**walk forward → swing harder → assume the build is working.**

It works until enemies stop dying before they can punish the commitment.

His main directions are:

- **War-cry** for control, buffs, and surviving pressure;
- **Sweeping Attack** for groups and crowded melee fights;
- **Crushing Blow** for heavier single-target pressure and offensive support.

For my first serious Farin build, I do not ignore War-cry just because Crushing
Blow produces more satisfying individual hits.

War-cry is part of what lets an aggressive melee character remain aggressive
when enemies stop folding immediately.

<h3 id="farin-stats">
  My Farin stat rule
</h3>

If melee is my plan, **Strength** is the obvious offensive direction.

I pair that with Stamina instead of pretending every level needs another damage
increase.

Once ordinary enemies are already dying comfortably, surviving the extra hit
that lets me finish a combo can matter more than another small offensive gain.

Will becomes more attractive only when Power is actually limiting how often I
can use the skills around which I built the character.

Dexterity matters more when I have deliberately turned the crossbow into a
meaningful part of the setup.

I do not raise every stat evenly just because equipment occasionally shows
different requirements.

A balanced-looking stat screen is not the same thing as a build with a clear
purpose.

<h3 id="farin-vs-eradan">
  When I choose Farin over Eradan
</h3>

I prefer Farin when the fight keeps forcing me into close range anyway.

If a room is full of clustered melee enemies, maintaining perfect bow distance
may cost more effort than it saves.

That is where Sweeping Attack and War-cry make more sense to me.

If the encounter gives me clean ranged lanes, dangerous explosive targets, or
situations where I need Evasion to reset pressure, Eradan becomes more
attractive again.

Farin is strongest for me when I am **deliberately choosing pressure**.

He feels much worse when I am simply panic-attacking because I ran out of
space.

<h2 id="gear-for-build">
  How I choose gear for each build
</h2>

I match the weapon to the build first.

Only after that do I decide whether the item deserves a long-term investment.

For Eradan, I care about whether the weapon supports the ranged or melee
direction I actually chose.

For Andriel, I want a Staff that supports the way I am using her Power and
ranged pressure.

For Farin, I want the weapon to support the close-range role rather than simply
having the largest number in my inventory.

<h2 id="when-i-respec">
  When I respec instead of forcing a bad build
</h2>

I do not respec because one boss gave me trouble.

One difficult fight can expose an execution problem rather than a build
problem.

I consider a respec when I can see a repeated structural mistake.

Examples:

- I invested deeply in bow skills but now spend most fights in melee;
- I spread points across several branches without unlocking the tools I
  actually use;
- I built Andriel around damage while repeatedly dying before Sanctuary can
  stabilize the fight;
- or I built Farin for pure offense and now spend every difficult encounter
  recovering from overcommitment.

That is when resetting points has value.

A respec should make the character's purpose clearer.

If I cannot explain what the new build is meant to solve, I am not ready to
spend money fixing the old one yet.

<h2 id="best-character-solo">
  Best character for solo play
</h2>

For a normal first solo run, I still give **Eradan** the edge.

His strength is not that he wins every individual category.

It is that I rarely reach a fight where his entire kit stops making sense.

I can attack from range.

I can move into melee.

I can use Evasion to reset pressure.

I can push toward dual wielding or heavier melee later if I want.

And Ranger signs give me another reason to check the edges of a level while I
am learning the campaign.

For a player who does not yet know what they will enjoy, that flexibility is
more valuable than beginning with the most specialized hero.

<h2 id="best-character-survival">
  Best character when survival is the real problem
</h2>

When I am repeatedly burning potions, failing revives, or losing fights after
one bad sequence, I stop pretending more damage is automatically the answer.

That is when I prefer **Andriel**.

Sanctuary gives me a tool that changes the shape of a bad fight rather than only
making a good fight finish faster.

If the build already survives comfortably and enemies are now taking too long
to kill, I can move more aggressively into her damage directions.

But I solve the failure I actually have first.

<h2 id="best-character-melee">
  Best character for deliberate melee pressure
</h2>

When I know the encounters and want to stay close, I prefer **Farin**.

The important word is **deliberate**.

I do not choose Farin because I ran out of room and ended up in melee.

I choose him because I expect the next section to reward War-cry, group pressure,
and heavier close-range attacks.

That difference matters.

A character forced into close range is surviving a problem.

Farin built for close range is using the situation he wanted.

<h2 id="best-character-hard-difficulty">
  Best character when harder difficulty exposes mistakes
</h2>

As difficulty rises, I stop asking which hero has the highest theoretical
damage.

I ask what repeatedly causes the failure.

If I cannot revive safely or ranged enemies keep controlling the fight, I lean
toward **Eradan and Evasion**.

If sustained damage and recovery are the problem, I lean toward **Andriel and
Sanctuary**.

If survival is already controlled and I need to remove priority targets faster
at close range, **Farin** becomes more attractive.

This is also where build discipline matters more.

A character with a clear role feels completely different from the same
character carrying scattered skill points and equipment that does not support
that role.

<h2 id="which-character-i-pick">
  Which character I pick for each problem
</h2>

If somebody asks me for one starting answer, I say **Eradan**.

If somebody tells me they keep dying, I ask them to look at **Andriel**.

If somebody already understands the fights and wants a more aggressive
close-range character, I point them toward **Farin**.

Those are different questions.

That is why I do not like a simple tier list here.

A tier list tries to turn three different tools into one ranking.

I get better results by choosing the tool that matches the failure in front of
me.

<h2 id="best-build-rule">
  My final build rule
</h2>

I do not choose a build because somebody called it overpowered.

I choose it because it fixes the part of the run that keeps failing.

If I am constantly exposed, I add control or defense.

If I cannot finish priority targets, I add damage.

If I run out of Power before the dangerous part of a fight, I fix Power
economy.

If my points are spread across abilities I barely use, I stop investing
randomly and consider a respec.

If the build is already doing its job, I stop changing it simply because
another skill or item looks interesting.

And if another character genuinely solves the next section better, Legacy
Edition gives me far less reason to stubbornly remain on the wrong hero.

That is why my first recommendation remains **Eradan**, but my actual answer to
"Who is the best character?" is:

**Eradan for flexibility, Andriel for safety, and Farin for deliberate melee
pressure.**

The best build is the one that makes me stop dying to the same problem twice.
