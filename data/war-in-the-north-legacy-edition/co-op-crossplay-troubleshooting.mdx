**War in the North: Legacy Edition** is still built around a three-character
Fellowship, but I decide how I want to connect before I commit a campaign save.

The Legacy Edition supports **online co-op** and **shared/split-screen co-op**
with up to **two friends**, and the Steam version also supports **Remote Play
Together**.

The one thing I do **not** assume is cross-platform multiplayer.

As of **August 13, 2026**, crossplay is not advertised as a Legacy Edition
feature, so I do not plan a PC-to-PlayStation, PC-to-Xbox, or
console-to-console campaign around it unless official support is added or the
game exposes a cross-platform invite system.

My other launch rule is even more important:

**Before starting a multi-hour campaign with friends, I run a short save test.**

I want to know exactly what the host keeps, what the joining players keep, and
whether everyone's equipment and progression survive leaving and rejoining
before I trust a long multiplayer session.

<h2 id="which-coop-mode">
  Which co-op mode I use
</h2>

There are three different things players can mean when they ask whether War in
the North has co-op.

I separate them immediately because troubleshooting the wrong mode wastes more
time than almost anything else.

| What I want to do | Mode I use | What matters most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Friends on separate systems on the same supported platform | **Online co-op** | Stable host, matching game version, save testing |
| Two or three people using the same system | **Split-screen co-op** | Controllers and local player detection |
| Steam friends playing a local-style session over the internet | **Remote Play Together** | Steam Overlay, Remote Play input and host connection |

I do not mix these modes mentally.

If I am trying to join an actual online lobby, changing Remote Play settings is
not my first fix.

If I am using Remote Play Together, searching for a normal online lobby is not
my first fix either.

That sounds obvious, but it prevents a lot of pointless troubleshooting.

<h2 id="online-coop">
  How I start an online co-op campaign
</h2>

For a normal online run, I decide who will be the long-term host **before**
anyone invests heavily in the campaign.

I prefer the player with:

- the most reliable connection;
- the most predictable schedule;
- and the person most likely to keep playing the same campaign through Normal,
  Heroic, and eventually Legendary.

That choice matters more in an RPG than it does in a ten-minute co-op match.

War in the North carries equipment, character progression, skills, money, and
campaign state across a run. I therefore do not casually change hosts every
session and assume everything will behave exactly as before.

### My first-session test

I do not make the first co-op session a three-hour commitment.

I start the campaign, play until everyone has had time to:

1. gain or change something visible on their character;
2. collect at least a few pieces of loot;
3. reach a normal progression point;
4. leave the session cleanly;
5. restart the game;
6. and rejoin the same host.

Then I check the joining characters.

I want to see their expected equipment and progression before continuing.

If anything is missing, that is the moment to investigate it.

Not after another six chapters.

<h2 id="split-screen">
  How I approach split-screen co-op
</h2>

Legacy Edition is different from the old PC release in one important respect:
**split-screen is now an advertised feature.**

That matters because old search results are full of answers telling PC players
that War in the North only supported online multiplayer.

Those answers describe the older PC version.

They are not the rule I use for Legacy Edition.

For the current release, I start with the native split-screen option instead of
installing old third-party workarounds designed to force local multiplayer into
the original PC version.

I also connect and verify the controllers **before** starting the real campaign.

If player two or player three is not appearing correctly, I solve the input
problem at the beginning rather than hoping another controller will magically
register halfway through the first level.

### I test all local players before investing in the save

I make each local player move, attack, open their interface, and confirm they
control the expected hero.

Only then do I continue.

That is especially useful in War in the North because the three heroes are not
cosmetic skins.

Eradan, Farin, and Andriel have different skills, weapons, resources, and
battlefield jobs.

A controller assigned to the wrong player is immediately more disruptive here
than it would be in a game where every character plays the same way.

<h2 id="remote-play-together">
  Steam Remote Play Together is not the same as online co-op
</h2>

On Steam, Legacy Edition also exposes **Remote Play Together**.

I treat that as a separate solution.

Normal online co-op connects separate game sessions.

Remote Play Together takes the local multiplayer experience on the host
machine and sends the session to the other players through Steam.

That distinction determines what I troubleshoot.

When Remote Play Together is my intended mode, I start the game on the host
machine first and use the Steam Remote Play Together flow to bring the other
players in.

If a remote player can see the game but cannot control a character, I look at
input assignment before blaming the campaign.

My order is:

**Steam connection → Remote Play invitation → controller recognition → player
assignment → campaign.**

I do not reverse that order and spend twenty minutes reloading a save when the
real problem is that Steam never delivered the second controller correctly.

<h2 id="crossplay">
  Does War in the North Legacy Edition have crossplay?
</h2>

This is the question where I stay conservative.

I can confirm that Legacy Edition exists across modern platforms and that the
game supports co-op.

I **cannot** treat that as proof that those platforms share the same multiplayer
network.

They are two separate claims.

Right now I do not see cross-platform play advertised as a Legacy Edition
feature, so my working rule is:

**Do not buy a second copy on another platform specifically to play together
until crossplay is explicitly confirmed.**

If I am on Steam and my friend is also on Steam, I use the Steam multiplayer
options.

If we are on the same console family, I use that platform's normal multiplayer
flow.

If one of us is on PC and the other is on PlayStation, Xbox, or Switch, I do
not assume the lobby will connect simply because both versions support online
co-op.

<h2 id="multiplayer-save">
  The multiplayer save check I do before a long run
</h2>

The host/client relationship is the part of launch-week multiplayer I take most
seriously.

War in the North is loot-heavy.

A player can spend an hour improving a weapon, changing armor, gaining levels,
or moving through quests and reasonably expect those changes to still exist
after reconnecting.

That is why I deliberately create a **known test state**.

For example, I note:

- my current level;
- one easily recognizable equipped weapon;
- one armor piece;
- and roughly where the campaign is.

Then I exit properly and reconnect.

I do not use rare Mithril investment as the test.

I do not use a valuable socketed weapon as the test.

I use ordinary progression I am willing to repeat.

If the client returns with the expected state, I am much happier committing to
a proper campaign.

If the state is wrong, I stop there.

The worst response to an uncertain save system is to keep playing because
"maybe it will fix itself later."

<h2 id="host-client-progress">
  When the host is farther ahead than another player
</h2>

I also avoid casually moving the main campaign forward when part of the group is
missing.

This is not only about story spoilers.

It creates ambiguity about which campaign state everyone expects when they
rejoin.

For a regular three-player group, I keep one save as the **group campaign**.

If I want to play alone between sessions, I prefer doing it on a separate run
rather than advancing the shared campaign far beyond the other players.

That gives me one simple expectation every time the group reconnects:

**this save belongs to this Fellowship.**

It is much easier to diagnose a genuine multiplayer problem when I have not
already mixed solo progression, different hosts, and different chapter states
into the same test.

<h2 id="revive-companion">
  Revives are the first co-op habit I want the group to learn
</h2>

War in the North does not make a downed companion irrelevant.

When one member drops, the other players have to decide whether the fight is
safe enough to finish first or whether somebody needs to create space and
revive immediately.

The game explicitly warns me to **rescue a companion quickly**.

In co-op, I do not have all three players sprint toward the downed character.

That usually concentrates the entire enemy group on the same location.

I would rather have:

**one player revive → one player pull pressure → one player control or kill the
most dangerous attacker.**

With only two human players, I make the same decision around what the
AI-controlled companion is already doing.

The important part is that somebody deliberately owns the revive instead of
everybody assuming someone else will do it.

Fellowship should not be treated as three independent damage dealers.

Her sphere can create a recovery point while the fight is still active.

<h2 id="gather-your-party">
  "Gather your party" is a real progression rule
</h2>

There are also points where one player cannot simply disappear down the next
corridor while everyone else continues looting.

The game can stop progression until the Fellowship gathers at the transition.

This changes how I handle side rooms.

If one player has found a chest or character-specific interaction, the rest of
the group should not stand on the next transition trying to drag the campaign
forward.

I use a simple callout:

**"Ready to move?"**

Only after the group is finished do I trigger the next section.

That habit becomes even more important when somebody is checking Ranger caches,
Dwarven breakable walls, Andriel interactions, Scroll drops, or equipment on
the ground.

The few seconds saved by rushing a checkpoint are not worth forcing another
player to wonder whether they just lost a collectible or piece of loot.

<h2 id="cannot-join">
  What I check when a friend cannot join
</h2>

When joining fails, I change one thing at a time.

I do not simultaneously change the host, reinstall the game, alter controller
settings, switch Remote Play on, and load a different save.

That makes it impossible to learn what was actually wrong.

My order is:

1. confirm everyone is using **Legacy Edition**, not an old War in the North
   installation;
2. confirm the players trying normal online co-op are on the platform setup I
   actually intend to use;
3. restart the multiplayer session;
4. restart the game if the session still cannot be joined;
5. on Steam, verify Steam itself is online and the Overlay is available;
6. distinguish a normal online invite from a **Remote Play Together** invite;
7. test with a fresh or low-value session before risking the main campaign
   save;
8. if one player consistently fails while the others join normally, isolate
   that player's connection or input setup next.

The important part is the order.

If normal online co-op is broken, enabling Remote Play does not prove the online
lobby works.

It only gives me a different way to play.

<h2 id="remote-play-input">
  Remote Play works but the second player cannot control anyone
</h2>

When the picture arrives but the player cannot control a hero, I treat that as
an input problem first.

I check that the remote controller is actually being passed to the host
session.

Then I check that the game recognizes another local player.

Only after those two steps do I start changing the campaign or reinstalling
anything.

I also avoid using two players who both expect to control the same keyboard and
mouse input.

For a local-style multiplayer session, distinct controller input is much easier
to reason about.

If the controller disappears after reconnecting, I rebuild the Remote Play
session before I touch the save.

<h2 id="disconnects">
  What I do after a multiplayer disconnect
</h2>

A disconnect is where I become conservative.

I do not immediately continue playing for another hour with whatever version of
the party happens to remain.

I first decide whether this is still the intended group campaign.

If the missing player is coming straight back, I stop at the next safe
opportunity and let them rejoin.

After the session, I verify progression again.

If the same player repeatedly disconnects, I do a shorter test session instead
of repeatedly pushing the main campaign forward and hoping the connection
improves.

My priority is:

**protect the save first, diagnose the connection second, recover lost playtime
never.**

<h2 id="coop-character-roles">
  I divide jobs by character instead of making everyone chase damage
</h2>

The three heroes already push the party toward different jobs.

I use that.

**Eradan** is the player I want checking ranged threats, using Evasion when a
revive becomes dangerous, and noticing Ranger-specific exploration
opportunities.

**Farin** is the player I am comfortable letting hold close pressure, interrupt
crowded groups, and create breathing room for the others.

**Andriel** gives the party the clearest recovery and protection tools while
still contributing ranged and area damage.

That does not mean each player must obey a rigid MMO-style tank/healer/DPS
assignment.

War in the North is more flexible than that.

It means that when a fight goes wrong, I already know which tool I expect each
character to reach for.

Three people dealing damage independently can win easy fights.

A Fellowship that knows who creates space, who revives, and where to recover is
much more reliable when the difficulty increases.

<h2 id="coop-troubleshooting-rule">
  My final co-op troubleshooting rule
</h2>

I solve War in the North multiplayer problems from the outside inward.

First I identify the mode:

**online, split-screen, or Remote Play Together.**

Then I verify the connection and player inputs.

Then I test the save.

Only after those are stable do I spend time diagnosing character progression
or campaign-specific problems.

And I never assume crossplay simply because two platforms both advertise
online co-op.

For a long campaign, the best setup is not the cleverest setup.

It is the one where the same host can reliably bring the Fellowship back
together, every player keeps the progression they expect, and nobody discovers
six hours later that the first ten-minute save test would have exposed the
problem.
