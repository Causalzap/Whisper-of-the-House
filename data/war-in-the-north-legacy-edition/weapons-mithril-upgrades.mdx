I stopped judging weapons in **War in the North: Legacy Edition** by one green
damage number.

That works early, when most drops are temporary.

It becomes a bad habit once I start seeing **sockets, jewels, species damage,
unique quest weapons, and Mithril upgrades**.

The clearest example I have is a max-level Staff comparison.

One Staff had two sockets and **2.3x Damage vs. Orc**. The other had no sockets
and could take more Mithril upgrades.

Against an Orc, the specialized Staff landed a **25,671-damage headshot**.

The comparison Staff without the Orc bonus produced about **11,000**.

That is the point where I stopped asking:

**Which weapon has the most upgrades?**

and started asking:

**Which weapon is actually better for the enemies I am about to fight?**

<h2 id="weapon-or-armor">
  Weapon or armor — what I choose at Angmir
</h2>

When I bring the excess Mithril to Angmir, he gives me a direct choice.

He can use it to strengthen my **armor**, or he can enhance a **weapon**.

I do **not** automatically choose the weapon.

I look at what is currently making me fail.

If enemies take too long to kill and that extra time is creating the danger,
weapon damage makes sense.

If my damage already feels comfortable but one bad exchange empties most of my
health bar, strengthening armor solves the more important problem.

That sounds simple, but it prevents one of the easiest upgrade mistakes:

**using an expensive offensive upgrade to solve a defensive failure.**

<h2 id="what-makes-weapon-worth-upgrading">
  What makes a weapon worth investing in
</h2>

Before I spend Mithril, I look at the weapon itself.

The four things I care about most are:

**the actual damage profile, sockets, inherent bonuses, and how long I expect
to keep using it.**

A slightly higher base number is not enough.

If another weapon gives me useful sockets or a large bonus against the enemies
I am repeatedly fighting, I may keep the lower-looking weapon instead.

The late-game Staff comparison makes that decision much easier to see.

<h2 id="sockets-vs-mithril">
  Two sockets can be worth losing two Mithril upgrade slots
</h2>

This is the mechanic that changed how I evaluate late-game loot.

In the Staff test I use here, the weapon with **no sockets** can take roughly
**8 Mithril upgrades**.

The Staff with **2 sockets** already has those socket positions counting toward
the fully upgraded weapon, leaving roughly **6 Mithril upgrades**.

At first, I thought the answer was obvious.

Eight Mithril upgrades sounds better than six.

But that ignores what I put into the sockets.

If the jewels give me a large bonus against the enemy type I am actually
fighting, two specialized sockets can be worth considerably more than two extra
generic upgrades.

That means I do not reject a good weapon because it has fewer available Mithril
positions.

Sometimes the sockets are exactly what make the weapon worth keeping.

After switching to the fully Mithril-upgraded Staff without extra Orc damage,
the same comparison produced headshots of about **11,000**.

That is not a small optimization.

The matching specialized setup was producing a little over **2.3 times** the
observed damage of the comparison hit.

I do not assume every species bonus, weapon, character, or enemy will reproduce
that exact ratio.

What I take from the test is the decision rule:

> **A large matching enemy bonus can outweigh additional generic upgrades.**

That is much more useful than memorizing one Staff as "the best weapon."

<h2 id="species-damage">
  Which species bonuses I actually keep
</h2>

The useful weapon bonuses are not limited to Orcs.

I also watch for extra damage against:

- **Undead**
- **Spiders**
- **Trolls**
- **Orcs**

By the time I am playing harder content, I am comfortable keeping more than one
weapon if the alternatives solve different stages.

For an Orc-heavy route, I equip the Orc weapon.

For an undead-heavy section, I can switch to an Undead specialist.

The same logic applies to spiders.

I am more conservative with Troll-specific investment.

If my general weapon already kills Trolls fast enough, I do not pour Mithril
into a second weapon simply because it says `Damage vs. Troll`.

That is an important distinction.

**Finding a specialist weapon does not automatically mean I should upgrade it.**

It has to solve a problem my existing weapon does not already solve.

<h2 id="orc-damage-first">
  Why Orc damage is my first specialization
</h2>

If I only have the resources to build one specialist weapon, I start with
**Orc damage**.

Orcs appear repeatedly across the campaign, so I get more opportunities to use
that investment than I would from a narrowly specialized weapon that spends
most of the game sitting in my inventory.

That does not mean I sell every good Undead or Spider weapon.

It means Orc damage gets the first serious upgrade budget.

Once I already have one excellent general weapon and one strong Orc weapon, I
can afford to become more selective about the next specialist.

<h2 id="when-spend-mithril">
  When I spend Mithril on a weapon
</h2>

I want at least two of these three things before I commit:

**1. The weapon already has a reason to keep it.**

That can be strong sockets, a useful inherent perk, or a species bonus I expect
to use repeatedly.

**2. The weapon fits the character I am actually building.**

A great Staff does nothing for my Eradan investment.

A powerful melee weapon does not become valuable simply because Farin could
theoretically use it if I am not spending time on that setup.

**3. More damage fixes the problem I am having now.**

If a dangerous enemy is surviving too long and turning every encounter into an
attrition fight, improving the correct weapon makes sense.

If enemies already die quickly, I save the investment.

I do not upgrade gear because I reached an upgrade NPC.

I upgrade because I know what I expect the new gear to fix.

<h2 id="when-upgrade-armor">
  When I choose armor instead
</h2>

There are runs where weapon damage is not the bottleneck.

I can kill ordinary enemies quickly and still lose against bosses or large
waves because I cannot survive the punishment long enough.

That is when Angmir's armor option becomes much more attractive.

I ask myself:

**Am I losing because this enemy is alive too long, or because I cannot survive
the time it is already alive?**

Those sound similar, but they lead to different upgrades.

If I am dying after two or three bad hits while damage output already feels
fine, more offense may only make a winning fight slightly faster.

Better survivability can turn a losing fight into a controllable one.

I especially become more willing to choose armor when the weapon in my hand is
already strong enough that I expect to replace it only for an exceptional
drop.

<h2 id="when-save-mithril">
  When I do not spend Mithril at all
</h2>

There are three situations where I walk away.

### The weapon is only a temporary upgrade

If I equipped it ten minutes ago because it happened to have a higher number,
that is not enough evidence that it deserves long-term investment.

I keep using it.

I keep looking at drops.

I keep the Mithril.

### The weapon has no identity

A weapon with no useful sockets, no meaningful specialist bonus, and no other
reason to survive the next round of loot is not something I try to rescue with
expensive upgrades.

Mithril makes a good weapon better.

I do not use it to convince myself that an average weapon was secretly good.

### My current setup already solves the fight

This is where I save the most resources.

If Trolls already die quickly with my normal Staff, I do not build a
Troll-specific Staff simply because one dropped.

If my Orc weapon already destroys Orcs, a tiny upgrade in the same role is not
urgent.

I would rather wait for a genuinely different piece of gear.

The clearest example is **Work of Westernesse**.

from Westernesse and says there is enough material to create a **unique
weapon**.

He also explains that a work like this can reflect the nature of the smith who

What I **do not** do is turn that statement into a fake universal ranking.

I do not say:

**"The Elven version is always best."**

or:

**"Always use a Dwarf."**

The information I have confirms that the smith can matter.

It does not justify pretending one outcome is automatically superior for every
build.

So I evaluate the finished weapon the same way I evaluate everything else:

**Does its actual stat package fit what I am building?**

The component locations and quest hand-in remain part of the Side Quests
guide. This page owns what I do with the weapon once gear decisions begin.

<h2 id="legendary-weapons">
  Legendary is where I become much more selective
</h2>

By the time I am playing Legendary, I no longer want one weapon that is merely
"pretty good at everything."

High-end drops are strong enough that I can start building a small collection
around specific enemy types.

That is where the switching strategy becomes practical:

**general weapon for mixed areas**

**Orc weapon for Orc-heavy stages**

**Undead weapon for undead-heavy stages**

**Spider weapon when spiders become the repeated problem**

I still do not create every possible specialist.

The tested Legendary approach does not bother fully upgrading Troll-specific
Staffs when the main Staff already kills Trolls quickly enough.

I like that rule because it stops this from becoming a gear-hoarding exercise.

The point is not:

**own one weapon for every label in the game.**

The point is:

**own another weapon when switching to it produces a meaningful result.**

<h2 id="vendor-gear">
  When I buy a weapon and when I keep my coins
</h2>

I am much less interested in shop gear once my build has a clear identity.

A shop weapon has to do more than move one number slightly upward.

I buy when it gives me something I am genuinely missing:

- a meaningful damage jump;
- sockets I can use immediately;
- an enemy-specific bonus that fills a real gap;
- or a weapon type my build actually needs.

I walk away when the upgrade is only marginal and I already have a weapon with
better long-term properties.

This becomes especially important once I have begun investing in Mithril.

Replacing a properly built weapon with every modestly higher drop defeats the
reason I invested in it in the first place.

At the same time, I do not become emotionally attached to an upgraded weapon.

If something substantially better drops, I use the better item.

Past Mithril spending is not a reason to keep making future fights harder.

<h2 id="my-upgrade-order">
  The upgrade order I actually use
</h2>

When several pieces of equipment are asking for resources at once, I use this
order.

**First, I establish one reliable general-purpose weapon.**

I want something that can finish ordinary fights without needing a perfect
enemy matchup.

**Second, I look for an Orc specialist.**

The campaign gives that bonus enough opportunities to matter.

**Third, I fix survivability if it is now the limiting factor.**

That can mean choosing armor instead of forcing another weapon upgrade.

**Fourth, I add another specialist only when a region gives me a reason.**

Undead and Spider setups are useful when those enemy types become concentrated
enough to justify the inventory slot and investment.

**Finally, I stop.**

I do not upgrade every technically good item.

The goal is not to produce the most expensive inventory possible.

It is to enter the next area with a weapon that fits the enemies there, enough
survivability to stay in the fight, and enough unspent resources that a
genuinely exceptional drop still matters.

That is the point where War in the North's gear system makes the most sense to
me:

**base damage tells me whether a weapon is usable; sockets and bonuses tell me
whether it is worth keeping; Mithril tells me whether I am willing to commit to
it.**
