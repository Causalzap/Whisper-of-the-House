<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Use **Parlay** first when the Pacification Quest fits what you are already doing. **Bribe** when immediate pacification or the free unit matters more than the Dust. After pacification, the village must be inside a Territory controlled by one of your **Cities** before you can Assimilate that Minor Faction; a Camp alone is not enough.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

A Minor Faction village can begin as a nuisance beside the capital and end up changing the City's population, army roster, Council, and empire-wide economy.

The important part is separating two decisions that happen at different times.

First, deal with the village itself.

Later, after its Territory belongs to a City, decide whether that Minor Faction is valuable enough to occupy a Protectorate slot.

A good Pacification target is not automatically a good Protectorate, and a faction with an excellent Assimilation bonus may not be worth chasing if only one distant village exists on the map.

<h2 id="village-options">
  Should you Parlay, Bribe, or Attack a Minor Faction village?
</h2>

Most Empires have three ways to handle an unpacified village.

<GuideTable
  caption="The three normal ways to deal with an unpacified Minor Faction village."
  columns={villageChoiceColumns}
  rows={villageChoiceRows}
/>

**Parlay** is usually my first check.

It does not require paying the Bribe cost, and completing the Pacification Quest handles every village of that Minor Faction in the same Territory at once. The quest can also provide rewards that make the detour worthwhile.

I still look at the objective before committing an important army to it. A short task on the same exploration route is easy value. A quest that sends the only useful army across the map while another border is becoming dangerous is a very different trade.

**Bribe** is the fast answer.

It affects the individual village rather than solving every village in the Territory, but pacification happens immediately and one Minor Faction unit joins you. That makes the Dust much easier to justify when the new unit fills a missing battlefield role or the village is producing roaming armies beside land I am about to settle.

**Attack** changes the state of the village completely.

Winning destroys it. The village stops functioning normally and must later be rebuilt by the City controlling that Territory before it can contribute to Assimilation. Starting the attack also fails an active Pacification Quest for that Territory.

So I do not treat Attack as a cheaper Bribe.

If I still want the village as part of a normal Protectorate plan, destroying it adds a rebuilding step that the peaceful routes avoid.

<h2 id="assimilation-requirements">
  What do you need before you can Assimilate a Minor Faction?
</h2>

Pacification alone is not enough.

The pacified village has to be inside a Territory controlled by one of your **Cities**.

A Camp by itself does not satisfy that condition.

This creates a common opening trap: I finish a Pacification Quest, see the village has stopped being hostile, and assume the Protectorate is ready. If the Territory is still only held by a Camp, Assimilation is not available yet.

The normal progression is:

1. Find and pacify the village.
2. Secure the surrounding Territory.
3. Turn that Territory into part of a City.
4. Open the Protectorates screen.
5. Assimilate the Minor Faction if a slot is available.

A destroyed village adds one more step because it has to be rebuilt before it counts.

Pacifying a village also does not protect the surrounding Territory from another Empire. When the Minor Faction is important enough to shape my economy, I budget the Influence needed to secure its land rather than treating the completed quest as the end of the job.

The broader Camp, City, and attachment decisions are covered in the <Link href="/endless-legend-2/beginner-guide">first 30 turns</Link>.

<h2 id="protectorate-benefits">
  What does Assimilating a Minor Faction actually give you?
</h2>

A Protectorate can affect several parts of the Empire at once.

Assimilation unlocks:

- the Minor Faction's empire-wide Assimilation bonus;
- its recruitable military unit;
- its population;
- its Notable and Council trait;
- and stronger scaling from additional pacified villages of the same faction.

That is why I rarely choose one from the Assimilation bonus alone.

A smaller economic bonus can still be the better choice when the population solves a City problem and the signature unit fills a gap in the army. Conversely, an excellent empire bonus is less attractive when its population and unit duplicate things the Empire already does well.

<h2 id="village-scaling">
  Why are several villages of the same Minor Faction so valuable?
</h2>

Many Protectorate effects combine a fixed bonus with another bonus for every pacified village of that faction under the Protectorate.

Foundlings show the pattern clearly.

Their Assimilation effect gives units:

- **+15 Health**
- plus **+4 Health for every pacified Foundlings village**

With one village, that becomes **19 Health**.

With ten villages, the same Protectorate reaches **55 Health**.

This can make the second or third village of an existing Protectorate more valuable than discovering an entirely different Minor Faction.

The map therefore matters as much as the tooltip.

Ametrine Corps with one village provide a useful Industry boost. A large cluster of Ametrine villages turns that same bonus into a much more substantial part of the Empire's production.

When I already know which Protectorate I want, neighboring Territories containing another village of the same faction become more valuable expansion targets.

<h2 id="population">
  How does Minor Faction population work?
</h2>

Assimilation adds population from that Minor Faction to Cities controlling its pacified villages.

Each relevant pacified village contributes population, so taking control of several villages can change the City's workforce immediately after Assimilation.

I use that population to solve a specific economic problem rather than growing it automatically.

Before choosing another Minor Faction population, I check its vocation strengths, its population effect, its Approval consequences, and whether the City actually has an open slot where the new worker can contribute.

That last check matters because a strong population type can still make the City worse when it arrives into an already crowded population economy.

If the additional worker becomes Destitute or pushes the City toward a Shanty, the problem is no longer the Minor Faction itself. The City needs more appropriate vocation capacity or an Approval fix first.

The relationship between population slots, Destitute workers, Shanties, and rebellion is covered in the <Link href="/endless-legend-2/approval">Approval guide</Link>.

<h2 id="which-minor-faction">
  Which Minor Faction should you Assimilate?
</h2>

I start with the Empire's current weakness, then look at how many villages of each candidate can realistically come under my control.

<GuideTable
  caption="Minor Factions that solve common economic or military problems."
  columns={minorFactionPriorityColumns}
  rows={minorFactionPriorityRows}
/>

The table is a starting point rather than a ranking.

Village density can outweigh the flat bonus. A faction with four nearby villages may become more useful than the theoretically perfect one whose only village sits on the other side of another Empire.

The signature unit can overturn the economic comparison too.

Foundlings are a good example because the **Occultist** brings **Blood Mending**, healing itself and adjacent friendly units when it attacks. For an army that otherwise struggles to recover during repeated fights, that unit can matter more than another small economic percentage.

A fast **Charger**, ranged **Scholastic**, defensive **Pantinel**, or another unusual Minor Faction unit should be judged the same way: not by whether its raw Damage is marginally higher, but by whether it gives the current army something it cannot already do efficiently.

<h2 id="protectorate-slots">
  How many Protectorates can you have?
</h2>

A normal Empire starts with **one Protectorate slot**.

Additional capacity comes from technologies and Deeds as the campaign advances.

One important Deed is **Bringer of Peace**:

**be the first Empire to pacify 10 Villages → gain one Protectorate slot.**

Only one Empire can claim that Deed, so I do not count on the slot until it has actually been secured.

The starting restriction makes the first choice important, but it does not mean the first Protectorate has to remain active for the entire game.

The better question is whether the current slot is still producing enough value for the Empire I have now.

<h2 id="assimilation-cost">
  How much does Assimilation cost, and what happens when you switch?
</h2>

The Empire's **first Assimilation costs 0 Influence**.

Every subsequent Assimilation costs **100 more Influence than the previous one**.

That cost continues to matter when Protectorates are switched, because the same Influence is also needed for expansion and other Empire actions.

Removing a Protectorate disables its current Assimilation benefits, including access tied to that relationship.

The Minor Faction population already living in your Cities **remains**.

There is one important limit:

**re-assimilating the same Minor Faction does not award its population again.**

So I can replace an early Protectorate when a stronger one becomes available, but I cannot cycle the same faction in and out to repeatedly collect population.

This is why I compare a switch against the next territorial expense.

Changing Protectorates may improve an empire bonus immediately, but losing a contested region because the last Influence was spent on a marginal upgrade can cost far more.

<h2 id="faction-exceptions">
  Which factions handle Minor Factions differently?
</h2>

Several factions change enough of the normal process that the standard Parlay → City control → Assimilation sequence cannot be copied without adjustment.

**Aspects** are built much more heavily around Protectorates. Their **Cohabitation** trait makes all Minor Faction Assimilations free instead of only the first, and their population collection thresholds are reduced. Their current faction setup also gives them additional Protectorate capacity from the beginning.

**Necrophage** do not use the normal peaceful relationship with Minor Factions. Their campaign expects a much more hostile interaction with villages, so Parlay and ordinary Protectorate planning are not the foundation of their opening.

**Severed Claws** cannot follow the normal Parlay or Bribe path. They destroy Minor Faction Villages for military units and rebuild useful villages for population. Their <Link href="/endless-legend-2/severed-claws">Village and population cycle</Link> changes both what a village is worth and how the single-city Empire grows.

**Last Lords** can turn suitable pacified villages into **Lord's Estates**, tying Minor Factions directly into their Dust and population economy. The <Link href="/endless-legend-2/last-lords">Last Lords guide</Link> covers when those villages are worth building the Empire around.

These exceptions are why a Minor Faction that looks mediocre beside one Empire can become a major expansion target beside another.

<h2 id="decision">
  What should you check before spending a Protectorate slot?
</h2>

By the time Assimilation is available, I want four pieces of information.

First, I check the **empire bonus** and how strongly it scales with the number of villages I can actually control.

Then I look at the **population** and whether it improves a real vocation or City weakness.

Next comes the **unit**. A new battlefield role is much more valuable than a slightly different version of something the army already fields.

Finally, I check the **Influence cost and slot pressure**. A good Protectorate can still be the wrong purchase on the turn when that Influence is needed to secure a contested Territory.

That leaves a much simpler decision than trying to name one universally strongest Minor Faction:

**use the Protectorate that removes the most expensive limitation from the Empire you already have.**
