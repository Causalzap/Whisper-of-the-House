<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
When Approval falls, check the **Approval breakdown and vocation slots before building anything**. If population is Destitute, create the job slots it actually needs and let the Shanty disappear. If every worker already has a job, look for overexpansion, population traits, or other Approval penalties instead. Delay additional population or Cities until the current penalty state is under control.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

Approval problems often begin before the Approval bar becomes alarming.

A City gains another population point, a new Minor Faction population enters a full vocation category, another independent City increases expansion pressure, or a faction effect adds a penalty that looked affordable several turns earlier. The economy can continue growing for a while before the combined result becomes obvious.

That is why I read **Approval, population, and vocation capacity together**. A City with room for more total population can still have no suitable job for the next worker, while a City with perfectly assigned workers can still be unstable because the real problem comes from expansion or a population trait.

<h2 id="approval-effects">
  What does Approval do in ENDLESS Legend 2?
</h2>

Approval can directly change City output.

In one City, **37% Approval** was still showing a neutral state without a production modifier. At **22% Mutinous**, the City showed:

- **-20% Food**
- **-20% Industry**

Those two values are useful examples of what the City screen can show; I do not treat them as universal boundaries for every Approval state.

The important point is the modifier itself.

Once Food falls, the next population point arrives more slowly. Once Industry falls, the building or district needed to stabilize the City also takes longer to finish. Approval has therefore stopped being a background number and started weakening the resources needed for recovery.

When a City suddenly feels much slower than it did several turns earlier, I check the Approval state before assuming the problem is simply weak terrain or insufficient workers.

<h2 id="destitute">
  Why does population become Destitute?
</h2>

Total population capacity and vocation capacity are different limits.

A City may still have room for another person overall while its Citizen, Artisan, Scribe, or another relevant vocation category has no open slot.

When the new population has nowhere to work, it becomes **Destitute**.

That distinction explains why simply increasing the City's nominal population limit does not always solve the problem. I need to know which vocation category is actually full.

The safest time to deal with this is before the final useful slot is filled.

If a City is one population point away from exhausting the jobs it needs, I start preparing more vocation capacity before activating a Food booster, buying population, or adding another population type through Assimilation.

Otherwise growth can create a worker the City cannot use before the next district is ready.

<h2 id="shanty">
  How do Shanties appear, and how do you remove one?
</h2>

Destitute population does not remain harmless indefinitely.

After the City keeps Destitute population for several turns, a **Shanty** can begin appearing automatically.

The sequence shown by the Shanty rules is straightforward:

- population has no suitable vocation slot;
- that population becomes Destitute;
- after several turns, a Shanty can appear;
- the Shanty provides limited economic value and can hurt Approval;
- enough vocation slots remove the Destitute state;
- the Shanty is then removed automatically.

So I do **not** solve a Shanty by trying to demolish the symptom.

I solve the missing jobs.

A Shanty also occupies a real tile in the City. That can interfere with a district location that was being saved for a Works, Laboratory, Communal Habitation, or another adjacency-sensitive building.

For that reason, preventing the Shanty is better than waiting for it to appear and then repairing the population shortage.

<h2 id="communal-habitation">
  When does Communal Habitation fix the problem?
</h2>

For a normal City, **Communal Habitation** can provide the vocation capacity needed to recover from overpopulation.

In the Order City where a Shanty began forming, the relevant Communal Habitation provided:

**+1 population slot per District Level.**

The important part was not merely constructing another district.

A Laboratory completed first, but it did not provide the Citizen slots that were missing. The population remained Destitute because the City had added economic output without adding the kind of capacity that was actually required.

Before choosing the fix, I therefore open the population panel and identify the full vocation category.

If that category needs the slots supplied by Communal Habitation, then the building solves the actual problem. If the shortage is somewhere else, I look for the district, improvement, or faction mechanic that creates the correct capacity instead.

Placement can still matter economically. A Communal Habitation may gain additional yields from neighboring district types, so an emergency capacity fix does not have to become a permanently awkward part of the City.

There is also an important faction exception. <Link href="/endless-legend-2/severed-claws">Severed Claws</Link> use Communal Habitation differently: their version accelerates native population growth rather than functioning as the standard source of population slots. Their City should not copy this recovery method mechanically.

<h2 id="approval-causes">
  What else can lower Approval?
</h2>

Destitute population and Shanties are only one path to low Approval.

The Approval tooltip is the quickest way to separate several different problems that can produce the same red number.

Common sources include:

- population traits or vocation effects;
- Shanties;
- overexpansion;
- faction-specific population mechanics;
- conversion or growth choices;
- and temporary effects that exchange Approval for another benefit.

Minor Faction population can create this kind of tradeoff.

For example, one Foundlings population interaction provided extra Science when another population type shared the same vocation category, while also applying **-2 Approval** under that condition.

The correct response is not automatically to remove a useful population. If the Science is valuable and the City has enough stability to absorb the penalty, the trade can still be worthwhile.

Choosing which population belongs in the Empire is covered in the <Link href="/endless-legend-2/minor-factions">Minor Factions guide</Link>. Once that population is inside the City, its Approval effect has to be judged alongside every other modifier in the Approval breakdown.

<h2 id="overexpansion">
  How is overexpansion different from a population problem?
</h2>

A City can have enough vocation slots, no Destitute population, and no Shanty while Approval is still falling.

That usually means I need to look beyond the City itself.

Expanding beyond what the Empire comfortably supports can add an **overexpansion Approval penalty**. In that situation, constructing another population-capacity district does nothing because unemployment is not the cause.

The response is strategic rather than local.

I stop founding independent Cities simply because land is available, check whether another Territory can be attached instead, and look for effects that directly reduce the expansion pressure.

One Counselor option in my 1.0 campaign provided **-50% Approval penalty from Overexpansion on Cities**. That does not remove overexpansion, but cutting its Approval penalty in half can make an Empire above its normal City Cap much easier to stabilize.

One Council option can directly soften this problem. In my 1.0 campaign, the **Crusader** effect reduced the **Approval Penalty from Overexpansion on Cities by 50%**.

This distinction matters because both problems can appear during rapid growth.

If the tooltip points to Destitute population, create jobs.

If it points to overexpansion, changing the job layout does not remove the penalty.

Reading the breakdown first saves several turns of building the wrong solution.

<h2 id="recovery">
  How do you recover a low-Approval City?
</h2>

I begin with the negative source rather than the largest Approval bonus currently available.

If the City has Destitute population, I pause deliberate growth and create the missing vocation slots. Once those workers have jobs, the Shanty can disappear and its additional pressure goes with it.

If the problem is overexpansion, I stop making the Empire wider until the existing penalty is manageable.

If a population trait or faction effect is responsible, I decide whether its economic benefit is still worth the Approval cost.

Only after that do I look at direct Approval improvements.

Research can matter here. An early Order campaign had to move toward **Creed** because the immediate City options were not enough to stabilize the situation, while later technologies can provide stronger direct Approval support.

The size of the bonus matters less than whether it changes the City's actual state.

A small Approval increase is useful when it moves the City out of a harmful penalty or offsets the remaining negative source. If the City stays deeply Mutinous afterward, spending Industry on a weak partial fix can leave the same Food and Industry penalties running while delaying the stronger solution.

So before queueing an Approval improvement, I check the projected result rather than simply choosing the first building with a positive number.

<h2 id="rebellion">
  What happens if Approval stays low for too long?
</h2>

Low Approval eventually stops being only an economic problem.

A City can trigger:

**“Prolonged low Approval. A Rebel Army has formed and will roam the vicinity, potentially sacking your Districts.”**

At that point the Empire has two problems at once.

The City is still suffering from whatever caused the low Approval, while an army now has to protect its Districts from the rebels. If the main forces are several Territories away, an Approval problem that could have been fixed earlier can suddenly interrupt exploration or a real war.

I therefore treat Mutinous Approval as something to resolve before deliberately adding another source of pressure.

Faction mechanics can change what “pressure” means. <Link href="/endless-legend-2/severed-claws">Severed Claws</Link> gain Approval while at war through Ever Fighting, so ending a war is not automatically their stability fix. <Link href="/endless-legend-2/last-lords">Last Lords</Link> can create the opposite problem by buying population faster than their Cities can comfortably support.

The cause still decides the response.

Once the negative source has been removed, enough vocation capacity exists, and the City is no longer losing critical output to its Approval state, population growth becomes useful again rather than creating another problem to repair.
