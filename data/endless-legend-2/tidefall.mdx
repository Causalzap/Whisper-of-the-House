<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Before Monsoon arrives, get armies off **temporary bridges**. During the storm, collect reachable temporary Curiosities without pulling every useful army away from the frontier. As Tidefall approaches, keep one mobile force near the coastline you care about most; once the sea recedes, scout the new land before spending Influence.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

Monsoon and Tidefall are one continuous map cycle.

The storm temporarily changes movement, scouting, Minor Factions, battle rewards, and several faction mechanics. When the Monsoon ends, Tidefall permanently exposes new land where coastal water used to be.

That means the useful decision often happens **before** the new Territory is visible. An army that finishes the Monsoon deep inland may need several turns to reach a new coastline, while another Empire that was already nearby can inspect and claim the same region immediately.

The weather does not require abandoning the Empire's existing plan. It changes which parts of that plan are safe to continue.

A nearby war can still be worth fighting with the **+20% Battle Spoils** bonus. An exposed army may still need to defend the border rather than chase Curiosities. The important adjustment is recognizing the temporary rules before they turn an ordinary movement or combat decision into a preventable loss.

<h2 id="monsoon-timing">
  How do you know when Monsoon or Tidefall will happen?
</h2>

The turn wheel gives an estimate rather than an exact early countdown.

At low prediction accuracy, the displayed window can be extremely broad; an early forecast can show something like **1–20 turns remaining**. Technologies can improve that prediction later, but I do not plan an entire army route around the optimistic end of a wide range.

Map generation also affects how quickly the coastline starts changing. **Archipelago** maps reach Tidefall more quickly, **Pangaea** delays it, and **Continents** sits between those extremes.

So I use the forecast to decide when positioning becomes important rather than trying to guess the precise turn.

When the remaining window starts to matter, I want at least one useful force close enough to a contested coast that it can react without sitting idle there. Clearing a nearby village, collecting a Curiosity, or guarding an existing Camp keeps the army productive while preserving that position.

<h2 id="during-monsoon">
  What should you prioritize during the Monsoon?
</h2>

Temporary bridges come first because the punishment is immediate.

Army-built crossings do not survive the weather transition, and a unit caught on one when it collapses can be destroyed. Permanent Bridge Districts are different; they are not the temporary crossings being washed away.

Temporary bridges also cannot simply be treated as normal infrastructure during the storm, so I check every army using a river crossing before committing the rest of its Movement.

After bridge safety, Monsoon Curiosities are the main temporary opportunity.

They disappear when the storm ends, and the rewards can include Dust and valuable resources such as **Titanium and Glassteel**. Cheap or fast units are useful here because one army does not need to collect every marker personally.

I spread out only as far as the strategic situation allows. A reward beside safe territory is easy to justify; a distant Curiosity is much less attractive if reaching it leaves a rival border undefended or strands the only army that could contest the next Tidefall.

If no Monsoon Curiosities appear at all, check the world settings as well: their appearance can be disabled when the game is created.

Unpacified Minor Factions add another risk. Their armies become hostile during Monsoon and can fight more aggressively, so a village beside an important road or coastal approach deserves more attention than it did under Clear Skies. The normal Parlay, Bribe, Attack, and Assimilation choices are covered in the <Link href="/endless-legend-2/minor-factions">Minor Factions guide</Link>.

<h2 id="tidefall">
  What does Tidefall actually change?
</h2>

Tidefall begins when the Monsoon ends and the sea recedes.

Tiles that were previously Coastal Water become usable land, permanently changing the shape of Saiadha.

The exposed area can open:

- new Territories;
- Curiosities and anomalies;
- strategic and luxury resources;
- routes between previously separated regions;
- new expansion directions;
- and dangerous sites such as Fortresses.

This can completely change the value of an existing border.

A Camp that looked trapped against the sea may become a gateway into several new Territories. Two Empires that previously had water between them may suddenly be competing for the same corridor. A mediocre coastal region can become much more valuable once its usable land expands.

So I rescout after Tidefall instead of assuming the old map still describes the strategic situation.

<h2 id="prepare-for-tidefall">
  What should you do before Tidefall exposes new land?
</h2>

Positioning matters more than guessing exactly what will appear.

I look first at coastlines beside rival Empires, existing Cities, important Camps, and geographic gaps that could open a new route between large areas of the map. The coast where losing the first move would hurt most gets the nearest mobile army.

That army does not need to carry enough Influence to claim everything immediately. Its first job is information.

I also avoid spending the Empire's last Influence on a marginal Foundation or low-priority Camp when Tidefall is close enough to change the expansion map. Holding every point forever is wasteful, but arriving at newly exposed land with no ability to contest a valuable Territory can be equally expensive.

The useful balance is flexibility: keep enough resources and Movement available to react once the new coastline is actually visible.

<h2 id="after-tidefall">
  What should you claim first after Tidefall?
</h2>

I scout before committing Influence.

The closest exposed Territory is not automatically the best one, and the first attractive resource can distract from a much more important geographic connection nearby.

My first comparison is strategic: does the new land connect two parts of the map, block another Empire, protect an existing City, or open access to a region that was previously unreachable?

After that I care about resources and long-term City value.

A strategically average region with a valuable resource can justify a fast claim. A high-yield region beside an existing City can be better than founding another independent City farther away. When a rival army is already approaching, a good Territory that can be secured now may also be worth more than an ideal one that will be gone next turn.

Hostile sites need a separate decision.

A scout that reveals a Fortress or dangerous creature has already done useful work. I map the surrounding route and identify what is guarding it before turning the scouting mission into a battle. Losing the only nearby mobile unit to a threat I did not need to fight can hand the rest of the Tidefall race to another Empire.

For the <Link href="/endless-legend-2/order-of-zelevas">Order of Zelevas</Link>, newly exposed Territory has additional faction value because territorial control advances their progression. The general Tidefall decision remains the same: reveal the new map first, then decide what is actually worth claiming.

<h2 id="later-tidefalls">
  How should you prepare for later Tidefalls?
</h2>

The first Tidefall teaches what a changing coastline can do. Later cycles are easier to use deliberately because more of the surrounding map has already been explored.

A coastal Camp may eventually sit on an interior route rather than an edge. A defensive army stationed near apparently empty shoreline can become the first force entering a newly opened region. Roads, Burrows, nearby Cities, and army positions all become more valuable when they shorten the trip into the next exposed area.

I therefore stop judging a coastline only by what it connects to today.

What matters is whether that position will still make sense after another section of Coastal Water turns into land.

There is no need to predict every exact tile in advance. The advantage comes from leaving the Empire capable of changing direction when the map does.

<h2 id="faction-effects">
  Which factions change how Monsoon and Tidefall work?
</h2>

Most Empires respond to the same weather cycle, but a few faction mechanics alter what the season means economically.

**Tahuks** have a direct Monsoon interaction through their Observatories. Outside Monsoon, Observatories contribute Science; during Monsoon, that production shifts toward Food. A ridge network that normally supports research therefore changes economic role while the storm is active.

**Sandshapers** interact with both halves of the cycle. Their Storm Cursed traits make Monsoon more painful, while **Lands of the Past** gives them advance information about the shape of land revealed through Tidefall.

That information does not tell me the exact resource value or whether an enemy army is waiting there, so I still scout after the sea recedes. It does let Sandshapers decide earlier which coastline deserves attention.

Their Ruin network, Sand economy, and expansion decisions are covered in the <Link href="/endless-legend-2/sandshapers">Sandshapers guide</Link>.

<h2 id="endless-monsoon">
  What is the Endless Monsoon?
</h2>

Late in a campaign, the game can enter the **Endless Monsoon**, a special endgame version of the weather event that makes Saiadha increasingly dangerous.

I do not treat it like another normal Monsoon where the main objective is waiting for the next Tidefall opportunity.

By that stage, the priority is finishing the campaign while keeping enough military and economic strength to handle the escalating world pressure. Armies positioned only for exploration or speculative coastal expansion become much less valuable if the Empire is already approaching its final victory objectives.

The ordinary Monsoon and Tidefall cycle rewards preparation for a changing map. The Endless Monsoon is the point where that preparation has to give way to surviving and finishing what the Empire has already built toward.
