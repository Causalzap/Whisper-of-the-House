<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Settle the first City **one or two tiles from a useful Buried Ruin**, Raise that Ruin early, and spend the first **Wish of Awakening** where Sand immediately improves Food or district space. Raise distant Ruins for permanent yields and Ancient Wisdom; Restore only the locations whose local bonuses or teleport position justify the extra Dust.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

Sandshapers begin by looking weaker than the terrain around them suggests.

Ordinary fertile land does not support their economy the same way it supports most factions, water can restrict both movement and City development, and much of their long-term power is still buried inside Ruins scattered across Saiadha.

The faction gets stronger by changing that map rather than adapting to it. Ruins become permanent yields, Wishes turn unsuitable terrain into Sand, resource deposits feed Food, and Restored Ruins eventually become a strategic movement network.

<h2 id="first-city">
  Where should Sandshapers build their first City?
</h2>

The first settlement should begin with a **Buried Ruin**, not with a conventional search for the greenest Food tiles.

A distance of roughly **one or two tiles** from the Ruin works well because the City stays close enough to benefit from early Sand conversion without placing the City Hall directly against the site.

Leaving space matters because Raising an early Ruin can create a **free Foundation** at the site. A City Hall or unnecessary district pressed directly against the Ruin can reduce how useful that free development becomes.

I also inspect the surrounding dry land before settling. A strong anomaly is not enough if most of the future City footprint is water or awkward terrain that cannot support the districts the faction needs.

The useful opening site therefore combines three things: a reachable Ruin, enough dry space for the City to grow, and a layout where early Sand can actually become workable Food and district terrain.

General settlement, Camp, and early-expansion decisions are covered in the <Link href="/endless-legend-2/beginner-guide">first 30 turns</Link>. Sandshapers change what counts as a valuable region, but they still need a settlement that can function while the Ruin network develops.

<h2 id="sand-economy">
  How do Sandshapers get Food and choose good Territories?
</h2>

For Sandshapers, terrain value changes once **Sand** and resource production enter the calculation.

Sand supports the faction's Food economy, so **Wish of Awakening** is much more than visual terraforming. Turning the right tiles into Sand can open workable Food around a City that initially looked unable to grow.

The second major Food source is **Mineral Bred**.

Strategic and luxury resource production contributes Food, which makes deposit-heavy Territories unusually valuable. A dry region containing several resources can support the faction better than a conventional river valley with attractive natural Food.

Water deserves the opposite treatment.

**Aquaphobic** prevents normal Bridge use, and water-heavy regions can leave too little dry district space for the City the Sandshapers eventually want. Their Dams can completely dry the river downstream, so water management becomes part of settlement planning rather than a simple movement convenience.

When choosing expansion land, I look first for a combination of:

- Buried or Raised Ruins;
- strategic and luxury deposits;
- enough dry space for districts;
- useful routes between existing settlements;
- and terrain that can be converted into productive Sand without wasting too much Dust.

Resource density becomes particularly powerful later because several systems can stack on the same Territory. Mineral Bred turns extraction into Food, Harvester Extractors improve resource development, and a Restored **Eminence** Ruin can push Extractor value further.

A region with several deposits may therefore provide resources, Food, Influence, and improved extraction from the same underlying map feature.

<h2 id="ruin-progression">
  Should you Raise every Ruin, and how should you spend Wishes?
</h2>

For most Ruins that can be reached safely, **Raise first and decide about Restoration later**.

An army can stand on a Buried Ruin, pay the Dust cost, and spend a turn Raising it. One early Raised Ruin provided permanent empire yields of:

- **4 Dust**
- **2 Science**
- **1 Influence**

That makes remote Ruins valuable even before the Empire decides whether the surrounding land deserves Influence.

Every Raised or Restored Ruin also advances **Ancient Wisdom**. At **16 total Raised or Restored Ruins**, the faction reaches the far end of that progression and gains access to its later abilities.

The first question for a newly discovered Ruin is therefore usually whether the army can reach it without creating a larger strategic problem.

The next question is how much Dust to invest around it.

**Wish of Awakening** begins at **100 Dust** and converts terrain around the Ruin into Sand. Repeated uses expand the converted area, with each additional cast around that Ruin costing another **50 Dust**. The transformed area can eventually extend roughly three tiles from the Ruin.

The first cast near a struggling capital can be extremely valuable because the new Sand becomes useful immediately. The same cast around an isolated Ruin may do almost nothing for the economy for many turns.

I spend the early Wishes where the converted tiles are already connected to a real City or near-term expansion. A remote Ruin can still be Raised for permanent yields and Ancient Wisdom without also consuming several rounds of terraforming Dust.

This prevents an easy Sandshaper failure: advancing the Ruin system rapidly while leaving the rest of the Empire too poor to build units, improvements, or the Cities that are supposed to use the transformed land.

<h2 id="restore-and-teleport">
  Which Ruins should you Restore?
</h2>

Restoration is more selective than Raising.

**Wish of Past Grandeur** turns a Raised Ruin into a Restored Ruin. That upgrade improves the location further, but much of the value depends on what surrounds the Ruin.

The same logic applies to remote locations. Raising a Ruin may be worthwhile for Ancient Wisdom and permanent yields even when no City is nearby. Restoring it immediately is harder to justify unless its position has another strategic purpose.

A Ruin that was Raised early only for progression can later become worth Restoring because it connects the frontier to the rest of the Empire.

The network works for exploration, defense, and offense. An army can push deep into newly exposed land and return without retracing every Territory, reinforce a threatened City from the opposite side of the Empire, or appear near a contested region that would otherwise require a long march.

That makes Restoration a choice between **local economic value** and **strategic location**. The best Ruin does not always need both, but it should have at least one.

<h2 id="monsoon-tidefall">
  How should Sandshapers handle Monsoon and Tidefall?
</h2>

Weather creates a harsher cycle for Sandshapers than for most factions.

**Storm Cursed** increases construction costs during Monsoon. If an important district or improvement is already affordable and the storm is approaching, delaying it can make the same project more expensive.

Aquaphobic also means the faction cannot rely on ordinary Bridge development as a generic answer to difficult waterways.

The compensation appears when the sea retreats.

**Lands of the Past** reveals the shape of land exposed by Tidefall even through the fog of war. It does not reveal every resource, Ruin, army, or exact tile value, but it gives Sandshapers an earlier picture of where the world has opened.

After Tidefall, I prioritize scouting toward new land that combines several faction needs:

- newly exposed Ruins;
- strategic or luxury deposits;
- wide areas of dry terrain;
- positions that strengthen the teleport network;
- or regions another Empire can claim before a Sandshaper army arrives.

A newly exposed region does not have to become a City immediately.

Finding and Raising a pair of Ruins can already improve the entire Empire without spending Influence on the surrounding Territories. That lets the faction extract value from newly opened parts of the map before committing to permanent expansion.

The storm hazards, temporary bridges, Curiosities, Battle Spoils, and coastline timing are covered in the <Link href="/endless-legend-2/tidefall">Tidefall and Monsoon guide</Link>.

<h2 id="combat">
  How do Stone Stance and Sun Shift change Sandshaper combat?
</h2>

Sandshaper combat rewards planning where units finish the round, not only what they attack during it.

**Stone Stance deals 8 damage to every adjacent enemy at the end of the battle round, and that damage ignores Defense.**

Several Sandshaper units can stand beside the same target and apply their own Stone Stance damage.

That can make a weakened enemy effectively dead before another direct attack is spent.

In one fight, an enemy ended the round at **21 Health** while adjacent to three Sandshaper units. Three separate 8-damage Stone Stance hits were enough to finish it.

Before using the final attacks of a round, I count how much Stone Stance damage is already guaranteed.

If the target will die anyway, another normal attack can be redirected into repositioning, defending, weakening the next enemy, or threatening a ranged unit.

The **Shard** adds another positioning tool through **Sun Shift**, a teleport with a range of **two tiles**.

Two tiles are enough to slip around part of a frontline, bypass a choke, or reach an Archer that expected to remain protected. The ability is strongest when the rest of the turn has already been set up.

Teleporting immediately can waste the opportunity if another unit was about to reduce the target's Defense or create a better Stone Stance position first.

I decide where the Shard needs to end the round, then work backward through the attacks and movement required to put it there.

That makes Sandshaper combat less about maximizing every individual attack and more about arranging several actions so that movement, direct damage, and end-of-round damage all contribute to the same outcome.

<h2 id="captured-cities">
  Should Sandshapers keep or raze captured Cities?
</h2>

Captured Cities need to be judged by their layout rather than their population number.

A City built by another faction may contain Farms, waterways, and district placements designed around an economy the Sandshapers cannot use efficiently.

One captured capital presented an **8-turn occupation** compared with **6 turns to raze and rebuild**. The existing City was large, but too much of its footprint had been developed around conventional terrain and Food assumptions.

In that situation, rebuilding offered a cleaner path.

That does not make razing the default response.

Keeping the City becomes much more attractive when it already has:

- a large dry district footprint;
- a useful Ruin nearby;
- several strategic or luxury resources;
- strong locations for future Sand;
- and infrastructure that still provides value after conquest.

The important comparison is not simply how many districts already exist.

It is whether those districts save enough time to justify inheriting the old layout.

A smaller City that fits Sandshaper terrain rules can be easier to turn into a strong settlement than a huge captured capital that spends the rest of the game fighting its own geography.

<h2 id="midgame-priorities">
  What should Sandshapers prioritize once the Ruin network is established?
</h2>

By the midgame, the faction should no longer be solving every problem through the first Ruin beside the capital.

Several Raised Ruins should already be adding permanent yields and advancing Ancient Wisdom, while the better Restored Ruins begin giving individual Cities distinct economic roles.

A Science-heavy settlement benefits more from Perception. A resource cluster can make Eminence much stronger. A growing City with limited room can make better use of Abundance.

At the same time, the first Restored Ruins should begin reducing strategic travel time rather than existing only as upgraded yield sources.

That changes how far armies can explore and how aggressively the Empire can use Tidefall. A unit can Raise Ruins on the frontier, scout resource clusters, and later return through the Ruin network instead of spending many turns walking back through established territory.

The same progression changes expansion.

Early Sandshapers need land they can make usable. Later Sandshapers can make more land useful themselves.

A remote Ruin can improve the Empire before its Territory is claimed. A poor-looking deposit cluster can become a major Food center through Mineral Bred. A distant Restored Ruin can become a military route rather than an economic site.

The faction becomes much easier once those systems overlap. Sand stops being a solution to one starving capital and becomes the terrain base for an Empire whose Ruins, resources, movement, and City specializations all reinforce one another.
