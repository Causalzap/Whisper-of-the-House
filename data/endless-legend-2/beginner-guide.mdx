<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
Scout just long enough to understand the starting region, then found the capital before exploration becomes delay. After that, keep asking one question: **what is preventing the next useful action?** Fix that shortage first, whether it is Industry, Influence, population capacity, army strength, or access to better land.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

The first 30 turns of ENDLESS Legend 2 are easier when they are treated as a sequence of decisions rather than a fixed build order.

The map may put the first useful Minor Faction in a different direction. Your faction may value Dust, Fortification, Ruins, or Villages more than ordinary terrain. Monsoon and Tidefall can change which coast matters. Even two starts with similar yields can need completely different first expansions.

The opening works when the capital, army, research, and territory growth stay close enough together that each new opportunity can actually be used.

<h2 id="first-city">
  When should you found your first City?
</h2>

You do not need to settle on the first hex where the starting army appears.

Your starting units can found the capital directly, and **Prepared Settlement** gives enough room to spend a short time learning the region before committing.

I use those scouting turns to understand the **region**, not to search indefinitely for one perfect tile.

A useful capital area should have enough workable terrain for several districts, room to grow without immediately hitting water or mountains, and access to features that matter later: anomalies, resources, Minor Faction Villages, or strong neighboring regions.

Once the important shape of the area is clear, I settle.

Walking several more turns because a slightly better hex might exist somewhere beyond the fog usually costs more than the information is worth.

Faction mechanics can change what “good terrain” means. Kin of Sheredyn care heavily about City shape and district development, Sandshapers care about Ruins and dry land, while Severed Claws place unusual value on nearby Minor Faction Villages. The <Link href="/endless-legend-2/factions">faction overview</Link> is useful when an apparently strong starting region does not match what your Empire actually needs.

<h2 id="capital-priorities">
  What should you do after founding the capital?
</h2>

Do not immediately decide that every opening needs maximum Industry or maximum Food.

Look for the resource currently delaying the next meaningful action.

Industry is usually valuable because the first improvements and districts need to come online quickly, but Industry stops being the answer once construction is already moving faster than the rest of the Empire.

A capital can produce buildings efficiently and still sit idle because the next Camp is waiting on Influence. Another City can grow quickly and run out of useful jobs. A third can unlock Technologies faster than it can afford to build anything they provide.

I keep four things visible during these first turns:

- how long important construction is taking;
- when the next population will arrive and where it will work;
- how much Influence the next territorial action will require;
- and whether the army is strong enough for the work already visible on the map.

Influence deserves particular attention because several expansion actions begin competing for it very quickly.

Camps, City creation, Territory attachment, and faction abilities can all use the same pool, while expansion costs rise as the Empire grows. If scouts have already found the next valuable region, I want enough Influence left to act before another Empire reaches it.

The opposite problem is hoarding resources with no plan.

Influence sitting unused while no worthwhile Territory has been identified is not helping the opening. Industry pushed far beyond what the construction queue needs is not automatically progress either.

Early workers should move toward the yield that removes the next delay.

Population needs the same discipline. Before another worker arrives, make sure the City has a useful vocation available. If growth has already created Destitute population, Shanties, or falling Approval, stop accelerating the problem and fix it first. The full recovery sequence is covered in <Link href="/endless-legend-2/approval">Approval, Shanties, and rebellion</Link>.

<h2 id="scouting">
  What should you scout for in the first 30 turns?
</h2>

Early exploration has a job: reveal enough information to decide where the Empire should spend its limited Influence and military time.

I want scouts to find:

- strong expansion regions;
- Minor Faction Villages;
- strategic and luxury deposits;
- nearby Empires;
- Curiosities;
- important coastlines;
- and routes that may become contested.

The main army does not need to uncover every empty tile itself.

When the map is safe enough to split units, lighter scouts can reveal terrain while the stronger force moves toward locations where combat power is actually needed. Sending the entire army several turns into harmless fog can leave a Camp, quest, or valuable Village unprotected.

The first Minor Faction is also worth inspecting before deciding that its Territory must be the first expansion target.

For factions using the standard village interactions, peaceful contact, Bribing, fighting, later Assimilation, population, units, and Protectorates can all change the value of that region. Some playable factions change those rules substantially.

The complete set of village decisions is covered in <Link href="/endless-legend-2/minor-factions">Minor Factions</Link>. During the opening, I mainly ask whether this particular Village gives the Empire something it is currently missing.

A useful population type, military unit, strategic position, or strong empire bonus can justify changing the route. A poor fit does not deserve the next Territory simply because it was discovered first.

<h2 id="first-camp">
  What should you do with your first Camp?
</h2>

Founding a Camp secures the region, but it does not answer what the Territory should become.

A new City makes sense when the region has enough workable terrain, district space, resources, and strategic importance to justify an independent production queue and population.

Attachment is often better when the region mainly provides resources, a Minor Faction Village, useful district space, or a strategic connection to a City that is already productive.

City Cap matters too.

A mediocre settlement can occupy capacity that would be far more valuable after another round of scouting or the next Tidefall. Some factions change this calculation — including factions built around one City or unusual territorial rules — so the generic answer should not override the faction itself.

There is also a small but useful Camp timing check when the region contains extractable resources.

Before integrating the Camp, inspect the resource deposits and their extractor costs. In some opening situations, purchasing useful extractors while the region is still a Camp can cost less than waiting until after integration.

I only delay integration for that saving when the region is safe.

If another Empire is threatening the claim, securing the Territory matters more than saving Dust on an extractor.

<h2 id="spending-and-research">
  What should you spend Dust and research on early?
</h2>

An early purchase is strongest when it removes a delay that would block several following turns.

Buying out an Influence improvement can make sense when a rival is approaching the Territory you want. Finishing a crucial military or economic building can also be worthwhile when the result changes what the Empire can do immediately.

Saving a couple of routine construction turns is less compelling.

Dust may soon be needed for an extractor, equipment, a replacement unit, or a faction-specific action. Some factions make that reserve much more important: Order of Zelevas recruit armies with Dust, while Last Lords use it throughout their population and military economy.

Research follows a similar rule.

A Technology matters when the Empire can use what it unlocks.

I choose early research to solve a concrete limitation: Influence income, Approval, population capacity, an important unit, access to a resource, or infrastructure required for the next expansion.

Racing into another era while Cities are still waiting to build everything from the previous one can leave the research screen far ahead of the actual Empire.

Before committing to a Technology, I want to know what I expect to build, recruit, or change once it completes.

Early **Deeds** are worth checking for the same reason.

Some reward goals the Empire may already be pursuing. A Deed involving **25 Foundations** can fit a faction naturally building one enormous City, while eliminating **30 units** can suit an aggressive campaign already fighting constantly.

I do not distort the entire opening to chase a Deed whose requirement fights against the map.

The best early race is usually one that rewards something the Empire wanted to do anyway.

<h2 id="first-army">
  How strong should your first army be?
</h2>

The opening army does not need to defeat another full Empire.

It needs to handle the work the map is already presenting.

That usually means clearing normal nearby threats, completing reasonable quest battles, protecting the first valuable Camps, and surviving combat without needing a complete military rebuild every time.

Repeated heavy losses are a warning that territorial growth has moved ahead of military strength.

Every new Camp creates another location that may need protection. Adding a second exposed frontier while the only army is already damaged does not create momentum; it creates another obligation.

When a fight is likely to remove several important units, I would rather reinforce first and take the battle cleanly than lose the force needed to protect the next Territory.

That does not mean waiting until every army is overwhelming.

The useful threshold is whether the army can do its current jobs reliably enough that one normal fight does not stop scouting, quests, expansion, and defense for several turns afterward.

<h2 id="monsoon-tidefall">
  What should beginners do when Monsoon and Tidefall begin?
</h2>

The first Monsoon is a good moment to stop issuing movement orders automatically.

Temporary Curiosities can appear, unpacified Minor Factions become more dangerous, vision changes, temporary bridges can disappear, and the storm eventually leads into Tidefall.

For a first campaign, three actions matter immediately:

1. make sure important units are not exposed on temporary bridges;
2. collect nearby temporary rewards without abandoning a more important objective;
3. keep a useful scout or army close enough to important coastline to inspect the new land after Tidefall.

When the sea recedes, previous scouting is no longer enough.

A region that looked like a dead end can gain a new route. A quiet coast can expose resources. A Camp that used to sit safely behind the frontier may suddenly be much closer to another Empire's expansion path.

I scout the exposed land before committing the next large Influence expenditure.

That is also why I avoid draining the entire Influence reserve on marginal City Foundations immediately before an expected Tidefall.

The weather effects, temporary bridges, Monsoon Curiosities, Battle Spoils, and changing coastline are covered in <Link href="/endless-legend-2/tidefall">Tidefall and Monsoon</Link>.

<h2 id="turn-30">
  What should a healthy Empire look like around turn 30?
</h2>

Turn 30 is not a deadline for owning a certain number of Cities.

Faction, game speed, map shape, wars, quests, and Tidefall can all create very different healthy positions.

Instead of counting settlements, I look for one thing: **are the important systems still allowing the next decision to happen?**

The capital should be able to continue developing without immediately crashing into a population or Approval problem. Influence income should be sufficient to act when scouts locate a valuable Territory. The main army should handle ordinary nearby threats without needing to be rebuilt after every engagement. Research should be unlocking things the economy can realistically use.

The Empire should also know more about the map than it did ten turns earlier.

Nearby Minor Factions should have a clear purpose or be intentionally ignored. The first important Camps should already have a reason to become Cities, attachments, or resource positions. Scouts should be looking beyond the original region rather than merely clearing fog around the capital.

Different factions can reach this point with completely different maps.

Kin of Sheredyn may still be compact around a highly developed capital. Order of Zelevas may already control far more Territory. Severed Claws can stretch one City across a large area through attached land. Sandshapers may have invested heavily into Ruins rather than conventional expansion.

The warning sign is not being smaller than another Empire.

It is when several unrelated decisions are all waiting on the same unresolved problem.

If the next Camp is waiting on Influence, another population is arriving with no useful job, the army is too damaged to clear the route forward, and research keeps unlocking buildings the City cannot afford, adding another Territory will usually make the position worse.

Stop adding obligations and repair the constraint.

That may mean several turns of Influence production, another military unit, additional vocation capacity, an Approval fix, or simply allowing Dust and armies to recover.

Once that bottleneck is gone, expansion becomes an opportunity again instead of another problem the Empire has to carry.
