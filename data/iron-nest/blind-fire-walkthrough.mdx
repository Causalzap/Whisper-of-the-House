**Mission 12 — Blind Fire removes the safety net you normally use to correct a
bad artillery solution.**

The anti-aircraft batteries across the Turia River block reconnaissance, and
the mission specifically warns that **shell impact imagery is unavailable while
those batteries remain active**.

I solve Blind Fire as a chain:

**Spotters → Reference Point Alpha → AA Batteries 1 and 2 → Reference Point
Bravo → AA Batteries 3 and 4.**

Do not rush the later batteries. Every point built from an earlier calculated
point can carry that earlier error forward.

In one operation, Alpha was located from approximately **131° from Spotter 1**
and **5.9 km from Spotter 2**. Battery 1 then used Alpha as one of its reference
points, and Bravo was later built from Battery 1.

For the early, cleaner positions I use a normal H solution. When the later
chained target has a visibly wider uncertainty area, I prefer a shell with more
forgiveness rather than pretending the estimated point is exact.

If a later battery looks wrong, return to the earliest uncertain reference
point. Do not copy the miss from one blind shot onto every other battery.

<h2 id="blind-fire-route">
  Mission 12: Blind Fire route at a glance
</h2>

Blind Fire felt uncomfortable for a different reason than High Tide.

High Tide gives me too many moving targets.

Blind Fire gives me enough time, but removes the information I normally use to
prove that my solution was good.

The advance on Valencia is stalled at the Turia River because enemy
anti-aircraft batteries are denying reconnaissance over the far bank.

My route is:

1. Mark Iron Nest and every supplied spotter.
2. Do not requisition a Scout Plane while the AA network is still active.
3. Locate Reference Point Alpha from the opening observations.
4. Use Alpha plus the direct spotter reports to solve the first two AA battery
   positions.
5. Keep those construction lines until the first chain is stable.
6. Build Reference Point Bravo from the confirmed earlier references.
7. Use Bravo to solve the later batteries.
8. Expect the uncertainty to increase deeper into the chain.
9. Use a normal H solution when the target point is tight.
10. Give later uncertain positions more blast-radius tolerance when needed.
11. Do not apply one blind-shot correction to every other target.
12. If a later battery looks impossible, return to Alpha or Bravo instead of
    moving the final marker by instinct.
13. Use a Forward Observer only when the supplied chain still leaves a specific
    unresolved problem.
14. Clear the required AA positions so reconnaissance over Valencia can resume.

For the generic geometry behind bearings, distances, and chained points, use the

  Iron Nest triangulation guide
</Link>

Blind Fire is about what changes when I **cannot verify the impact afterward**.

<h2 id="why-recon-does-not-work">
  Why reconnaissance does not work in Blind Fire
</h2>

The opening order is unusually specific.

The anti-aircraft batteries deny reconnaissance aircraft access to the far bank,
and while they remain active I do not receive normal reconnaissance imagery.

More importantly, the restriction includes **shell impact imagery**.

That changes how I handle a miss.

In a normal mission, I can often:

1. fire;
2. inspect the impact;
3. measure the miss;
4. apply a correction.

Here, that feedback loop is unavailable.

I therefore spend more time proving the map **before** I buy and fire the
round.

Forward Observers are still available if I need additional intelligence.

That does not mean I buy one immediately.

My opening data already provides a structured route through Alpha, the first
batteries, Bravo, and the later batteries.

I use that free information first.

<h2 id="mark-opening-points">
  Start by separating known points from calculated points
</h2>

In my operation, Iron Nest was around **F2 0:7**.

I also had three known spotter positions.

Those belong in a different mental category from Alpha and Bravo.

The spotters are **given positions**.

Alpha and Bravo are **calculated positions**.

That difference becomes critical later because an error in a given spotter
coordinate is usually a plotting mistake on my side, while an error in Alpha
can propagate into everything calculated from Alpha.

I use two types of markers on my map:

- confirmed known positions for Iron Nest and spotters;
- temporary construction points for Alpha, Bravo, and batteries that are not
  yet fully trusted.

I do not clean the temporary lines too early.

In Blind Fire, those old lines are often the only way to work backward when
Battery 3 or Battery 4 stops making sense.

<h2 id="reference-alpha">
  Solve Reference Point Alpha first
</h2>

The cleanest first step in this operation was Reference Point Alpha.

The observations were approximately:

- **131° from Spotter 1**
- **5.9 km from Spotter 2**

I draw the 131° bearing from Spotter 1.

Then I measure the 5.9 km distance from Spotter 2.

Where those constraints agree is Alpha.

This is a good place to stop if the map looks wrong.

If 131° and 5.9 km do not produce one believable Alpha area, I go back and
check:

- the correct Spotter 1 origin;
- the correct Spotter 2 origin;
- the subgrid positions;
- whether I reversed the bearing;
- whether I measured 5.9 km from the wrong point.

I do **not** keep moving Alpha until the later batteries happen to line up.

Every later calculation depends on getting this first reference right.

<h2 id="battery-one">
  AA Battery 1: use Alpha as a reference, not as a firing origin
</h2>

After Alpha was established, the first AA battery used another pair of
observations.

In this operation they included approximately:

- **074° from Alpha**
- **006° from Spotter 3**

That gives me Battery 1.

Notice what has changed.

Alpha is now an observation origin.

It still is **not** Iron Nest.

Once Battery 1 is marked, I draw a separate firing line from the actual Iron
Nest position to Battery 1.

That final line provides the turret bearing and range.

This distinction keeps the page from becoming another generic triangulation
tutorial:

**Alpha finds the battery. Iron Nest fires at the battery.**

I never use the 074° Alpha bearing as the turret bearing unless the final firing
line independently happens to match it.

<h2 id="battery-two">
  AA Battery 2 gives me the first real chained-error warning
</h2>

Battery 2 also depended on Alpha.

One set of observations in this operation used approximately:

- **352° from Alpha**
- **4.87 km from Spotter 1**

By this point I have already created Alpha from two earlier observations.

That means Battery 2 contains a dependency:

**Spotters → Alpha → Battery 2**

A small Alpha placement error can move Battery 2 even if I draw the second-stage
measurements perfectly.

I therefore do not judge Battery 2 only by whether its own two constraints
cross.

I also ask whether Alpha was solid enough to deserve being used as a reference
point.

This is the first place where Blind Fire becomes less about drawing lines and
more about **confidence in the chain**.

<h2 id="first-two-shots">
  I fire the cleaner batteries before solving the messiest ones
</h2>

Once the first two positions were stable, I prepared them before committing
ammunition to Battery 3 and Battery 4.

That had two advantages:

- I could remove two well-supported AA positions first;
- later intelligence could be checked against a simpler map.

One observed early solution was approximately:

- **69.9° bearing**
- **11.31 km range**
- H shell
- **3 charges**
- **45.24° elevation**

Another was prepared around:

- **38.9° bearing**
- roughly **10 km range**
- H shell
- **2 charges**
- **46.38° elevation**

These are worked examples from one operation, not fixed answers.

The first shots landed close enough to produce kills.

That gave me confidence that the early part of the chain was basically sound.

But it did **not** prove that every later position inherited exactly the same
error.

That assumption becomes dangerous later.

<h2 id="reference-bravo">
  Reference Point Bravo is where the chain gets longer
</h2>

Bravo depends on an already calculated battery position.

In this operation, the useful constraints were approximately:

- **253° from Battery 1**
- **8.7 km from Spotter 2**

So the dependency has now become:

**Spotters → Alpha → Battery 1 → Bravo**

This is where deleting old measurements too aggressively hurts.

If Bravo looks wrong and I have already erased the Alpha and Battery 1 work, I
have no easy way to tell whether:

- Bravo is wrong;
- Battery 1 was slightly misplaced;
- Alpha was the real source of the drift.

I keep the chain visible until Bravo agrees with its independent Spotter 2
distance.

Only then do I start removing unnecessary clutter.

<h2 id="battery-three">
  Battery 3 is where compounding error becomes obvious
</h2>

Battery 3 depended on Bravo plus another bearing.

One operation used approximately:

- **317° from Bravo**
- **323° from Iron Nest**

This looks like a normal two-line problem.

It is not quite as clean as Battery 1.

The Iron Nest bearing begins at a confirmed origin.

The Bravo bearing begins at a calculated reference point that already depends
on Battery 1, which itself depended on Alpha.

So one side of the intersection carries much more accumulated uncertainty than
the other.

That is why I do not insist that both lines meet on one perfect pixel.

I look for a defensible overlap area and compare it with the uncertainty already
present in the chain.

If the gap is small, I can build a shot with enough blast-radius tolerance.

If the gap is large, I do not solve it by simply taking the midpoint.

I go backward.

<h2 id="battery-four">
  Battery 4 is the point where guessing becomes expensive
</h2>

The fourth battery was the least comfortable position in my operation.

Its chain used previous calculated battery positions again, including a
measurement around:

- **136° from Battery 2**
- approximately **2.9 km from Battery 3**

That means the final point can inherit uncertainty from several earlier stages.

By this point the chain effectively contains:

**known spotters → Alpha → batteries → Bravo → more batteries**

That is exactly why the later target can look less precise even when every
individual measurement seems reasonable.

I do not treat that as permission to randomly choose a nearby building.

I use three options, in this order:

1. recheck the earliest questionable reference;
2. use the wider uncertainty area to choose a more forgiving destructive shell;
3. requisition a Forward Observer if the supplied information still cannot
   support one defensible target area.

The Forward Observer is the **last** step here, not the first.

If my actual problem is that I drew `317°` from the wrong reference, buying more
information does not fix it.

<h2 id="when-to-use-hch">
  When I accept a wider shell instead of pretending the point is exact
</h2>

For the first two batteries, the map construction was relatively tight.

I was comfortable using H.

The later positions were different.

Their locations depended on calculated reference points, and the uncertainty
was visibly larger.

That is when I consider a wider HCH solution.

I am not choosing it because “HCH is better.”

I am choosing it because the target position itself has a larger uncertainty
area.

My decision is:

### Use the smaller solution when

- both constraints cross tightly;
- the target is supported by at least one direct known reference;
- there is little doubt about which point I am firing at.

### Use more blast-radius tolerance when

- the target depends on several calculated points;
- the later lines miss each other slightly;
- I cannot get visual impact correction afterward;
- the wider effect area does not endanger friendlies.

That is a Blind Fire decision.

The Hub can explain what the shells do.

This page explains **why the target-confidence level changes the shell I buy**.

<h2 id="no-impact-correction">
  Do not copy one blind-shot miss onto the next battery
</h2>

This was the biggest mistake in the later part of my attempt.

One early shot landed with some deviation.

It is tempting to think:

> If Battery 1 was off by this much, I will apply the same correction to
> Battery 3 and Battery 4.

I do not do that.

Battery 3 and Battery 4 do not necessarily inherit the same error.

They are built from different combinations of:

- Alpha;
- Bravo;
- Battery 1;
- Battery 2;
- Battery 3;
- direct spotter observations.

A miss on one target may come from that target's chain rather than from a global
gun offset.

Because shell impact imagery is unavailable, I need to be even more disciplined
about this.

If Battery 4 looks wrong, I return to Battery 4's construction.

I do not automatically drag it by the amount another shell appeared to miss.

<h2 id="when-to-use-forward-observer">
  When I finally requisition a Forward Observer
</h2>

The briefing explicitly allows Forward Observers when additional intelligence
is needed.

I save them for a specific failure state.

I buy one when:

- Alpha has been checked;
- Bravo has been checked;
- the correct reference origins are confirmed;
- a later battery still has two genuinely plausible positions;
- the uncertainty is too large for a safe destructive shot.

I do **not** buy one when:

- I have not finished the supplied chain;
- I used the wrong spotter;
- I deleted a reference point and no longer remember its origin;
- the only problem is that two correct lines miss by a tiny amount.

This is where I am willing to spend requisition.

A Forward Observer that removes a real ambiguity can be cheaper than another
blind destructive shot.

<h2 id="when-to-return">
  Where I return when Blind Fire stops making sense
</h2>

Blind Fire becomes much easier when I stop responding to every bad intersection
in the same way.

### Return to the teleprinter when

I cannot tell which observer owns a bearing or distance.

The order is the source of truth.

### Return to Alpha when

Battery 1 and Battery 2 both seem displaced in a similar way.

A shared upstream reference is more suspicious than two independent batteries
failing at once.

### Return to Battery 1 when

Bravo does not agree with both of its constraints.

Bravo depends on Battery 1, so I verify that point before moving Bravo.

### Return to Bravo when

Battery 3 begins drifting while the direct Iron Nest observation looks
reasonable.

### Return to requisition when

The construction has been checked and still leaves a real unresolved target
area.

### Return to the gun only when

I can explain why the target marker is where it is.

That last rule saves the most ammunition.

<h2 id="blind-fire-mistakes">
  Blind Fire mistakes that make the mission feel inconsistent
</h2>

### Buying aerial reconnaissance at the start

The AA batteries are the reason normal reconnaissance is unavailable.

### Expecting an impact image after the first shot

The briefing specifically removes shell impact imagery while the batteries
remain active.

### Treating Alpha as a confirmed map landmark before checking both observations

Alpha is calculated. A bad Alpha contaminates later targets.

### Entering the Alpha-to-battery bearing directly into the turret

That line locates the target. The final gun bearing still comes from Iron Nest.

### Deleting Alpha after finding Battery 1

Battery 2 and Bravo can still depend on the earlier chain.

### Assuming Bravo is independent

Bravo itself depends on Battery 1 in this operation.

### Expecting Battery 3 and Battery 4 to be as precise as Battery 1

Their chains contain more calculated links.

### Using AP because the target feels difficult

A smaller impact does not help when the location itself is uncertain.

### Using the largest shell on every battery

The wider shell is most valuable where the positional uncertainty actually
justifies it.

### Copying the same deviation onto all four batteries

Different targets inherit different errors.

### Buying a Forward Observer before checking the chain

Extra intelligence should remove a specific unresolved ambiguity, not replace
basic map checking.

### Firing because the marker looks plausible

In Blind Fire, I cannot rely on a convenient impact photograph to rescue a weak
solution afterward.

<h2 id="blind-fire-completion">
  What counts as the Blind Fire objective
</h2>

The opening order defines the required problem clearly:

**clear the enemy anti-aircraft batteries so active reconnaissance can resume
and the advance on Valencia can continue.**

That is the route I build the page around.

One of my attempts ended with an unusual separate malfunction message during
the final blind-fire sequence. I do not treat that event as a reproducible
solution step or tell players to trigger it.

The useful, repeatable route is still:

**solve the AA network → destroy the batteries → restore the reconnaissance
corridor.**

The completion achievement is **Nothing Was There — Endure Blind Fire**.

The all-Gold achievement is **Unshaken — Earn gold on all medals in "Blind
Fire."**

The available achievement data confirms those names, but it does not provide
the exact numerical Gold thresholds. I therefore do not claim that a specific
shell count, requisition total, or accuracy percentage guarantees Gold.
