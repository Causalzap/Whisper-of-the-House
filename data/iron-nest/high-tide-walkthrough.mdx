<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
In **High Tide**, I do not try to destroy every landing craft before it reaches
the beach.

The mission is a timed defense. The opening order has the defensive line
holding until reinforcements arrive at **07:14**, with first contact around
**06:59**.

For each landing craft, I take its reported position, course, speed, and time,
pick a future point on that course, calculate a firing solution to that future
point, then subtract shell flight time from the intended impact time.

I use **AP against the landing craft**, keep one active solution assigned to
each gun, and stop chasing perfect offshore interceptions once the incoming
reports begin arriving faster than I can calculate them.

If a craft is already too close to the beach, I move the intercept point
farther down its course instead of rushing a shot from stale numbers.

Most importantly: once High Command tells me I may **Stand down**, I stop.
High Tide does not require me to clear every remaining contact on the water.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="how-to-beat-high-tide">
  How to beat High Tide in Iron Nest
</h2>

High Tide was the first mission where I stopped feeling as though I was solving
one artillery problem at a time.

The individual shots are not especially strange.

The problem is that the next landing craft can be reported while I am still
calculating the previous one.

Then another appears while one gun is loaded and the other is still rotating.

By the time I have four or five sets of numbers on the map, the dangerous part
is no longer ballistics. It is remembering **which numbers belong to which
craft**.

My reliable route is:

1. Mark Iron Nest before first contact.
2. Keep both guns available for AP shots.
3. Wait for an actual landing-craft report instead of guessing a target point.
4. Mark the reported crossing position.
5. Extend its stated course toward the beach.
6. Choose an intercept point far enough ahead that I can actually prepare it.
7. Calculate the shot to that future point.
8. Check shell flight time.
9. Work backward to the time I need to fire.
10. Give the second incoming craft to the other gun.
11. Delete confirmed kills and obsolete construction as soon as possible.
12. If I fall behind, move the intercept point toward the beach rather than
    forcing a late offshore shot.
13. Hold the defensive line until the stand-down order arrives.

The key change from
<Link href="/iron-nest/iron-road-walkthrough">
  Iron Road
</Link>
is that I am now running several moving-target solutions at once.

That makes **queue management** just as important as accuracy.

<h2 id="opening-briefing">
  The opening objective: survive until 07:14
</h2>

In my operation, Iron Nest began at approximately **N1 2:7**.

The briefing established two times I cared about immediately:

- first contact at roughly **06:59**;
- reinforcements arriving at **07:14**.

That gives the mission its real shape.

I am not being asked to sink an unlimited fleet.

I am buying roughly fifteen minutes for the defensive line.

That distinction changes how I play the second half of the mission.

Before first contact, I do not spend the opening minute drawing speculative
ship paths.

I use it to:

- mark Iron Nest accurately;
- clear unnecessary old construction;
- make sure both guns are available;
- check that I have powder charges ready;
- leave map space for several simultaneous routes.

The incoming reports become dense very quickly, so a clean map at the start is
worth more to me than an extra speculative marker.

<h2 id="first-landing-craft">
  First landing craft: build the intercept before touching the gun
</h2>

The first report in this operation was a good demonstration of the whole
mission.

The craft was reported crossing approximately **P10 6:9**, steering
**180°**, at about **19.4 knots**.

Another order projected a beach approach approximately **5 km south** of that
position.

The trap is aiming at `P10 6:9`.

By the time an AP round arrives there, the craft has already moved.

Instead, I projected the craft forward along 180° and chose a point roughly
**3 km** farther along the route.

At the reported speed, that gave me about **five minutes** of movement to work
with.

The resulting firing distance from Iron Nest was approximately **6.67 km** in
this run.

The gun was set with:

- AP loaded;
- **2 powder charges**;
- approximately **40.02° elevation**.

The calculator showed about **26 seconds of shell flight time**.

So I did not fire at the intended impact time.

I fired 26 seconds before it.

That first interception landed successfully.

<h2 id="high-tide-timing">
  The timing rule I actually use
</h2>

For High Tide I keep one relationship in mind:

**planned impact time − shell flight time = fire time**

That is simple on paper.

What makes the mission difficult is that I cannot know the flight time until I
have already:

1. projected the ship forward;
2. measured Iron Nest to that future point;
3. chosen charges;
4. calculated elevation.

Only then do I know how early I must fire.

A later craft in this operation produced a much longer shot of roughly
**13.4 km**.

That solution used three charges and an elevation around the low-50° range.
The calculated flight time was approximately **35 seconds**.

For an intended impact around **07:08**, that meant firing at roughly
**07:07:25**.

I do not treat those numbers as a reusable solution.

The useful lesson is the sequence:

**predict position → solve range → get flight time → calculate fire time**

If I reverse that order, I end up staring at the stopwatch while the craft is
still moving away from the point I originally measured.

<h2 id="manage-multiple-craft">
  The real High Tide problem is keeping multiple craft separate
</h2>

Once the second and third reports arrive, I stop identifying targets in my head
as simply “craft 2” or “craft 3.”

That failed very quickly for me.

At the busiest part of the mission I had enough contacts arriving that I
started asking myself which numbered craft I had already fired at.

That is exactly where avoidable misses begin.

I keep the working queue much smaller now.

### Left gun: one live target

I keep one complete firing solution attached to the left gun.

Once that shell is fired, I either wait for confirmation or deliberately mark
that target as already engaged.

### Right gun: one live target

The right gun handles the next confirmed solution.

I do not casually overwrite its bearing or elevation with numbers from a third
craft while the current round is still waiting to fire.

### New reports wait on the map

A new craft can be marked immediately without immediately becoming a gun
solution.

That separation matters:

**target identified** does not mean **gun ready**.

If I have three new reports but only two guns, the third target waits until one
firing lane becomes free.

This is slower on paper, but much faster than realizing ten seconds later that
I have loaded the correct AP shell into a gun holding the wrong elevation.

<h2 id="when-to-buy-ap">
  When I buy another AP shell — and when I wait
</h2>

High Tide can make me panic-buy ammunition because new contacts keep arriving.

I do not do that anymore.

I buy another AP shell when:

- I have a confirmed landing craft;
- I know which gun will take it;
- that gun does not already have its next AP round;
- I have enough time to load it before the chosen intercept.

I wait when:

- the current craft has not been assigned to a gun;
- I am still unsure whether the previous AP round hit;
- I am already behind on two active solutions;
- the craft is so close to the beach that I first need a new intercept point.

Powder charges are easier to underestimate.

Several medium-to-long solutions in this mission used two or three charges, so
I watch the charge supply whenever both guns are being prepared at once.

A shell without enough charges is not really “ready,” even if it is already
sitting beside the gun.

<h2 id="when-to-stop-offshore-intercepts">
  When I stop trying to hit the craft offshore
</h2>

This was the most important adjustment I made during the mission.

One later craft was already too close for the original timing plan.

Instead of forcing the same intercept, I extended its course farther and gave
myself another minute or more to work.

In another case I moved from an earlier offshore point to a position about
**4 km** farther along the route because I simply could not prepare the gun in
time.

Eventually even that becomes unrealistic.

Once the landing craft are close enough to the beach, I stop treating
“intercept before landing” as a rule.

I treat the shoreline as the next predictable point on their course.

This is the point where High Tide stops being a perfect-accuracy exercise.

The defensive line can survive **some** attackers.

It cannot survive all of them.

My job is therefore to reduce enough incoming pressure to reach the
reinforcement time, not to produce a flawless naval gunnery score.

That also means I do not restart because one craft reaches the beach.

I restart only when the defensive situation is actually collapsing or the
mission fails.

<h2 id="ap-or-h-shell">
  AP for the craft; do not turn every beach contact into another AP problem
</h2>

I keep AP focused on the landing craft.

Its small effective area makes the shot unforgiving, which is another reason
the future intercept needs to be clean.

Later in the operation, once units were already reaching the beach, I also had
infantry contacts appearing around the landing area.

That is where I separate two questions:

**Is this still a landing craft?**

or

**Am I now looking at infantry on the beach?**

I do not keep spending AP simply because the target came from a landing craft
report earlier.

Likewise, I do not switch to a wider explosive shell against a craft simply
because an AP intercept has become difficult.

If I am no longer sure what is actually at the target point, I return to the
map and current reports before spending another round.

This is especially important late in the mission because old landing-craft
markers become stale very quickly.

<h2 id="do-you-need-scout-plane">
  Do I buy a Scout Plane in High Tide?
</h2>

Not early.

The mission already feeds me frequent movement reports, and my early bottleneck
is processing those reports fast enough.

Spending attention on a reconnaissance route while two AP solutions are waiting
usually makes the queue worse.

I only consider extra reconnaissance late, when all three are true:

- several craft have already reached the beach;
- I genuinely cannot tell which reported contacts remain active;
- High Command has not yet issued the stand-down order.

In my successful operation I reached that point and considered sweeping the
beach because the target list had become messy.

The mission ended before that extra information became necessary.

So I do not treat a Scout Plane as part of the standard opening route.

<h2 id="common-high-tide-mistakes">
  The mistakes that cost me the most time
</h2>

### Firing at the reported position

The crossing coordinate is already aging the moment I read it.

I project the craft forward first.

### Choosing an intercept that is too early

A theoretically perfect point is useless if I do not have time to load and aim.

I push the target farther along its route.

### Forgetting shell flight time

The shell must **arrive** at the intercept time.

That means firing earlier.

### Mixing solutions between the two guns

This happened once the map filled with contacts.

I now keep one live target per gun.

### Calling every contact “craft 5,” “craft 6,” or “craft 7”

The numbering becomes hard to track when confirmations arrive late.

I care more about the route, intended impact time, and gun assignment.

### Continuing to chase offshore targets after the window is gone

Once the craft is already reaching the beach, I stop solving an impossible
earlier intercept.

### Running out of charges in the middle of the queue

High Tide burns through charges faster than the first few missions because both
guns can be preparing medium-range AP shots at the same time.

### Assuming one miss means the run is dead

It does not.

High Tide is a hold-until-relief mission.

I keep fighting until the game actually tells me the defense failed.

<h2 id="stand-down">
  You do not need to destroy every landing craft
</h2>

This is the most useful thing I learned from the mission.

Late in the operation I still had uncertainty on the map.

Some craft had been destroyed.

Some had reached the beach.

I was considering another reconnaissance pass because I was no longer certain
which landing-craft marker represented a live target.

Then the order changed.

**Stand down.**

I do not keep firing after that just because I can still see something offshore.

The primary objective is already complete.

That makes the late-game decision much easier:

**before stand down:** reduce enough landing pressure to keep the line alive;

**after stand down:** stop spending ammunition on a mission that is already
won.

<h2 id="high-tide-route">
  My High Tide route from first contact to completion
</h2>

This is the compact version I use on a repeat attempt:

1. Mark Iron Nest immediately.
2. Clear the map before first contact.
3. Keep both guns free and make sure charges are available.
4. Read the first landing-craft position, course, speed, and time.
5. Extend its route toward the beach.
6. Pick a future intercept with enough preparation time.
7. Calculate the firing solution.
8. Subtract flight time from the desired impact time.
9. Assign that solution to one gun.
10. Give the next craft to the other gun.
11. Keep later reports as map targets until a gun becomes free.
12. Confirm hits before recycling a solution whenever possible.
13. If the queue grows, choose later intercept points instead of rushing.
14. If offshore interception is no longer realistic, move the target toward the
    beach.
15. Keep AP focused on confirmed craft.
16. Use extra reconnaissance only if late contacts become genuinely ambiguous.
17. Continue until the stand-down order arrives.
18. Stop once the primary objective is complete.
