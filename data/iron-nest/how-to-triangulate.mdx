<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
To triangulate in **Iron Nest**, place every spotter at the coordinate printed
in the order, then draw each reported bearing **from that spotter**.

The point where the observations agree is the target.

Do not put a spotter bearing directly into the turret.

After locating the target, draw a completely new line from the **current Iron
Nest position to the target**. That final line gives the bearing and range you
actually use for the shot.

A clean Fire and Light example uses spotter reports of **96°** and **53°**.
Those observations locate the enemy, but the final firing solution from Iron
Nest is approximately **65.7° / 12.91 km**.

Later missions add distance measurements, chained reference points, and
Position Reports, but the rule stays the same: **locate the point first, then
measure the shot from Iron Nest.**
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="how-triangulation-works">
  How to triangulate in Iron Nest
</h2>

The mistake that caused me the most confusion early on was treating every
bearing in the teleprinter as a gun bearing.

It is not.

A bearing belongs to the position named in the report.

If the order says:

**96° from Spotter 1**

I begin that line at Spotter 1.

If it then says:

**53° from Spotter 2**

I begin the second line at Spotter 2.

The intersection tells me where the enemy is.

Only after that point is fixed do I solve the actual artillery shot.

My basic route is:

1. Mark the current Iron Nest position.
2. Mark Spotter 1.
3. Mark Spotter 2.
4. Draw Spotter 1's reported bearing from Spotter 1.
5. Draw Spotter 2's reported bearing from Spotter 2.
6. Extend both lines until their intersection is clear.
7. Put one target marker at that intersection.
8. Draw a fresh line from Iron Nest to the target.
9. Read the bearing and range from that final line.
10. Continue with the normal firing solution.

The first six steps answer:

**Where is the target?**

The final measurement answers:

**Where does Iron Nest point?**

Mixing those two problems together is why a map can look correct while the
shell still goes somewhere completely different.

Once I understand which position owns each report, I do not always redraw the
whole construction by hand. I use the
<Link href="/iron-nest/fire-mission-helper">
  {" "}Fire Mission Helper
</Link>
to combine bearing or distance reports, then carry the calculated point straight
into the final firing solution.

<h2 id="fire-and-light-example">
  Fire and Light is the cleanest triangulation example
</h2>

Mission 2 — Fire and Light gives one of the easiest real examples to check
against.

In one operation:

- Iron Nest was at **H3 5:5**;
- Spotter 1 was at **L9 8:6**;
- Spotter 2 was at **L2 3:8**;
- Spotter 1 reported **96°**;
- Spotter 2 reported **53°**.

I place the two spotters before drawing anything.

Then I draw:

- a long **96°** line from Spotter 1;
- a long **53°** line from Spotter 2.

Their intersection gives the hostile position.

What matters is what happens next.

I do **not** enter 96° or 53° into the turret.

I draw a new line from Iron Nest to the intersection. In that operation the
result was approximately:

- **65.7° bearing**
- **12.91 km range**

The completed firing solution then used:

- **3 powder charges**
- approximately **51.64° elevation**

<h2 id="bearing-origin">
  Every bearing has an origin
</h2>

I now read every positional report as if the game had written the origin in
bold.

Instead of seeing:

**96°**

I read:

**From Spotter 1, draw 96°.**

Instead of seeing a Position Report such as:

**336° from A9 4:7**

I read:

**Starting at A9 4:7, Iron Nest lies somewhere along the 336° line.**

That change in thinking fixes a surprising number of mistakes.

Before drawing any bearing, I ask three questions:

- **Where does this line begin?**
- **What point am I trying to locate?**
- **Is this an observation bearing or the final firing bearing?**

If I cannot answer all three from the current order, I return to the
teleprinter before adding another line to the map.

<h2 id="draw-long-lines">
  Draw observation lines long enough to prove the intersection
</h2>

Correct bearings are still useless when I draw them too short.

I do not stop a line where I think the enemy probably is.

I extend both observations far enough that they visibly cross.

Then I zoom in.

For a clean two-spotter problem, I want a small intersection area rather than a
wide wedge.

If two lines almost meet but do not form one perfect point, I check the setup
before trying to compensate manually.

My order is:

1. recheck both spotter coordinates;
2. recheck the internal subgrid positions;
3. confirm each bearing belongs to the correct spotter;
4. make sure neither line was drawn in the opposite direction;
5. remove old lines that may be confusing the result.

Only after those checks do I consider a small mismatch to be observation error.

I do not simply place the enemy halfway between two badly constructed lines.

<h2 id="bearing-and-distance">
  Later missions combine bearings with distances
</h2>

Iron Nest eventually stops giving two neat bearing lines.

Rock of Gibraltar is a good example.

The first reference point, **The Mole**, still behaves like normal
triangulation.

One operation used:

- **096° from Spotter 1**
- **051° from Spotter 2**

Those lines located The Mole.

The next point, **Dockmaster's House**, changed the problem.

Its reports gave approximately:

- **5.69 km from The Mole**
- **086° from Spotter 1**

Now I am not looking for two bearing lines to cross.

I need the point where the distance from The Mole agrees with the bearing from
Spotter 1.

This is where I stop thinking in terms of “draw two lines.”

The more useful rule is:

> Use every independent observation to narrow the same point.

That observation may be:

- a bearing;
- a distance;
- a second spotter bearing;
- another known reference point;
- a Position Report.

<h2 id="two-possible-points">
  What to do when two locations both look possible
</h2>

A bearing-and-distance construction can leave me with two believable
possibilities.

Rock of Gibraltar produced exactly that problem around Dockmaster's House.

The wrong response is:

**This is a coastal mission, so I will choose the point closer to the water.**

That can be a useful sanity check, but it should not decide the solution.

Instead, I keep both candidate points temporarily and use the next independent
report.

For HMS Rockingham, another operation supplied approximately:

- **6.09 km from Dockmaster's House**
- **062° from Spotter 2**

When those measurements were added, the Rockingham solution converged on one
consistent point.

That is when I delete the rejected candidate.

I requisition additional positional information only if the mission's own
remaining constraints still leave two believable final locations.

I do not buy a Forward Observer just because the first intermediate point looks
messy.

<h2 id="chained-measurements">
  Chained measurements amplify an early mistake
</h2>

Siege of Cartagena makes this problem explicit.

The mission warns that spotter bearings can carry an error margin of up to about
**0.5°**, and that errors compound through chained measurements.

That matters because later measurements may begin from points I calculated
myself.

If Reference Point 1 is slightly wrong:

- Reference Point 2 begins from the wrong place;
- the next distance is displaced;
- the next bearing intersects somewhere else;
- the final target can drift much farther than the original error.

When the end of a chain looks wrong, I do not keep adjusting the final marker.

I return to the **earliest uncertain link**.

For example:

- if the third point looks wrong, I recheck the second;
- if the second depends on the first, I recheck the first;
- if the first came from two spotters, I recheck those original bearings.

This is why I keep useful construction lines until the target is confirmed.

Once I know which solution is valid, I remove rejected lines before creating
the final firing line.

<h2 id="position-reports">
  Position Reports use triangulation to find Iron Nest
</h2>

Triangulation is not only used to find enemies.

After an Emergency Move, I can have the opposite problem:

**I know where the enemies are, but I no longer know where Iron Nest is.**

A Position Report gives me a bearing from a known reference point.

One report can tell me the line on which Iron Nest sits.

It usually cannot tell me one exact position.

In one recovery sequence, the first report gave approximately:

- reference point **A9 4:7**
- bearing **336°**

That means Iron Nest lies somewhere along the 336° line from A9 4:7.

It does **not** mean Iron Nest is at A9 4:7.

It also does not give me a safe new firing origin by itself.

A second report then gave approximately:

- reference point **B9 7:5**
- bearing **303°**

Now the two independent lines can intersect.

That intersection gives the new Iron Nest position.

The geometry is the same.

What changes is the unknown point.

Once I locate Iron Nest again, I delete every old firing line and rebuild my
shots from the new origin.

I can keep a confirmed enemy marker if that enemy did not move.

I cannot keep its old bearing, range, or firing solution.

<h2 id="one-position-report">
  Why one Position Report is not enough
</h2>

This was one of the easiest mistakes for me to make because a Position Report
looks very specific.

A report such as:

**336° from A9 4:7**

still describes an entire line.

Every possible point along that line satisfies the report.

If I choose a convenient point on it and calculate a shot, the calculator can
still give me completely reasonable numbers.

The problem is that I calculated them from a firing origin that may not be
where Iron Nest actually is.

My rule after movement is:

  <div className="font-semibold text-amber-950 dark:text-amber-200">
    One report gives me a line. Two useful reports can give me an intersection.
  </div>
  <div className="mt-2 text-amber-900/80 dark:text-amber-100/80">
    I do not rebuild expensive AP shots until the new firing origin is pinned
    down.
  </div>
</div>

If the mission has moved Iron Nest and my next live shot depends on its exact
position, the second Position Report is worth the requisition.

Trying to save that cost by guessing can waste the shell, the timer, and the
entire firing solution.

<h2 id="when-to-buy-more-intel">
  When I buy another Position Report or observer
</h2>

I do not requisition extra information simply because the map looks busy.

I wait when:

- two supplied bearings already meet cleanly;
- a bearing and distance leave one defensible point;
- another free mission constraint can resolve the current ambiguity;
- I have not yet checked whether I used the correct origin.

I buy another positional report when:

- one Position Report leaves Iron Nest somewhere along a line;
- two target locations remain equally plausible;
- a chained construction no longer overlaps well enough to justify an
  expensive shot;
- an AP shot depends on choosing between two candidate points;
- Emergency Move has made the old firing origin unusable.

More information is useful when it removes a specific uncertainty.

It does not fix a solution built from the wrong spotter or reference point.

<h2 id="lines-do-not-cross">
  If the triangulation lines do not cross, work backward
</h2>

When two observations should describe the same location but do not, I use this
order.

### Check the origin

Did I begin each line at the named spotter or reference point?

### Check the coordinate

Did I place the correct grid square and internal subgrid position?

### Check the bearing

Did I read 096° correctly, or accidentally reverse the line?

### Check which spotter owns the report

A perfectly drawn bearing from the wrong spotter is still wrong.

### Check the previous link

If this is a chained problem, is the reference point I am measuring from itself
uncertain?

### Then consider the reported error

A small mismatch can come from observation uncertainty.

A large mismatch usually means the construction is wrong.

I do not solve a large mismatch by dropping a target marker somewhere between
the lines and hoping the shell radius covers it.

<h2 id="target-found-but-miss">
  Why a correctly triangulated target can still miss
</h2>

Triangulation only solves the location problem.

If the target marker looks good but the shot still fails, I check what happened
after the target was found.

### The turret is using a spotter bearing

The firing bearing must be measured from Iron Nest.

### The range begins at a spotter or reference point

The calculator needs the range from Iron Nest to the final target.

### Iron Nest moved

The enemy marker can still be valid while every old firing number becomes
invalid.

### Two candidate target markers are still on the map

Delete the rejected one before measuring the final line.

### The firing card belongs to the old position

A changed origin or changed target means a new bearing and range.

### I fired before the location was actually resolved

If I still cannot explain why one candidate is correct and another is wrong, I
am not ready to spend the expensive shell.

<h2 id="triangulation-workflow">
  The triangulation workflow I use now
</h2>

My current process is deliberately repetitive in one useful way:

1. Read the complete positional report.
2. Mark every known spotter or reference point.
3. Identify who owns each bearing or distance.
4. Draw long support lines.
5. Add distance constraints from their named origins.
6. Keep multiple candidates when the information genuinely allows them.
7. Use the next independent observation to remove ambiguity.
8. Mark one final target or one final Iron Nest position.
9. Delete rejected construction.
10. Draw a fresh firing line from the current Iron Nest marker.
11. Use that final line for bearing and range.
12. Buy or load the final destructive shell only when both origin and target are
    defensible.

If the problem is still at the **locating stage**, I stay on the map.

If the target is known but the firing numbers look wrong, I rebuild the final
Iron Nest-to-target line.

If Iron Nest has moved, I stop using every old firing card until its new
position is confirmed.
