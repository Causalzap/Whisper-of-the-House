<h2 id="aliens-fireteam-elite-2-beginner-guide">
  Aliens Fireteam Elite 2 beginner guide
</h2>

The first hours become much easier once I settle three things before spending Requisition: what I can clearly see, what responsibility I want during a fight, and what enemies the next mission actually contains.

A new weapon does not fix Xenomorphs disappearing into dark walls, an incorrectly connected Perk Grid, or thermal damage being carried into a Synthetic-heavy mission. I get those basics working first, then spend when I can identify a specific problem the purchase will solve.

Aliens: Fireteam Elite 2 uses four-marine squads. Empty positions are filled by Alpha, Beta, and Gamma with their own loadouts, while Quickplay can match with other players across platforms. That makes every Kit usable for solo progression, but some roles still require more positioning or team awareness than others.

For a first campaign run, I value consistency more than a specialized setup that depends on the entire squad playing around it.

<h2 id="change-these-settings-before-the-first-mission">
  Change these settings before the first mission
</h2>

I start with aim sensitivity and ADS sensitivity.

Enemies can attack from the front, rear, walls, ceilings, and vents, so a sensitivity that feels comfortable while tracking one target can still be too slow when a runner crosses behind the squad. I test a full turn and a short weak-point correction separately rather than judging the setting from one stationary target.

**Aim Snap** is useful when tracking small, fast enemies is the main problem. I am more likely to enable it for a new controller player, then turn it off later if it begins pulling the reticle away from an elite or weak point I deliberately selected.

**Field of View** and **ADS Field of View** change how much of the room remains visible while aiming. A wider view helps me catch flanks, but pushing it too far can make distant weak points harder to read.

**Persistent Shoulder ADS** is one of the settings I change immediately in a third-person shooter. When enabled, aiming keeps the shoulder currently in use instead of returning automatically to the right shoulder. That removes an unnecessary camera correction when I am firing from left-side cover.

If visibility is the problem rather than aim, I enable **permanent enemy outlines** or **ADS outlines**.

This helps most in the mine, Hive, and Engineer interiors, where dark enemies can blend into the environment until they are already inside melee range. I prefer outlines to raising brightness until the entire image becomes washed out, because the environment remains readable while the actual threat is marked clearly.

<h2 id="choose-a-first-kit-by-range-and-responsibility">
  Choose a first Kit by range and responsibility
</h2>

Kit choice changes abilities, signature weapons, and the type of problem I can solve during a holdout. Early on, I find it more useful to choose by the job I want to perform than to compare every perk before I have unlocked enough of the system to build around them.

### Duelist

**Duelist** is straightforward when I want direct gun damage without managing a deployable.

It carries two primary weapons instead of a class-specific signature weapon, gets a 20-round Burst Configuration weapon, and uses a Shrapnel Grenade that applies bleed. I pick it when I want flexible weapon ranges and a simple damage-first role.

### Machinist

**Machinist** fits a squad that needs help controlling lanes or preventing flanks.

Its Sentry Turret covers a position, Bodyguard follows and attacks nearby enemies, and Dual Targeting adds a base **10% damage** against enemies recently hit by an ability or turret item.

The most important habit is retrieving deployables before leaving a safe room. Recalling one remotely causes a **20-second cooldown**, while physically picking it up reduces the downtime to about **7 seconds**.

I walk back and collect it when the room is safe. I only use remote recall when returning would expose the squad or interfere with forward progress.

### Marauder

**Marauder** brings heavy weapons and immediate crowd control.

The starting Smart Gun is forgiving against moving targets, Titan Rockets provide area damage, and Blast Cannon can knock back enemies and stagger many elites.

I choose Marauder when I want to stop pressure in a lane rather than rely on precise weak-point targeting.

### Hunter

**Hunter** rewards accurate, deliberate fire.

Particle Pulse can be charged for more damage and an area effect, Stasis Bolt slows enemies inside its field, and Puncture can create a new weak point after concentrated damage.

I choose Hunter when I am comfortable keeping distance and prioritizing elites. I do not choose it simply because a long-range weapon shows the largest number on the loadout screen.

### Medic

**Medic** works close to the squad with shotguns or SMGs.

Advanced Trauma Station grants temporary health in its area and recharges as enemies die there. Combat Stims adds temporary health and reduces ability cooldowns, while Guardian gives the Medic and a defended ally **20% damage resistance for 3 seconds**.

I pick Medic when I am willing to watch teammate position and health throughout the fight. Running far ahead wastes much of what the close-range support kit provides.

### Specialist

**Specialist** is the flexible build-crafting Kit. It mixes abilities, perks, and weapons already unlocked through the other Kits.

That makes it more interesting later than at the start. I do not judge Specialist from a thin early selection of components. I first level the Kit that gives me the weapons and core perks I actually want, then return to Specialist once those building blocks exist.

<h2 id="build-the-perk-grid-correctly">
  Build a legal Perk Grid before chasing a perfect build
</h2>

At class level 1, only **12 grid boxes** are open: six on the left and six on the right. Five more sections unlock at their required levels.

The active abilities sit on the left and right edges, while the passive ability runs along the bottom. That limited early space is why a level-one build can feel cramped even when several useful modifiers have already appeared.

Ability-specific modifiers must physically touch the ability they modify.

If I place one in an open square without connecting it to the correct ability, it takes up space without providing the intended effect. I start from the ability edge, connect the most important modifier first, then use the remaining squares for weapon or miscellaneous perks.

Whenever another section of the grid opens, I rebuild outward rather than keeping the original level-one arrangement just because it still fits.

<h3 id="combat-rating-is-a-gate">
  Treat Combat Rating as a gate, not a build score
</h3>

Missions require a minimum **Combat Rating** to launch, and the number changes as equipment and perks are added.

Meeting that requirement means the loadout is allowed into the mission. It does not prove the weapon ranges, elements, abilities, and cooldowns work well together.

If the game refuses to launch the mission, I fill valid Perk Grid space and equip progressed equipment first.

If the mission launches but the squad still fails repeatedly, I stop treating Combat Rating as the problem and look at what is actually going wrong.

<h2 id="understand-the-three-weapon-slots">
  Understand the three weapon slots before buying a gun
</h2>

The three weapon slots do different jobs, which matters more to me than simply comparing their displayed damage.

### Primary weapons

The **primary** slot is a universal weapon pool shared across Kits.

Duelist carries two primaries, while the other Kits combine one primary with a signature weapon. That makes a dependable primary my safest early investment because I can continue using it after changing Kits.

### Signature weapons

The **signature** slot defines the Kit more strongly.

Marauder uses heavy weapons, Machinist uses heat-based plasma weapons, Hunter gets long-range and beam options, and Medic uses close-range weapons such as shotguns and SMGs.

I buy a signature weapon when I expect to continue playing that Kit. An expensive Kit-specific weapon sitting on an unused loadout does nothing for the current campaign.

### Sidearm

The **sidearm** has unlimited ammunition.

I use it against isolated runners and the last few enemies before a resupply point, especially when both main weapons are already low.

It is an emergency ammunition solution, not my answer to an elite or boss. Buying several sidearm variants early has little value when the important function I need—something that never runs dry—is already available.

<h2 id="how-to-spend-early-requisition">
  Spend early Requisition on equipment you will keep using
</h2>

Requisition arrives slowly at the beginning, while the Armory offers weapons, attachments, consumable items, Challenge Cards, and perks.

I spend in this order: make the weapon I already use more reliable, make the current Perk Grid functional, then buy extras for a problem I have actually encountered.

A small improvement used in every combat room gives me more value than several options left in storage.

<h3 id="when-to-buy-attachments">
  Buy attachments for weapons you already use
</h3>

I buy an attachment when it improves a weapon that is already doing most of the work.

Compatibility matters. A heavy weapon and a standard rifle do not necessarily use the same magazine, barrel, optic, or armature, so I check the available slot before spending.

Some attachments can also arrive through mission or Challenge Card rewards, but those rewards are not always predictable. I use the Armory when one specific missing attachment solves a problem I already understand.

<h3 id="when-to-buy-consumables">
  Buy consumables for a known difficult holdout
</h3>

I avoid spending heavily on consumables while I am still learning a route.

Turrets, grenades, stims, and elemental ammunition can rescue a difficult defense, but they disappear without creating a permanent loadout improvement if the attempt fails.

I buy them after identifying the room where I need them, not simply because there are empty spaces on the radial wheel.

<h3 id="when-to-use-challenge-cards">
  Use Challenge Cards on missions you can already clear
</h3>

Challenge Cards change mission conditions, including usable weapons, enemies, rewards, and Requisition.

Cards that make the run easier reduce the payout, while harder conditions can increase it. Some cards can also award attachments.

I use a reward-focused card on a mission the group already clears consistently. I do not add a weapon restriction to the first attempt at a new campaign stage just for a larger reward.

<h2 id="rank-one-dependable-weapon-first">
  Rank one dependable weapon before spreading XP around
</h2>

Weapons gain XP after extraction and unlock something at every star:

- **1 star:** unlocks the weapon trait, which can also be used on other weapons;
- **2 stars:** unlocks a colorway;
- **3 stars:** unlocks a universal core perk usable across Kits;
- **4 stars:** opens the weapon's augment slot.

Augments can do more than add a small stat increase.

One can replace a weapon's elemental damage, while another can change how the weapon behaves. **Smart Aim Onboard Chipset**, for example, can turn a conventional weapon into a smart-aim gun.

That makes four stars a meaningful breakpoint rather than just another incremental level.

During the first campaign, I keep one dependable primary in regular use long enough to begin unlocking those shared benefits.

I change earlier when the weapon cannot cover the required range, its damage type is badly suited to the next mission, or the Kit's signature weapon is clearly the better tool for the encounter. I do not switch simply because another level-zero weapon has appeared in the Armory.

<h2 id="match-damage-type-to-the-enemy-list">
  Match damage type to the mission's enemy list
</h2>

The mission selection screen shows which enemies are expected in the operation, so I read that before changing gear.

**Xenomorphs are vulnerable to thermal damage and resist electric damage.**

**Synths are vulnerable to electric damage and resist thermal damage.**

I still do not replace a ranked, accurate weapon with an unfamiliar level-zero gun for a small elemental advantage.

First I ask whether the mission is dominated by one enemy family and whether the current weapon's damage is actually being resisted.

Thermal is an easy choice for an all-Xenomorph operation. A mixed mission may be better handled by giving the two main weapons different jobs instead of forcing one element onto the entire loadout.

Elemental ammunition is most useful during the elite encounter or fixed defense that actually benefits from it. Activating it while walking through scattered runners wastes the duration.

If a favorite weapon has the wrong built-in element for the missions I want to run, I inspect compatible Augments after extraction instead of repeatedly buying temporary ammunition.

<h2 id="use-ammo-crates-and-aid-kits-before-moving-on">
  Treat ammo crates and Aid Kits as departure checkpoints
</h2>

During a defense, I stay close enough to the ammunition crate that a refill does not mean abandoning the team.

I refill before the wave, during a safe gap when necessary, and once more before crossing the next door.

That final refill is the easy one to skip. The objective updates, everyone starts moving, and the next door closes behind the squad while both main weapons are still sitting at half reserve.

Aid Kits usually appear near supply points, with one for each human player, and I can carry only one.

If everyone has claimed their kit, another one remains available, and I already carry one while missing a meaningful amount of health, I use the carried kit and take the replacement.

I do not heal a tiny scratch just to clear the inventory slot, and I do not take another player's kit simply because they have not reached the crate yet.

A supply point is also where I decide whether the current setup still makes sense.

I continue when both main weapons are full, the squad has its Aid Kits, and the loadout can handle the objective ahead. Even if one purchase was imperfect, route knowledge and weapon XP still make completing the attempt valuable.

I make another equipment decision after extraction when a new grid section, attachment, weapon rank, or enemy matchup creates a specific reason to change something.

<h2 id="choose-a-difficulty-you-can-learn-on">
  Choose a difficulty you can learn on
</h2>

The campaign has five difficulty settings from **Casual through Insane**. Insane becomes available after completing the campaign on another difficulty.

For a first clear, I choose a setting where I can learn holdout positions, objective interactions, and resupply locations without repeatedly exhausting consumables.

I move up when I can complete familiar missions while keeping ammunition and Aid Kit usage under control.

Higher difficulty also makes team spacing more important because friendly fire becomes a real threat. A stronger Combat Rating does not compensate for four players repeatedly crossing the same firing lane.

<h2 id="fix-the-cause-of-a-failed-mission">
  Fix the cause of a failed mission before buying more gear
</h2>

When a mission fails, I separate **loadout problems** from **tactical problems** before spending anything.

A loadout problem has recognizable signs:

- the mission refuses to launch because Combat Rating is too low;
- basic enemies take too long to kill even when weak points are being hit;
- ammunition runs out despite using every available supply point.

Those are reasons to revisit the Perk Grid, keep ranking a weapon, change an unsuitable damage type, or buy a compatible attachment.

A tactical failure looks different.

The squad leaves an ammo crate without refilling. Four players shoot through the same narrow lane. A Machinist remotely recalls a turret immediately before the next defense. Everyone carries thermal damage into a Synthetic-heavy mission.

Buying another weapon without changing those decisions usually produces the same failure with a slightly larger number on the loadout screen.

Once the Combat Rating is legal, the weapons cover the ranges I need, and the resupply routine is working, I stop rebuilding the loadout after every difficult room.
