<h2 id="first-priorities">
  What should I do first in RuneScape: Dragonwilds?
</h2>

The opening becomes much easier once your base can reliably send you out for a full objective and receive you again without another emergency gathering trip.

Before I start pushing far into Ashenfall, I want five things working together: a **claimed bed**, cooked food, clean water, storage, and repaired equipment. I also keep enough free carry weight to bring materials home instead of leaving useful ore and drops behind because the backpack was already full when I left.

The early quests introduce most of these systems naturally. Follow them long enough to unlock the basic crafting loop, then start extending your trips as your supplies improve. If every expedition still ends because thirst, Rest, broken equipment, or inventory pressure forces you home, the base is not ready for longer exploration yet.

Starting alone also does not prevent you from opening the world to friends later. If you are setting up a cross-platform game or deciding between a normal friends world and an always-online server, see the <Link href="/runescape-dragonwilds/crossplay-coop">crossplay and co-op setup</Link>.

<h2 id="food-and-water">
  Should I cook food or eat it raw?
</h2>

Cook it when you have the opportunity.

One early comparison makes the trade-off clear: a raw berry gave **10 health and 10 sustenance**, while the cooked version gave **20 health and 20 sustenance**. Processing the same food doubled both values, so eating a whole raw stack beside your base wastes healing and sustenance that cooking would have preserved.

I still use raw food when I am stranded far from home and need health immediately. Otherwise, it comes back to the cooking station.

Water works the same way. Dirty water can be purified at a cooking station, so there is little reason to leave on a long trip with a weak water supply once that loop is available. I fill enough containers for the route before leaving, especially when the next objective is a dungeon or a region I have not explored before.

<h2 id="rest">
  Why did my stamina and carry weight suddenly go down?
</h2>

Check **Rest** before changing equipment or assuming a stat has permanently dropped.

As Rest falls, part of your normal stamina becomes unavailable. In the low-Rest states I encountered, maximum usable stamina dropped from **100 to 80 and then 60**. Carry capacity can also fall to **300**, which means an inventory that was comfortable when you left can suddenly become a weight problem halfway through the trip.

A claimed bed fixes the problem.

I rest before a long resource run or difficult fight instead of waiting until stamina has already collapsed. The penalty affects sprinting, gathering, dodging, climbing, and carrying loot at the same time, so leaving home tired can make an otherwise routine expedition feel much harder than it should.

<h2 id="knowledge-and-recipes">
  Why am I missing a crafting recipe?
</h2>

Dragonwilds often waits until you **obtain or process the relevant material** before revealing the next recipe.

Finding a raw ingredient is not always enough. Coarse Thread, for example, opens additional crafting possibilities after you actually produce it. Other processing chains work the same way: the useful recipe may only appear after the refined material exists in your inventory for the first time.

When I bring back an unfamiliar resource, I do three things before assuming the game has not given me its recipe:

1. pick the material up at least once;
2. check whether it can be processed at an existing station;
3. inspect the stations again after producing the refined version.

This also explains why a material may suddenly appear under a station's accepted items after processing something new. The crafting system is continually expanding as new materials enter the save.

<h2 id="classes-and-builds">
  Are there classes in RuneScape: Dragonwilds?
</h2>

You are not locked into Warrior, Ranger, or Mage at character creation.

Combat progression follows what you actually use:

- melee weapons increase **Attack**;
- bows increase **Ranged**;
- spells and magic weapons increase **Magic**.

If I want Ranged to be my main style, I start using bows while normal enemies are still forgiving. Waiting until late game and then switching from an underused skill means the weapon may be appropriate for the region while the combat skill behind it is not.

Armor gives you more freedom. Full sets can favor a particular style, but nothing stops you from mixing melee equipment with useful spells or carrying a bow beside a melee weapon.

Early on, I concentrate most combat experience into one dependable damage style and use the others when they solve a specific problem. That gives me a strong default answer without giving up the range and utility that later encounters sometimes demand.

<h2 id="power-level">
  How do I know if my gear is too weak for a region?
</h2>

Your equipment contributes to **Power Level**, and stronger regions expect stronger equipment. Entering a new region slightly below its local tier is normal because that region often contains the resources needed for the next upgrade.

The useful test is not the number by itself. It is what ordinary enemies are doing to your supplies.

If common encounters take a long time, normal hits remove huge portions of health, and I am spending expensive food simply travelling between objectives, I go back and improve the equipment. When enemies are dangerous but manageable and I can still gather the new region's materials efficiently, I continue.

That prevents unnecessary grinding while still catching a genuine gear wall before it turns every quest into repeated deaths.

<h2 id="special-meter">
  What is the Special bar below stamina?
</h2>

Weapon special attacks in 1.0 use a separate **Special Meter**.

That matters because spending a special attack no longer means sacrificing the stamina you need for every defensive action. The Special Meter recovers independently, while stamina remains available for movement, blocking, parrying, and evading.

That is especially useful against enemies that punish one extra greedy combo with a large hit.

<h2 id="parry-block-evade">
  Should I parry, block, or evade?
</h2>

Each defensive option solves a different problem.

A successful **parry** restores stamina, staggers the enemy, and creates a strong counter window. I use it when I already recognize the attack timing.

Blocking is the safer choice when several enemies are attacking at once or when I do not trust the timing yet. The reward is lower, but so is the risk of mistiming the defence.

Evade has an important variation that is easy to miss. Pressing evade **without a movement direction** performs a shorter evasive move that still gives you invulnerability frames while keeping you close to the target. A full directional roll creates more distance but also forces you to close that distance again before attacking.

Against a familiar melee enemy, I often use the short neutral evade so the counterattack starts immediately. Against a large area attack or a bad position, I use the full roll and create space instead.

<h2 id="ranged-and-magic">
  When are Ranged and Magic useful if I play melee?
</h2>

Both are worth carrying even when melee is doing most of the work.

A bow lets you pull one enemy away from a group instead of walking directly into every target at once. It also gives you a safer option against enemies that are difficult to reach or punish close-range positioning.

Magic has an even broader role. Rune-powered abilities are used for combat, gathering, movement, building, and other utility tasks, so Rune Essence remains useful even on a character that is not built around spell damage.

I keep collecting Rune Essence whenever it is convenient and maintain at least one ranged answer in the inventory. That flexibility is more useful than trying to force the same melee solution onto every encounter.

<h2 id="lodestones">
  Why does Lodestone fast travel cost so many Law Runes?
</h2>

Lodestone cost changes with **distance and carried weight**.

With a lighter inventory, example trips cost **3 and 6 Law Runes**. After the inventory reached roughly **345 weight**, comparable trips increased to **6 and 13 Law Runes**.

That makes unloading part of the travel decision.

Before a long teleport, I put stone, logs, ore, and other materials into storage unless they are needed at the destination. Moving a heavy stack across the map simply because it happened to be in the backpack can more than double the Law Rune cost.

Long trips become expensive quickly. One route back toward the main base reached **37 Law Runes**, so repeatedly teleporting while overloaded can burn through a travel reserve surprisingly fast.

Free world connections are worth using when they shorten the paid leg. A free journey followed by a shorter Lodestone jump is often cheaper than teleporting directly from the original location.

<h2 id="runes">
  How should I manage Rune Essence and Runes early?
</h2>

Do not spend every Rune Essence immediately.

Rune sources appear throughout Ashenfall, while enemies and chests can also provide finished Runes. Once the Rune Altar is available, Rune Essence can be converted into the specific elemental Runes needed for spells and other abilities.

I keep part of the supply uncommitted because Runes serve several jobs at once. They are combat resources, utility resources, and — in the case of Law Runes — travel currency.

Running out of an elemental Rune can disable a spell. Running out of Law Runes can turn the trip home into a long walk.

A small reserve solves both problems better than converting everything for whatever ability happens to be useful at that moment.

<h2 id="storage-ledger">
  How does the Storage Ledger work?
</h2>

The **Storage Ledger** is one of the biggest improvements to a base once the number of chests starts getting annoying.

Interact with the Ledger and matching items in your inventory are deposited into nearby linked chests that already contain those item stacks. Instead of opening six or eight boxes after every expedition, one interaction can clear most of the backpack.

Nearby storage also interacts with crafting. A crafting bench can pull some required materials directly from linked storage, so the ingredients do not always need to sit in your personal inventory before crafting begins.

Production stations are not all identical. A furnace still needs ore and fuel to be loaded into it manually.

That is why I place the main chests, Ledger, and frequently used crafting stations close together. The goal is a compact working area where unloading, crafting, and preparing the next trip happen without crossing the entire building several times.

<h2 id="first-base-size">
  How big should my first base be?
</h2>

Large enough to support the work you are actually doing.

My first base needs a bed, cooking, water handling, repair access, storage, Rune-related stations, and whatever crafting stations match the current material tier. Once those fit comfortably, extra floors and decorative wings can wait.

Building too large early has two practical costs. Construction consumes materials that could become tools or combat equipment, and a sprawling layout increases the walking distance between storage, crafting, and processing.

I expand when a new station, storage function, or group of crafting stations needs room. That keeps the building useful as progression changes instead of spending the opening hours finishing rooms that have no job yet.

<h2 id="ready-to-progress">
  When am I ready to leave the base and push the next objective?
</h2>

Before a long trip, I check the same things every time: Rest is restored, food is cooked, water is full, the main weapon is repaired, enough Runes are packed for the abilities and return trip, and the backpack has space for materials from the destination.

I also carry a second way to deal damage when the next area might punish my main style. A bow beside a melee setup or useful magic beside a ranged setup can prevent one awkward enemy from becoming an unnecessary trip home.

Once those basics are covered, I leave. Waiting until every armor slot, skill level, potion stack, and base station is perfect usually costs more time than learning the next area with a solid but unfinished setup.
