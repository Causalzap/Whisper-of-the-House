<h2 id="first-priorities">
  What should I do first in RuneScape: Dragonwilds?
</h2>

The opening gets much smoother once one trip away from home can actually reach
an objective and return without being interrupted by thirst, broken gear, or a
full backpack.

Before I start pushing deeper into Ashenfall, I want five things working:

1. a **claimed bed**;
2. cooked food;
3. clean water;
4. storage and repair access;
5. enough free carry capacity to bring useful materials back.

The early quests introduce most of those systems naturally, so I follow them
until the basic crafting loop is working instead of disappearing into the
wilderness immediately.

My sign that the setup is ready is simple: ordinary exploration no longer ends
because something unrelated to the destination forces me home.

The first shelter does not need to become a huge permanent build. Once chests,
workstations, farming, and processing start competing for space, the
<Link href="/runescape-dragonwilds/starter-base">
  starter base guide
</Link>
covers where I would build the first long-term base and how I divide its
working areas.

<h2 id="food-and-water">
  Should I cook food or eat it raw?
</h2>

Cook it whenever returning to a cooking station is practical.

One early comparison makes the difference obvious: a raw berry gave **10
health and 10 sustenance**, while the cooked version gave **20 health and 20
sustenance**.

That is double the value from the same item.

I still eat raw food when I am stranded away from home and the immediate
health matters more than efficiency. Near the base, I bring the stack back and
process it instead.

Water deserves the same preparation. Dirty water can be purified once the
cooking loop is available, so I fill enough containers before a dungeon or a
region I have not explored yet.

A trip ending because thirst ran dry usually tells me the preparation was too
light, not that the destination was unreachable.

<h2 id="rest">
  Why did my stamina and carry weight suddenly go down?
</h2>

Check **Rest** before changing equipment or assuming a stat has permanently
dropped.

Low Rest affects more than one system at once. In the low-Rest states I
encountered, usable stamina dropped from **100 to 80 and then 60**, while carry
capacity could fall to **300**.

A claimed bed restores Rest.

I prefer fixing it before a long gathering run or difficult fight. Low stamina
hurts sprinting, climbing, gathering, dodging, and combat at the same time,
while reduced carry capacity can turn loot that fit comfortably at the start
of the trip into an encumbrance problem later.

That combination is why an ordinary route can suddenly feel much harder even
though neither the enemies nor the equipment changed.

<h2 id="knowledge-and-recipes">
  Why am I missing a crafting recipe?
</h2>

New recipes often depend on actually **obtaining or processing the material**
that belongs to the next crafting step.

Picking up the raw ingredient is not always the final trigger.

Coarse Thread is a good early example: producing the refined material opens
additional crafting possibilities. Other chains follow the same pattern, so I
do not assume a recipe is missing just because the raw resource is already in
storage.

When something seems absent, I check the progression in this order:

1. pick up the new material;
2. process it at the station that already accepts it;
3. return to the relevant crafting bench;
4. check the available recipes again.

That last return to the bench matters. A material can already be in your
inventory while the useful recipe does not become obvious until you go back
through the crafting step that uses it.

The system therefore rewards processing unfamiliar materials at least once
instead of leaving every new resource as an untouched stack in storage.

<h2 id="classes-and-builds">
  Are there classes in RuneScape: Dragonwilds?
</h2>

There is no permanent Warrior, Ranger, or Mage choice at character creation.

Combat skills grow through the equipment you actually use:

- melee weapons increase **Attack**;
- bows increase **Ranged**;
- spells and magic weapons increase **Magic**.

That makes an early decision useful even though the character remains
flexible.

If I want Ranged to become my main damage style, I start using bows while
ordinary enemies are still forgiving. Waiting until a late region and then
switching to an almost-unused skill means the weapon may belong to the correct
gear tier while the skill behind it is still lagging badly.

Armor gives more freedom than the skill system might suggest. Nothing stops a

Early on, I concentrate most combat experience into one dependable style while
keeping the others available for jobs that the main weapon handles poorly.

<h2 id="power-level">
  How do I know if my gear is too weak for a region?
</h2>

Equipment contributes to **Power Level**, but I treat the number as a warning
rather than a rule that says I cannot enter the next region.

Being slightly below the local tier can be normal because the materials for
the next upgrade may come from that region.

The better test is what ordinary combat is costing.

When basic enemies take too long to kill, normal hits remove large chunks of
health, and routine travel is consuming the food I wanted for the actual
objective, I go back and upgrade.

When enemies are dangerous but manageable and I can still collect the new
materials without draining the whole inventory, I keep going.

That avoids grinding an old region simply because one displayed number is not
perfect while still catching a real equipment wall before it turns into
repeated deaths.

<h2 id="special-meter">
  What is the Special bar below stamina?
</h2>

Weapon special attacks in 1.0 use a separate **Special Meter**.

That separation changes how I manage stamina. A special attack can add damage
without consuming the same resource I need for defensive movement, parries,
blocks, and evades.

<h2 id="parry-block-evade">
  Should I parry, block, or evade?
</h2>

They solve different situations.

A successful **parry** restores stamina, staggers the enemy, and opens a strong
counter window. I use it when I already recognize the timing.

**Block** is the safer answer while learning an enemy or when several attacks
are overlapping. It gives up some offensive reward in exchange for a less
demanding defensive choice.

Evade has another option that is easy to overlook.

Press evade **without holding a movement direction** and the character performs
a shorter evasive movement with invulnerability frames while staying close to
the target.

That short evade is often better than a full roll against a familiar melee
enemy because the counterattack begins immediately. A directional roll covers
more ground, which is what I want when escaping an area attack or fixing bad
positioning.

My choice therefore depends on what happens after the defence:

- parry when I want the counter window;
- short neutral evade when I want to stay in range;
- full roll when I need space;
- block while the timing or situation is still uncertain.

<h2 id="ranged-and-magic">
  When are Ranged and Magic useful if I play melee?
</h2>

Both solve problems that melee handles inefficiently.

A bow can pull one enemy away from a group instead of starting an entire pack
at once. It also gives me an answer for targets that are dangerous or awkward
to stay beside.

Magic covers even more jobs. Rune-powered abilities are used in combat,
gathering, movement, building, and other utility systems, so Rune Essence
remains useful even when spell damage is not the main build.

I therefore keep collecting Rune Essence when I pass a convenient source and
carry at least one ranged answer into unfamiliar territory.

That does not mean every character needs three equally levelled combat skills.
It means one difficult enemy should not force a return to base simply because
the main weapon cannot solve the position safely.

<h2 id="runes">
  How should I manage Rune Essence and Runes early?
</h2>

Rune Essence is useful far beyond direct spell damage, so I avoid converting
or spending every piece as soon as I find it.

Enemies, containers, and Rune sources can provide finished Runes, while the
Rune Altar expands what can be produced as Runecrafting progression improves.

The useful early habit is keeping a reserve.

Elemental Runes power combat and utility magic. Law Runes become especially
important once you start depending on teleportation. Reaching a distant
objective and discovering that the return trip needs a resource you already
spent on convenience is avoidable.

I do not manage the full teleport network from this early-game loop, though.
Once Lodestones start becoming part of normal travel, the
<Link href="/runescape-dragonwilds/fast-travel">
  fast travel guide
</Link>
covers Vault Cores, Lodestone construction, Law Rune costs, carried-weight
scaling, and when another forward teleport point is worth building.

<h2 id="storage-ledger">
  How does the Storage Ledger work?
</h2>

The **Storage Ledger** becomes valuable once returning from an expedition means
opening chest after chest just to unload the backpack.

Interact with the Ledger and matching items from your inventory are deposited
into nearby linked chests that already contain those stacks.

That makes the Ledger most useful when storage already has some organization.
The destination chest needs the matching stack, so throwing every resource
randomly into empty boxes undermines the convenience.

Nearby storage also helps with crafting. Some crafting stations can use
materials directly from connected storage instead of requiring every ingredient
to sit in the character inventory.

Do not assume every machine works that way. A furnace still needs ore and fuel
loaded manually.

For the early game, the practical result is simple: keep storage and the
stations used most often in the same working area. Once the whole property
starts needing more deliberate indoor and outdoor zones, use the
<Link href="/runescape-dragonwilds/starter-base">
  starter base layout
</Link>
rather than continuing to enlarge the original shelter one random station at
a time.

<h2 id="ready-to-progress">
  When am I ready to push the next objective?
</h2>

Before a long trip, I run through the same checks:

- Rest restored;
- cooked food packed;
- clean water filled;
- main weapon repaired;
- enough Runes for the abilities I expect to use;
- free carry capacity for materials at the destination;
- one backup way to deal damage when the main style is a bad match.

That is enough.

Waiting until every armor piece, skill, food stack, workstation, and base
upgrade is perfect usually delays progression more than it helps.

A solid but unfinished setup teaches me more by entering the next region than
another hour spent polishing systems that are already doing their job.

The first major fight that brings the early survival and combat systems
together is Velgar. Once ordinary exploration feels stable, continue with the
