A shop can have thousands of credits worth of water, wine, or rat meat and still struggle to make its next rent payment. Inventory value isn't the same as money available to spend. The buyer may not arrive, the market may change, or the goods may take another night to produce.

That makes choosing the most profitable business more complicated than comparing the highest-priced products. **How much cash do you need to commit, how long will it be unavailable, and how much can you realistically sell before rent is due?**

Use the current prices from your own shop to compare those outcomes. Water Merchant, Wine, Rat Farming, and ordinary buy-and-resell trading all fit the same calculation, even though their production methods are different.

## Compare Your Money-Making Options

Choose one of the three starting situations below, then replace the sale prices, quantities, production costs, and waiting times with the figures from your current game.

<MoneyMakingPlanner />

The Planner's **Day 1–7** inputs represent the next seven days from your current shop position, not the absolute day number shown on the Calendar. If you are currently on game Day 77 and a sale is expected tomorrow, enter the next day in the Planner's seven-day window rather than entering 78.

Enter the cash you have available **after any purchases already completed**. Costs entered as paid now are deducted at the beginning of the projection. Only expected sales within the seven-day window contribute to the displayed cash flow.

The comparison uses the sale quantity you enter, not the entire estimated value of your inventory. A customer who can afford only one piece of meat cannot turn five pieces into five completed sales.

**Full Batch Cost Basis** records what the batch cost to acquire or produce, including amounts paid before today. **Of That, Cash Paid Now** records only the portion that will leave your current balance at the start of this calculation.

For example, if you bought a household item for **6 Credits yesterday** and your Current Cash already reflects that purchase, enter **6** as its cost basis and **0** as cash paid now. Selling it for **7 Credits** produces **1 Credit in gross trading profit**, while the seven-day cash projection receives the 7-Credit sale without deducting yesterday's purchase again.

If you are buying that same item today instead, enter **6** in both cost fields. Do not add the same purchase again under Extra Operating Costs Now.

A new machine goes under **New Equipment Cost Now**. Its purchase reduces your cash balance immediately, while its longer-term payback is calculated separately from the profit on a single batch.

For a Chemist business, only count Dream Dust that remains available for sale **after reserving the Cartel's required delivery**. Include Dream Caps, Alcohol, power, and processing losses when estimating the batch cost. The sale price must reflect the finished product's actual grade and the buyer's offer. [Check the Dream Dust production and delivery requirements](/probably-stolen/chemist) before treating the whole batch as saleable inventory.

## Case 1: Make Enough Money Before the Next Rent Payment

Load **Rent Survival** to compare a shop with **400 Credits** available and **240 Credits in rent due on Day 6**. This example comes from a particular Hard Mode opening with the Bad Lease perk; it is not the guaranteed starting balance or rent payment in every game.

Without additional sales, the shop would have **160 Credits remaining after rent**. That is the amount available for optional investment if the next payment must remain protected.

Replace these starting values with your actual cash, rent, and due date. Then compare three possible decisions:

- **Sell stocked water:** Use water already in storage, enter its actual sale quote, and estimate how much you can sell before rent.
- **Purify then sell:** Include any new filters, energy, or other processing expenses and the day when the finished water can actually be sold.
- **Buy equipment first:** Include the equipment purchase and production costs, then check whether the expected sale occurs early enough to protect rent money.

The important result is the **lowest cash balance**, not simply the largest projected profit. A machine may pay for itself eventually while leaving you unable to afford a payment due in two days.

There's also a practical limit to the calculation: money from a buyer who hasn't arrived yet is projected income. For rent due immediately, preserve the required credits before counting on another sale.

<section id="water-crisis">

## Case 2: Sell Water Now or Process It During a Water Crisis?


During a **Water Supply Crisis**, compare the customer's current offer with the Credits you still need for rent. The quoted price may already include the event bonus, while improving the water could require another overnight processing cycle.

The decision depends on whether selling immediately, purifying first, or holding the inventory leaves you with enough cash when the next payment is due.

### Test three decisions with your current inventory

**Sell now.** Enter the amount a buyer is currently offering for the water you can sell immediately. Include the number of bottles or jugs they can actually afford.

**Purify first.** Enter the expected price of the improved water, plus any additional filters, energy, or materials you must purchase. Set the sale day to account for processing time.

**Hold the water.** Enter the price you would need to receive later and the day when you expect to sell it. The Planner can show whether that price would make the decision worthwhile, but it cannot guarantee the market event or buyer will still be available.

These are alternative uses of the same inventory, so compare them separately. Do not combine all three as if the shop could sell the same water, purify it afterward, and sell it again.

When the crisis ends, update the quote and compare again. A stored batch may still be valuable, but its prospective sale price is no longer the same.

If your limitation is the actual water grade rather than the financial decision, follow the [Pure Water production steps](/probably-stolen/water-purification) before estimating the finished batch's value.

## Case 3: Is Water, Wine, or Rat Farming More Profitable?

A single sale can make wine or rat meat look much more profitable than bottled water. That comparison changes when the products require different amounts of starting stock, feeding, production time, and packaging.

Load **Water vs Wine vs Rats**, then enter one realistic batch for each business.

### Water: Include the cost of getting it ready for sale

Water may already be sitting in your storage when a buyer arrives. In that case, the new cash needed to complete the transaction can be small, even though the original water and its processing equipment were not free.

For water you still need to produce, include the additional materials and energy you expect to use. Enter the number of saleable bottles or jugs rather than the number of containers you own.

Producing more water won't improve your cash flow if storage is full or customers cannot afford the finished stock. Before buying another machine, [check whether containers, storage, or rent are limiting your water business](/probably-stolen/water-merchant-guide).

### Wine: Count what you put into the batch

Wine requires a **Wine Bottle, BloomBerry, and water**, with Yeast affecting fermentation time and quality. Count the materials used for the actual bottle you plan to sell, rather than comparing its final price against water or rat meat without production costs.

For a new batch, enter the cost of the Wine Bottle, BloomBerry, water, and any Yeast you purchase. Set the expected sale day after fermentation finishes. If you intend to age the finished wine, use the later sale date and the price you reasonably expect to receive.

A higher-value bottle is not automatically the better short-term investment. Wine that remains in storage cannot cover rent until a suitable customer buys it. Compare the additional sale value from aging with the time your Credits remain tied up.

### Rats: Include the food needed to reach harvest

Rat farming has a different cost pattern because the colony needs to be kept alive and fed while the animals grow.

Count the feed, cage maintenance, and other expenses assigned to the animals you intend to harvest. Enter the number of saleable meat items you expect to obtain, not simply the number of adult rats in the cage.

For example, a breeding operation may contain valuable adults that should remain alive for future litters. Excluding those animals from the planned harvest gives a more realistic estimate of the meat available for sale. An unsold Steak still has value in storage, but it does not contribute Credits to the current seven-day cash balance.

Before counting a rat as saleable meat, [check whether it should be kept for breeding](/probably-stolen/rat-breeding). Enter only the harvest you actually intend to sell.

### Compare the time required, not just the batch profit
Enter two batches with the same expected profit but different sale dates. Moving one sale from Day 2 to Day 5 may leave the final profit unchanged while pushing your lowest cash balance below the next rent payment.

Compare the minimum projected balance before choosing the slower route. A larger batch is only useful when the shop can afford its costs until the money arrives.

## Buying Cheap and Reselling: Don't Confuse Markup With Profit

Ordinary item trading can also be evaluated without a production machine.

One shop transaction involved buying a household item for **6 Credits** and selling it for **7 Credits**. The difference was **1 Credit before any other expenses**.

For one unit:

- Buy price: **6 Credits**
- Actual sale price: **7 Credits**
- Gross trading profit: **1 Credit**
- Return relative to purchase cost: approximately **16.7%**

That percentage may look attractive, but selling a single cheap item doesn't produce much cash. Large percentage returns and large absolute profits aren't the same thing.

The more important questions are whether the shop can purchase enough suitable stock, whether buyers will purchase the quantity, and how long the inventory remains unsold.

Enter the purchase cost as the batch cost, the cash spent today as the immediate payment, and the actual customer offer as the unit sale price.

Avoid treating every item with a high estimated value as a guaranteed opportunity. Its displayed value, the price you're offered, and the amount a buyer is able to pay can differ.

A **Perfume** bonus can improve the return from a valuable trade, but the extra revenue needs to exceed its cost. In one Red Ink run, Perfume provided a **15% trading bonus** at approximately **30 Credits per use**. At that cost, the break-even transaction value is **200 Credits**, assuming the full bonus applies to the completed sale.

A 100-Credit sale would gain only 15 Credits, leaving a 15-Credit loss after using Perfume. A qualifying 400-Credit sale would gain 60 Credits, leaving **30 Credits in additional profit** after the Perfume cost. This makes the bonus more attractive for expensive water sales or other high-value transactions than for small purchases.

Calculate the bonus from the amount the customer can actually pay, not the estimated value of everything on your counter. If the quoted sale price already includes Perfume's effect, enter that final price in the Planner without adding another 15%. Include the Perfume expense in your costs only once.

## How Profit, Equipment Payback, and Seven-Day Cash Flow Work

The Planner deliberately separates three calculations that answer different questions.

### Batch profit

**Batch Profit = Actual or Expected Sale Revenue − Full Batch Cost − Additional Operating Costs**

Full Batch Cost Basis includes the purchase or production costs assigned to the goods, including materials bought earlier. Extra Operating Costs should contain only expenses that have not already been included in that cost basis.

For example, if the cost basis of a wine batch already includes its BloomBerry, water, and Wine Bottle, do not enter those materials again as extra operating costs. Otherwise, the projected profit will be understated.

The sale revenue uses the smaller of the number of units available and the number you expect to sell. Goods left in storage are not counted as completed sales.

### Equipment payback

Buying a machine reduces available cash immediately. It isn't the same as consuming all of its purchase value in one batch.

The estimated payback time compares its purchase cost against the projected earning rate of the selected batch. That estimate assumes comparable batches and selling conditions can continue.

A changing market, inconsistent buyers, production interruptions, or a different recipe can all make the actual return take longer.

### Seven-day cash flow

The cash simulation starts from your entered balance and applies:

1. Cash purchases, operating costs, and new equipment investment at the beginning of the period.
2. Expected sales on the day specified for each selected business.
3. The next rent payment on its due day.

The lowest projected balance matters because a profitable plan can run out of cash before a delayed sale occurs.

A negative balance indicates that the assumed spending or timing is not affordable under the conditions entered. Increasing the sale price won't solve a cash shortage that happens before the sale.

The model treats each selected route as **one batch within seven days**. It does not automatically repeat production, predict customer arrivals, or invent future events.

Because the Planner places new purchases and operating costs at the beginning of the seven-day period, it is a conservative estimate when some of those expenses would actually occur later. It does not schedule individual daily purchases or simulate repeated production cycles.

Use the result to judge whether the current plan is affordable. Recalculate after completing a sale, purchasing supplies, or reaching the next rent payment.

### Combining two businesses

Water and Wine, or Water and Rat Farming, can be compared as a combined operation when they use genuinely separate inventory and costs.

Only enable two-route combinations after confirming that the same supplies, spending, or items have not been assigned to both businesses.

For example, water already reserved as a wine ingredient cannot also be counted as a separate finished-water sale. Counting both would overstate the value of your inventory.

The combined cash balance includes both sets of upfront spending and both expected sale dates. That allows you to see whether a slower business can be supported by an earlier-paying one without spending money the shop doesn't yet have.

## How Do 1.0 Equipment Prices Change Your Profit Calculation?

The 1.0.0.2 water balance update reduced the base prices of several important production tools:

- **Water Purifier:** 300 → 250 Credits.
- **Moisture Farm:** 650 → 350 Credits.
- **Water Filter:** 50 → 25 Credits.

The same update lowered Dr. Jackson's supplier markup on Normal difficulty from **25% to 15%**.

These are baseline balance changes, not guaranteed purchase quotes for every customer, supplier, difficulty, or market event.

The cheaper Moisture Farm makes starting your own water production more accessible than it was under the earlier prices. However, a lower purchase cost does not automatically make a new production line profitable. You still need enough power, containers, and customers to turn its output into completed sales.

When evaluating an upgrade, enter the price currently offered in your shop under **New Equipment Cost Now**. Use the equipment payback result to judge whether its expected production is worth the upfront expense.

### When Should You Save Instead of Reinvesting?

Once your business produces consistent income, compare the next equipment purchase with the Credits required to buy the store.

A Water Merchant already holding several full jugs may gain little from another Moisture Farm. A Rat Wrangler with a stable breeding group may benefit more from harvesting existing livestock than expanding into an enclosure it cannot afford to feed.

The Planner only evaluates the next seven days, so it cannot predict exactly when you will afford the store Deed. Use it to protect rent and short-term production cash, then keep additional Credits available for your ownership goal.

For the **Prodigious** achievement, the store must be purchased before Day 50. [Check the ownership achievement requirements](/probably-stolen/achievements) before committing funds that could delay the purchase.
