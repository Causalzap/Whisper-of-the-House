<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
When I command the USA, I do not begin by looking for the biggest ability I can
drop on the enemy point.

My opening loop is:

**get Engineers building Nodes → establish backup Garrisons → identify the
helicopter crews that are actually communicating → keep Transport and Supply
Helos on useful jobs → watch the backline for NVA Tunnel pressure.**

I protect **Munitions** in particular.

A USA Supply Drop costs **150 Munitions**, so I do not automatically spend one
every time a Squad Leader asks for supplies if a Supply Helicopter, Supply
Truck, or nearby Specialist can solve the same problem.

Once the team has resource income and more than one useful route back into the
fight, I start spending Commander abilities.

My rule for Recon, Reinforce, Napalm, and Artillery is the same:

**use the ability when the team can immediately turn its effect into map
control.**
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="first-three-minutes">
  My first three minutes are about infrastructure and communication
</h2>

The opening is where I can prevent a lot of the emergencies that show up later.

I do not spend the first few minutes staring at the Orders panel waiting for an
ability to become useful.

I start with three conversations.

**First: Engineers.**

I ask for Resource Nodes early.

**Second: Squad Leaders.**

I want a quick command-channel response so I know which units are listening and
which ones I can rely on when I need a defensive rotation or another Garrison.

**Third: helicopter crews.**

The USA has a major mobility advantage, but only if somebody is doing useful
work with it.

I want to know:

**Who is moving infantry?**

**Who is running supplies?**

**Who will report a hot LZ instead of repeatedly landing into it?**

**Who is using Transport Helo reconnaissance when we need information?**

At the same time, I look at the defensive spawn situation.

I do not need a perfect layout three minutes into the match.

I need an answer to:

**If the first useful Garrison dies, where do we spawn next?**

If the answer is HQ, I already have a problem.

<h2 id="get-nodes-up">
  I get Nodes working before the expensive part of the match
</h2>

Commander abilities draw from three resources:

**Manpower**

**Munitions**

**Fuel**

Engineers can build up to **three Nodes of each resource type**.

In one live Commander match, each completed Node was contributing **+10** of its
resource.

With three built Nodes of the same type, that becomes meaningful recurring
income rather than a small Engineer side project.

I also saw **Encouraged** used around this economy.

With the Nodes already built, their contribution increased from **+10 to +20**
during the five-minute effect.

That gives me a useful order of operations:

**build the economy first → amplify a working economy later.**

I do not wait until Munitions are nearly empty and then suddenly ask whether
anybody built Nodes twenty minutes ago.

If I see weak resource income early, I ask for an Engineer while the team still
has time to fix it.

<h2 id="assign-helicopters">
  I give helicopters jobs instead of just asking for helicopters
</h2>

A helicopter being airborne is not automatically helping me.

It needs a job.

When enough pilots are available, the opening balance I like is roughly:

**two Transport Helicopters + one Supply Helicopter.**

That is a preference, not a rule.

I care more about covering the jobs than hitting an exact aircraft count.

Transport crews are useful for:

- moving infantry across long distances;
- repositioning Squad Leaders;
- picking up groups that would otherwise spend several minutes walking;
- getting troops into a new sector quickly;
- and using their reconnaissance tools over suspicious approaches.

The Supply Helo gives me another way to put supplies where a future Garrison
needs them without paying Commander Munitions every time.

That distinction matters a lot on USA.

If a pilot can safely solve the supply problem, I would rather keep my Munitions
for situations where speed or Commander abilities matter more.

I do not micromanage pitch, yaw, Collective, landing, or Helipad technique from
the Commander seat.

Those belong in the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/helicopter-controls">
  {" "}Helicopter Controls guide
</Link>
.

As Commander, the question is:

**Where can this aircraft create the most value next?**

<h2 id="defense-before-offense">
  I build the boring defensive route before the exciting offensive one
</h2>

One of the most expensive mistakes I make is seeing a great deep offensive
Garrison opportunity while the previous point is still depending on one spawn.

That feels aggressive.

It is usually fragile.

NVA pressure does not have to arrive directly from the visible frontline.

A Tunnel route can make a quiet part of the backline relevant very quickly.

So my preferred defensive state is:

**at least two useful Garrisons around or behind the defensive area**

plus:

**another route that lets defenders approach from a different direction.**

I do not treat that as a strict geometric triangle.

Terrain, legal placement, supplies, and the current objective can make the
actual shape messy.

The principle is redundancy.

If one Garrison disappears, the team should not immediately be asking:

**Why am I spawning all the way back here?**

Exact Garrison supply costs, territory rules, and spacing belong in the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/spawn-garrison-outpost-guide">
  {" "}Spawn, Garrison, and Outpost guide
</Link>
.

From the Commander seat, I keep one simpler question in my head:

**If this spawn dies, where does the defense come from next?**

If I cannot answer that, I fix defense before requesting another deep offensive
spawn.

<h2 id="watch-for-tunnel-pressure">
  Strange pressure behind the point makes me investigate the backline
</h2>

NVA Tunnel pressure often does not look like a normal frontline attack.

If a rear Garrison suddenly becomes pressured, or infantry begins appearing from
an area that looked quiet a minute ago, I stop assuming the main fight explains
everything.

I look for three clues.

### A Garrison is pressured from the wrong direction

If the visible fight is east of the point but a western or rear spawn is suddenly
warning about enemies, I take that seriously.

### Reconnaissance shows movement away from the obvious attack

A Transport Helo can help expose movement over jungle, river edges, and routes
that ground infantry has not been watching.

### I hear the Tunnel radio sound

When I personally move through the backline, I do not sprint continuously.

I pause occasionally and listen.

The Tunnel sound can be faint enough that movement noise makes it easier to miss.

The full placement, range, connection, cooldown, and fast-travel rules belong in
the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/nva-tunnels-guide">
  {" "}NVA Tunnels guide
</Link>
.

As USA Commander, the conclusion I need is simpler:

**quiet terrain is not automatically safe terrain.**

<h2 id="commander-outpost">
  I use the Commander Outpost when I have to work in the backline myself
</h2>

The Commander Outpost was one of the tools I forgot about most often early on.

I would move a long distance to:

- inspect suspicious Tunnel pressure;
- prepare another Garrison;
- move through an exposed backline;
- or reach a position the Tactical Map could not solve by itself.

Then I would die.

Without my own useful spawn nearby, that one death could reset the entire trip.

Vietnam gives Commander a personal Outpost.

I use it most when I am:

**checking for Tunnels**

**setting up rear Garrisons**

**moving through the backline**

or:

**working far enough from HQ that another death would waste several minutes.**

I still do not turn Commander into an infantry role.

The Tactical Map, resource economy, command channel, and spawn network remain my
main jobs.

The OP only makes the times when I need to be physically present less punishing.

<h2 id="commander-mobility">
  I use a Transport Helo when it saves Commander time, not because I want to fly
</h2>

I am willing to ride a Transport Helicopter as Commander.

I am not willing to spend the whole match in one.

A useful ride gets me quickly to:

- a future Garrison position;
- a backline that suddenly needs checking;
- the opposite side of the map after the objective changes;
- or another location where my Commander OP can anchor the next job.

Then I get out.

The helicopter is transportation.

It is not my office.

If I am still in the air while the defensive spawn network disappears beneath
me, the mobility advantage has become a distraction.

<h2 id="manage-munitions">
  The 150-Munition USA Supply Drop changes when I call supplies myself
</h2>

The USA Supply Drop is one of the clearest examples of why I watch resources
instead of using Commander abilities on autopilot.

A USA Supply Drop costs **150 Munitions**.

Before calling one, I ask:

**Can the Supply Helo make the drop?**

**Can a Supply Truck solve this?**

**Does a Specialist already have supplies close enough?**

**Do I need these supplies immediately?**

The last question matters most.

I am happy to spend 150 Munitions when:

**the defensive spawn collapsed and I need a replacement quickly**

or:

**a Squad Leader is already in a strong position and waiting for supplies**

or:

**I am pre-positioning supplies for a route the team is very likely to need**

or:

**our Munitions income is healthy enough that the map value is worth the cost.**

What I do not do is scatter Commander Supply Drops around simply because the
ability is available.

Several careless 150-Munition decisions can remove options I need later.

<h2 id="convert-resources">
  I convert resources when one pool is piling up and another is becoming the bottleneck
</h2>

I do not use resource conversion on a fixed schedule.

I look at what the current team is consuming.

If **Fuel** keeps accumulating while the abilities I need are limited by
**Munitions**, I am willing to convert toward the resource the match is actually
using.

If armor and other Fuel demands suddenly rise, I stop converting Fuel away.

The same logic applies to any conversion:

**surplus resource → currently useful resource.**

I make the decision before the target resource reaches zero.

Waiting for an ability to become unavailable before reacting means I identified
the resource problem too late.

<h2 id="ability-timing">
  I use Commander abilities to change the next infantry decision
</h2>

The individual abilities do different things, but I use one rule across all of
them:

**What can the team do after this ability lands that it could not do before?**

If I cannot answer that, I usually wait.

<h3 id="use-recon">
  Recon answers a question before I commit squads or Munitions
</h3>

I do not use Recon just because the cooldown ended.

I want information that changes a decision.

For example:

**Is the next hard cap heavily occupied?**

**Which side of our defensive sector is actually receiving pressure?**

**Is there enough movement behind the point to justify pulling a squad back?**

Commander Recon can expose a snapshot of infantry and vehicle positions across a
broad section of the battlefield.

The information only becomes valuable after I turn it into something actionable:

- an attack marker;
- a defensive marker;
- a call to one Squad Leader;
- a squad rotation;
- or the setup for another Commander ability.

Information I never communicate is just extra detail on my Tactical Map.

<h3 id="reinforce">
  Reinforce buys time for a defense that still exists
</h3>

Reinforce is not something I save until the defensive point is already lost.

I want:

- friendly infantry still fighting around the point;
- a spawn route that can bring in more defenders;
- and enough time for those reinforcements to matter.

If:

**the Garrisons are already gone**

**the defenders are mostly dead**

and:

**the enemy is almost finished capturing**

then Reinforce is trying to rescue a problem I should have addressed earlier.

The ability buys time.

It does not rebuild a missing spawn network.

<h3 id="napalm">
  Napalm matters most when infantry is already ready to move
</h3>

Napalm is where it is easiest to confuse a good scoreboard with a good
Commander decision.

The initial strike can destroy exposed enemy infrastructure such as Garrisons and
Tunnels, and the burning area continues to make movement through the strike zone
dangerous.

In one Offensive Commander match, I finished with **96 Commander kills**, with
Napalm contributing heavily.

That is a memorable number.

But it is not the number I use to judge the ability.

A more useful result came when a Napalm strike removed both a Tunnel and a
Garrison while friendly infantry was in position to move onto the point.

That changed the map.

Before I commit Napalm, I ask:

**Are our infantry close enough to move?**

**Do we know which area or spawn route we are trying to clear?**

**Will the strike create a route into the point?**

If nobody can exploit the result, a large kill number may still have very little
effect on the objective.

<h3 id="artillery-strike">
  Artillery prepares an attack instead of replacing one
</h3>

Commander Artillery is another wide-area offensive tool, delivered through
multiple rounds over the target area.

I use it when a squad is preparing to move and I want to make that movement
easier.

That might mean:

**soften the hard cap → infantry crosses the final approach**

or:

**disrupt the enemy position → Squad Leaders move while the pressure is active.**

I do not fire it at a distant cluster just because I can see enemy activity
there.

If nobody is moving afterward, I have probably created damage without changing
possession of the ground.

The same Commander principle keeps coming back:

**damage is useful when it changes what my infantry can do next.**

<h2 id="when-defense-collapses">
  When the defense collapses, I stop financing the attack
</h2>

This is the Commander decision I try to make fastest.

If I open the Tactical Map and realize we have:

**no useful defensive Garrison**

or:

**one distant route back to a point that is already under pressure**

I stop planning the next aggressive spawn.

I rebuild the route home.

Depending on what is available, that can mean:

**send the Supply Helo backward**

**redirect a Supply Truck**

**ask a Specialist and Squad Leader to build**

**move personally using my Commander OP**

or:

**pull one communicative squad out of the attack long enough to stabilize the
point.**

I do not care which method looks most elegant.

I care that defenders can spawn where the defense is happening.

An attack that leaves the previous objective dependent on HQ is not free
momentum.

It has created a debt.

I pay that debt before putting more resources into the next push.

<h2 id="commander-priority-loop">
  The Commander loop I return to whenever the match becomes chaotic
</h2>

When several things happen at once, I stop trying to solve every radio call in
the order I hear it.

I return to the same priority loop.

### Do we have resource income?

If Nodes are missing, I find an Engineer.

### Can the defense survive one lost Garrison?

If not, spawn redundancy becomes the priority.

### Are the helicopters solving real jobs?

I want supplies, transport, reconnaissance, pickups, or repositioning.

If they are only circling, I give them a specific problem.

### Is pressure appearing somewhere that does not fit the visible frontline?

I check the backline for NVA Tunnel activity.

### Is one resource becoming the bottleneck?

I spend or convert before the emergency.

### Will this Commander ability change map control?

If Recon, Reinforce, Napalm, or Artillery does not give the infantry a better
next move, I usually keep the resources.

That is the loop that made USA Commander manageable for me.

I stopped trying to personally direct fifty players.

I focused on keeping the team supplied with the things individual infantry
cannot easily create for themselves:

**information, resource income, mobility, backup spawns, and a way to recover
when the first plan fails.**
