<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
My first matches got much easier once I stopped treating Hell Let Loose: Vietnam
like a normal run-and-gun shooter.

Before worrying about kills, I now do five things:

**1. Finish Getting Started.**

I want the Tactical Map, radio channels, capture points, and basic spawn
structures to make sense before the shooting starts.

**2. Join a normal infantry squad.**

I learn how the squad moves before taking responsibility for leading one.

**3. Check Unit Radio and Proximity Chat.**

On the default PC bindings shown in training, **C is Unit Radio**, **V is
Proximity Chat**, and **X is Leadership Radio** for roles that use it.

**4. Check the Tactical Map before every spawn.**

I find the objective, my squad, and a useful spawn before clicking Deploy.

**5. Redeploy when my position no longer has a job.**

I would rather reset onto a useful route than spend several minutes running
toward a battle that has already moved.

If I feel like I am running more than fighting, I check the map, my squad, and
the spawn situation before changing weapons or blaming my aim.

<h2 id="do-getting-started-first">
  I finish Getting Started before my first real match
</h2>

The fastest way I found to make Hell Let Loose: Vietnam less confusing was to
do **Getting Started** before trying to learn everything under fire.

The training covers the systems I immediately need:

- movement, crouching, prone, swimming, and healing;
- basic weapon handling;
- the command structure;
- radio channels;
- the Tactical Map;
- battlefield markers;
- basic spawn structures;
- capture points and sectors.

I do **not** finish every specialist Tutorial before playing normal infantry.

That would solve problems I have not encountered yet.

For my first match, I only want to answer four questions without guessing:

**Where is my squad?**

**Where should I spawn?**

**Which objective are we actually fighting over?**

**Which radio channel lets me talk to the people I need?**

Once those four answers feel normal, the deeper Tutorials make much more sense.

Squad Leader Training was a good example for me.

Its spawn mechanics felt abstract before I had played a real match.

After experiencing a push where our forward route disappeared and everybody
suddenly had a much longer trip back, I understood why those systems mattered.

<h2 id="fix-radio-keybinds">
  I check the radio keys before changing controls
</h2>

Communication is easy to break accidentally.

The default PC bindings shown during Getting Started are:

**C — Unit Radio**

**V — Proximity Chat**

**X — Leadership Radio**

Leadership Radio is for roles that participate in the leadership channel rather
than ordinary infantry.

I learned this the annoying way.

I moved one of my familiar control binds onto **C** and only later realized I
had overwritten my squad radio.

The training itself was what exposed the problem.

So if I can hear squadmates but cannot answer them, I check **Unit Radio**
before troubleshooting anything more complicated.

I also keep Proximity Chat available even when my squad is quiet.

It is useful for nearby teammates who are not in my unit.

I do not need constant conversation.

Short information is enough:

**enemy on my ping**

**tank right**

**OP is down**

**Medic here**

**moving to the point**

One useful call can save much more time than trying to independently understand
everything happening through the jungle.

<h2 id="pick-first-role">
  I learn the battle from a normal infantry role first
</h2>

My first role does not need to be the most powerful-looking option.

I want a role that lets me stay with the unit and learn:

- how quickly the front moves;
- what squad markers look like;
- how deaths change my route;
- when an objective becomes urgent;
- and how other players communicate.

That is why I do not force myself into **Squad Leader** or **Commander** during
my first match.

Those roles carry responsibilities that affect other players, and they also have
their own training prerequisites.

If one of those roles shows **Requirements Not Met**, I use the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/unlock-squad-leader-commander">
  {" "}Squad Leader and Commander unlock guide
</Link>
rather than turning the Beginner Guide into a leadership-unlock walkthrough.

For the first few infantry matches, I would rather understand where the squad
needs me.

<h2 id="medic-first-role">
  Medic taught me that being useful is not the same as chasing kills
</h2>

Medic was one of the roles that gave me a very obvious first job:

**stay near other players → keep them alive → learn where the fight is moving.**

That was much easier to understand than wandering through the jungle looking for
my own gunfight.

In one early match, my Support Score reached **920** within roughly the first
five minutes.

A little later I was sitting at:

**3 kills**

**3 deaths**

**11 revives**

The important part was not that those numbers made Medic objectively the best
beginner role.

They showed me how much value I could create without needing a high kill count.

That is the habit I carry into other roles.

I ask:

**What is this role helping the squad do?**

before:

**How many kills am I getting?**

During the first match, I care more about learning the battle than optimizing a
loadout I do not understand yet.

<h2 id="check-map-before-spawning">
  The spawn screen is my first tactical decision after every death
</h2>

A bad spawn can create several minutes of bad gameplay.

My early mistake was simple.

I would die,

see an icon that looked reasonably close to the objective,

click it,

and start running.

Only afterward would I discover that:

- my squad was fighting somewhere else;
- the objective had changed;
- our useful forward spawn was gone;
- the team was falling back;
- or another spawn was about to create a much better route.

Now I answer those questions **before** deploying.

My order is:

**1. Find the active objective.**

**2. Find my squad and Squad Leader.**

**3. Find the squad's useful forward spawn.**

**4. Compare it with the useful team spawns.**

**5. Deploy only after I know what route I am taking.**

I do not automatically choose whichever icon looks closest to the capture point.

The correct spawn is the one that lets me continue the squad's actual job.

If our forward OP disappears, I do not need to understand every spawn rule as a
new player.

I only need to recognize:

**the route I was using no longer exists.**

At that point I check whether another forward spawn is coming, whether the squad
has changed direction, or whether the battle has moved somewhere else.

The full difference between Outposts and Garrisons, supply requirements,
territory rules, placement restrictions, and rebuilding the network belongs in
the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/spawn-garrison-outpost-guide">
  {" "}Spawn, Garrison, and Outpost guide
</Link>
.

For a beginner, my rule is shorter:

**if the spawn situation is bad, running harder usually does not fix it.**

<h2 id="use-map-to-find-the-fight">
  I use the Tactical Map instead of following gunfire
</h2>

The jungle makes battlefield direction difficult to read.

I can hear shots,

see friendly icons through foliage,

and still have no idea whether I am approaching the important fight from a
useful direction.

The Tactical Map fixes that faster than staring through the trees.

My normal check is:

**objective → squad → friendly spawns → pings → route.**

I start with the objective.

Following random gunfire is one of the easiest ways to drift into a fight that
has nothing to do with what my squad needs.

Then I find my squad.

If the unit is spread out, I normally move toward the Squad Leader or the
largest useful group instead of inventing my own route.

Then I look at pings and markers.

An infantry marker,

tank marker,

enemy spawn marker,

or movement ping

gives me context before I push through cover.

The map does not just tell me where things are.

It helps me decide which information actually matters.

<h2 id="understand-capture-point">
  I fight for the objective before chasing another enemy
</h2>

Reaching the general battlefield is not enough.

Getting Started introduces the wider capture area and the **hard capture
point**, which gave me a much better way to judge whether I was contributing to
the fight.

The mistake I try to avoid is:

**see enemy → chase enemy.**

That enemy can lead me farther and farther away from the place that decides the
sector.

So when I see somebody leaving the objective area, I ask:

**Is this player stopping us from taking or holding the point?**

If yes, I deal with the threat.

If not, I check the map before following.

The same idea applies when I discover an enemy spawn near the objective.

Removing the route bringing several enemies back into the battle can be more
valuable than chasing one person away from it.

I do not need to know every Garrison rule yet.

I only need to start noticing where enemy waves are coming from.

<h2 id="redeploy-instead-of-running">
  I redeploy when my current position no longer has a job
</h2>

Redeploy felt wasteful to me at first.

I was still alive.

I had already crossed part of the map.

Giving up that position seemed like throwing progress away.

Then I spent far too much time walking from places that no longer mattered.

Now I redeploy when:

- my squad has moved to another objective and I am stranded behind it;
- a much better forward spawn has appeared;
- the area I am holding no longer protects anything important;
- my role needs to change for a specific squad problem;
- or the front has moved far enough that walking is clearly the slower option.

I do **not** redeploy simply because I have gone thirty seconds without seeing an
enemy.

Holding a flank,

protecting a route,

watching a spawn,

or waiting for an enemy push

can all be useful jobs.

The question I ask is:

**Why am I still standing here?**

If I have a good answer, I stay.

If I do not, I open the map.

<h2 id="dont-chase-advanced-systems">
  I leave the specialized systems for later
</h2>

Hell Let Loose: Vietnam puts a lot of interesting systems in front of me very
quickly.

Helicopters are tempting.

So are NVA Tunnels, Commander abilities, construction, mortars, reconnaissance,
vehicles, and deeper role progression.

Trying to learn everything during the first match made the game harder to
understand.

My order is:

**infantry movement → Tactical Map → communication → spawn awareness →
objective play → specialized systems.**

I do not jump into a helicopter just because one is available at HQ.

Flying adds pitch, roll, yaw, Collective, landing, logistics, and Helipad
behavior on top of everything I am already learning.

When I deliberately want to learn that system, I use the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/helicopter-controls">
  {" "}Helicopter Controls guide
</Link>
.

I treat NVA Tunnels the same way.

As ordinary infantry, I only need to understand that a working Tunnel network
can move players around the battlefield.

Placement, connections, relay planning, range, Fast Travel, and troubleshooting
belong in the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/nva-tunnels-guide">
  {" "}NVA Tunnels guide
</Link>
.

I learn those systems when I am ready to use them rather than letting them
distract me from the first-match loop.

<h2 id="first-match-routine">
  The six-step routine I use whenever I feel lost
</h2>

When the match starts feeling chaotic, I return to the same sequence.

### 1. Open the Tactical Map

I find the current objective before moving.

### 2. Find my squad

I stop drifting behind random teammates unless they are clearly moving toward
the same job.

### 3. Check the useful spawn

If our forward route disappeared, that often explains why the squad suddenly
feels scattered.

### 4. Listen before sprinting

Squad radio, Proximity Chat, gunfire, and pings tell me what I am about to walk
into.

### 5. Move toward a job

That might mean:

**attack the point**

**defend the point**

**revive teammates**

**cover a route**

**protect a spawn**

or:

**remove an enemy spawn.**

I do not need to move toward noise just because noise exists.

### 6. Redeploy when the map has moved on without me

I would rather deliberately lose one life than spend several minutes reaching a
battle that is already finished.

That was the biggest change between my first confused matches and the point
where Hell Let Loose: Vietnam started making sense.

I stopped asking:

**Where are the enemies?**

and started asking:

**Where does my squad need me next?**
