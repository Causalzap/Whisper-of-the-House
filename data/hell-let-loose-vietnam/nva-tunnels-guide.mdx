<GuideQuickAnswer title="Quick Answer">
I use NVA Tunnels as a **fast-travel network**, not as replacement Garrisons.

My basic route is:

**safe Garrison → Tunnel → relay Tunnel → useful flank or fallback route.**

The hard rules I plan around are:

- Tunnels are **free to place** and do not require supplies;
- they can be placed in **friendly, neutral, and unlocked enemy territory**;
- Tunnel placement has a **5-minute cooldown**;
- Tunnels need at least **100 meters of spacing**;
- connected structures can be up to **400 meters apart**;
- the team can have up to **12 Tunnels**, increased to **15** through a Commander Ability;
- a Tunnel network can contain up to **5 connections**;
- Tunnel → Tunnel and Tunnel → Garrison work;
- Garrison → Garrison does not.

I decide where the route needs to go **before** placing the next Tunnel. A safe
Tunnel in the wrong place can still waste the cooldown and leave the next useful
position outside connection range.
</GuideQuickAnswer>

<h2 id="tunnel-vs-garrison">
  A Tunnel is a travel node, not another Garrison
</h2>

This distinction cleared up most of my early confusion with NVA mobility.

A **Garrison is a spawn point**.

A **Tunnel is not a spawn point**.

I use Tunnels to move between connected parts of the network after I already
have somewhere useful to enter that network.

That changes how I position the two structures.

I am comfortable keeping a Garrison farther back where it can remain a
dependable team spawn, then extending mobility toward the current fight with
Tunnels.

A simple route might look like:

**safe Garrison → central Tunnel → forward Tunnel.**

If the front moves again, I can add another useful relay instead of immediately
risking the Garrison itself.

If my actual problem is Garrison supplies, territory, spacing, or rebuilding the
spawn network, I use the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/spawn-garrison-outpost-guide">
  {" "}Spawn, Garrison, and Outpost guide
</Link>
instead.

<h2 id="place-tunnel">
  I plan the next link before I place the Tunnel
</h2>

My worst Tunnel placements happened when I saw a good bush or a quiet corner and
thought:

**this looks safe, so I should put a Tunnel here.**

Safety matters, but that is only half the decision.

Before placing one, I open the Tactical Map and identify:

- the Garrison or Tunnel I want to connect from;
- the direction the squad actually needs to travel;
- whether the next position will still be within connection range;
- whether the location is covered without sitting directly on an obvious route;
- and whether the front is stable enough for the Tunnel to matter.

Only then do I place it.

Tunnels can be placed in:

**friendly territory**

**neutral territory**

and:

**unlocked enemy territory.**

I do not keep walking deeper into locked enemy territory hoping the Field Pad
will eventually accept the placement.

If the territory state blocks the route I wanted, I move the route rather than
trying random patches of ground.

<h2 id="tunnel-supplies">
  A Tunnel does not need supplies
</h2>

This is one of the most useful differences between extending a Tunnel route and
building another Garrison.

A Tunnel is **free to place**.

I do not need a Specialist supply box, Supply Helicopter, Supply Truck, or
Commander Supply Drop just to put one down.

That lets me separate two jobs in my head:

**Tunnel job → extend mobility.**

**Garrison job → create another team spawn and solve its supply requirement.**

If all I need is one relay so the squad can travel farther toward a flank, I do
not delay the Tunnel while waiting for a logistics chain it does not require.

The limitation is elsewhere:

**placement cooldown, spacing, range, and total Tunnel count.**

<h2 id="tunnel-cooldown">
  Every placement commits me to a 5-minute cooldown
</h2>

This is why I no longer place speculative Tunnels.

After placing one, there is a **5-minute cooldown** before another Tunnel can be
placed through that placement cycle.

Earlier in live play I saw a timer with roughly 200 seconds remaining, but that
was the **remaining cooldown at that moment**, not the full Tunnel cooldown.

The actual placement rule is five minutes.

That makes three questions important before I commit:

**What am I connecting this Tunnel to?**

**Where will the next useful link need to go?**

**Will this location still matter if we take or lose the objective?**

If I cannot answer those, I usually wait.

A Tunnel hidden perfectly in the wrong sector can be more damaging than a
slightly less hidden Tunnel that actually completes the route.

<h2 id="tunnel-limits">
  I separate Tunnel count, spacing, range, and connections
</h2>

There are several different limits, and mixing them up makes route planning much
harder.

**100 meters** is the minimum spacing between Tunnels.

**400 meters** is the maximum connection distance I plan around.

**12 Tunnels** is the normal team-wide Tunnel limit.

A Commander Ability can increase that to **15 Tunnels**.

And a Tunnel network supports up to **5 connections**.

Those numbers solve different problems.

The 100-meter rule stops me stacking Tunnel placements too tightly.

The 400-meter rule tells me when I need an intermediate relay.

The 12/15 limit tells the team how much infrastructure can exist.

The five-connection limit tells me not to draw every possible line between that
infrastructure.

<h2 id="connect-tunnels">
  I create the network from the Tactical Map
</h2>

Placing the Tunnel and connecting the Tunnel are separate actions.

I use the **Field Pad** to put the Tunnel into the world.

Then I open the **Tactical Map** to create the route.

The valid network connections are:

**Tunnel → Tunnel**

and:

**Tunnel → Garrison.**

I do not try to create:

**Garrison → Garrison.**

Only the **NVA Commander and Squad Leaders** manage these Tunnel connections.

Normal infantry can use the resulting route, but they cannot stand beside two
structures and create the missing connection themselves.

<h2 id="five-connections">
  Five connections means I do not link everything
</h2>

The maximum of **five network connections** changes how I use the Tactical Map.

I do not draw a line simply because two structures are available.

I ask:

**What new route does this create?**

For example, I would rather have:

**rear Garrison → central Tunnel → forward Tunnel**

plus a useful side branch than several connections that all move infantry toward
the same patch of jungle.

A connection earns its place when it:

- shortens a long exposed run;
- creates a genuinely different approach;
- reconnects a useful spawn to the front;
- gives the squad another fallback route;
- or keeps a new objective reachable.

If two connections solve essentially the same movement problem, I keep the
network simpler.

<h2 id="tunnel-distance">
  I add a relay when the next useful position is beyond 400 meters
</h2>

The distance rule is where aggressive Tunnel placement can fail.

The maximum connection distance is **400 meters**.

If my next useful flank is beyond that, I do not place the Tunnel directly at
the final destination and hope it somehow connects.

I divide the trip into another stage.

That gives me:

**existing node → relay Tunnel → forward Tunnel.**

That leads to one of my most useful Tunnel rules:

**I do not place the next Tunnel where I ultimately want to arrive. I place it
where it keeps the next useful destination reachable.**

A relay in a less exciting location is better than an aggressive Tunnel with no
working route behind it.

<h2 id="when-to-use-relay">
  I add another Tunnel when the front has moved beyond the current route
</h2>

This comes up constantly after a successful push.

The Tunnel network was built for one objective.

We take that ground.

Now the squad is fighting another sector farther forward.

At that point I decide between:

**keep running from the current exit**

or:

**invest another Tunnel in a relay.**

If the team is likely to fight around the new objective for more than one short
skirmish, I usually prefer the relay.

The route becomes:

**safe entry → Tunnel A → Tunnel B → new forward area.**

I do not make that investment if the situation behind me is already collapsing.

If our anchor Garrison is gone or the previous Tunnel is being cleared, another
forward Tunnel only extends a network the team can no longer reliably enter.

That is when I go backward first.

<h2 id="fast-travel">
  I use Tunnel travel instead of treating every move as another respawn
</h2>

Once the network is connected, I can enter the connected structure, view the
route, and choose where I want to travel.

Travel is not instant.

The game states that travel time varies with the **distance between the connected
structures**.

That still makes the destination a tactical decision.

Before travelling, I check whether:

- the objective is still active;
- the exit still gives us a useful approach;
- the enemy has started pressuring that branch;
- and another route would put me in a better position.

I do not automatically take the farthest connected Tunnel just because it exists.

<h2 id="enemy-near">
  If the Tunnel shows Enemy Near, I treat the exit as compromised
</h2>

A Tunnel can warn me that an enemy is nearby.

That warning changes my decision even before the structure is destroyed.

If another branch reaches roughly the same area, I use it.

If this is the only forward route, I tell the squad the exit is pressured and
approach the problem as a spawn-defense issue rather than blindly feeding more
players into it.

The important lesson is:

**a Tunnel existing on the map does not mean its destination is safe.**

<h2 id="destroyed-while-traveling">
  A destroyed Tunnel can interrupt me during travel
</h2>

One of the harsher lessons came after I had already entered the network.

The Tunnel was destroyed while I was in the travel sequence.

Instead of arriving safely, I ended up back in a redeploy state.

That makes forward Tunnel survival more important than squeezing out the final
few meters of placement.

I prefer positions that are:

- away from the most obvious road or trail;
- outside the center of the firefight;
- covered enough that passing infantry does not find them accidentally;
- but still close enough to save meaningful travel time.

The best Tunnel is not necessarily the closest Tunnel to the objective.

It is the Tunnel that remains alive long enough to keep the route useful.

<h2 id="dismantling-tunnels">
  I check the network before dismantling one of my own Tunnels
</h2>

Dismantling a Tunnel does more than remove the object from the ground.

It also breaks the connections using that structure.

That is why I do not automatically dismantle a badly positioned Tunnel the
moment I decide I want it somewhere else.

First I open the Tactical Map and ask:

**What route disappears when this Tunnel goes away?**

If it is an isolated dead end that no longer serves the battle, I clear it.

If it sits in the middle of a working chain, I try to establish the replacement
route first.

There is one useful cooldown interaction here as well:

dismantling the structure can remove its placement cooldown and free the space
for another structure.

That can make dismantling the correct choice when the Tunnel truly is wasted.

But I still check what I am disconnecting before I do it.

<h2 id="safe-anchor">
  I prefer a safe anchor and more aggressive Tunnel extensions
</h2>

The network became much more reliable when I stopped trying to put every
important structure as far forward as possible.

A setup I like is:

**safe Garrison**

→ **first Tunnel away from the obvious spawn approach**

→ **relay Tunnel toward the active side of the fight**

→ **another branch only when the battle actually needs it.**

This gives the team somewhere dependable to enter the system even if the forward
branch disappears.

It also avoids putting:

- the Garrison;
- every Tunnel;
- the squad;
- and the enemy's likely approach

on one narrow line.

One enemy sweep should not remove every useful route at once.

<h2 id="when-not-to-push-tunnel">
  I stop extending when the network behind me is already failing
</h2>

Tunnel building can become tempting because every new node looks like another
flank.

But there are moments when another forward placement makes the network worse.

I stop extending when:

- the useful Garrison behind us has gone down;
- the last relay already shows enemy pressure;
- the objective flipped and the route now points at the wrong fight;
- the next destination is beyond connection range and I have no sensible relay;
- or I would be spending the current Tunnel cooldown on a speculative position.

When that happens, I go backward.

I restore the part of the network the squad can actually use.

Then I extend again.

That has been much more reliable than continuously chasing the newest front edge
of the map.

<h2 id="enemy-tunnels">
  When I find an enemy Tunnel, I treat the structure as the higher-value target
</h2>

The same network logic changes how I fight against NVA Tunnels.

If I reach an enemy Tunnel behind the line, I do not automatically chase the
nearest infantryman first.

The player is one threat.

The Tunnel can keep moving more players into the same area.

When the position is safe enough, I dismantle the infrastructure.

In one deep push, removing an enemy Tunnel immediately took away the route the
enemy had been using in that area.

For achievement cleanup, dismantling an enemy Tunnel also matters for **Caved
In**, which I track in the
<Link href="/hell-let-loose-vietnam/achievements">
  {" "}Achievements guide
</Link>
.

The Tunnel mechanics themselves stay here.

<h2 id="tunnel-troubleshooting">
  What I check when an NVA Tunnel route is not working
</h2>

When the network fails, I diagnose the route instead of placing random new
Tunnels.

### The Tunnel exists but goes nowhere

I open the Tactical Map and check whether the structure actually has a useful
connection.

### I cannot connect the next position

I check the **400-meter connection limit**.

If the destination is too far away, I add a relay.

### I cannot place two Tunnels close together

I check the **100-meter minimum Tunnel spacing**.

That is a placement problem, not a connection problem.

### I am trying to connect two Garrisons

I stop.

The network uses:

**Tunnel → Tunnel**

or:

**Tunnel → Garrison.**

### The network is already complicated

I count what each line accomplishes.

There are only **five connections**, so duplicate routes are taking capacity
away from something more useful.

### The Tunnel shows Enemy Near

I treat the exit as compromised and check whether another branch can do the same
job.

### A Tunnel disappeared

I re-check every route that depended on that node before travelling.

### I cannot place the next Tunnel yet

I check the **5-minute placement cooldown**.

I do not interpret a remaining timer such as 200 seconds as the full cooldown.

### We already have a lot of Tunnels

I check the team-wide count.

The normal maximum is **12**, with a Commander Ability able to raise it to
**15**.

Once I started reading the system as a connected mobility network rather than a
collection of holes in the ground, the NVA side became much easier to manage.

A good Tunnel is not valuable because of where it sits.

It is valuable because of **where it lets the team go next**.
