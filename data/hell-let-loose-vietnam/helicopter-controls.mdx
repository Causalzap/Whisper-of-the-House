<h2 id="helicopter-controls">
  The four helicopter controls I learn before takeoff
</h2>

The first time I entered helicopter training, the hardest part was not getting
off the ground.

It was understanding what each input was doing to the aircraft.

I separate the controls into four jobs:

- **Pitch** — nose forward or backward;
- **Roll** — bank left or right;
- **Yaw** — rotate the helicopter;
- **Collective** — increase or decrease lift.

That sounds basic, but it stopped me from trying to correct every mistake with
the same input.

If I am drifting too far forward, I work the pitch.

If my helicopter is leaning too far to one side, I correct the roll.

If the aircraft is facing the wrong direction, I use yaw.

If I simply need more or less altitude, I change collective.

During my PC training run, **Space increased collective** and **Left Ctrl
decreased it**. I also confirmed **S** as the backward-pitch input in that
setup.

I still check the current Helicopter Controls menu before flying because the
bindings are unusual enough that I immediately wanted to remap some of them.

In particular, I tried to move forward pitch onto a mouse-style input and found
that the control setup did not behave the way I expected.

That is a problem I would rather discover while parked than with a squad sitting
behind me.

<h2 id="takeoff">
  I go straight up first, then start building forward movement
</h2>

My safest first takeoff is deliberately boring.

I start the engine, wait until the helicopter is ready, then increase collective
until I have enough clearance from the ground.

  <Image

I do not immediately pitch hard forward while I am barely above the Helipad.

That is how a small correction becomes a tree, roof, or ground strike.

My order is:

**collective up → establish altitude → gentle forward pitch → correct roll →
turn with yaw.**

Once I have space around the aircraft, I can combine those controls much more
aggressively.

The jungle is the reason I prefer this order.

There are trees, poles, uneven ground, buildings, riverbanks, and landing areas
where the obstacle that matters is often beside me rather than directly in
front.

Getting ten more meters of clearance before accelerating has saved me much more
time than trying to make the takeoff look fast.

<h2 id="pitch-roll-yaw">
  I use small corrections instead of fighting the helicopter
</h2>

The helicopter started feeling controllable when I stopped holding every input
until the aircraft visibly reacted.

There is momentum.

If I pitch forward hard and wait until I am obviously moving too quickly, the
correction has already become larger than it needed to be.

The same applies to roll.

I make a small bank, let the aircraft start moving laterally, then begin
bringing it back toward level before I reach the exact position I want.

Yaw is most useful when I want to change where the nose is facing without
turning the maneuver into a huge sweeping bank.

The mistake I avoid is stacking large pitch, roll, yaw, and collective changes
all at the same time.

That makes it hard to tell which control caused the problem.

While learning, I would rather fly a slightly wider route and make one clean
correction at a time.

<h2 id="collective">
  Collective controls altitude, but I do not use it as an emergency brake
</h2>

The control that mattered most for my landings was collective.

On my default PC setup:

**Space = increase collective**

**Left Ctrl = decrease collective**

Increasing collective gives me more lift.

Reducing it lets the helicopter descend.

The important part is that lowering collective does not automatically remove my
forward momentum.

If I am still moving quickly toward the landing zone and simply hold the
descent input, I am not really landing.

I am flying downward.

That difference is why my early approaches felt much harder than they needed to
be.

<h2 id="how-to-land">
  My landing sequence is speed first, altitude second
</h2>

The training finally made landing click for me with one sequence:

**reduce forward movement → establish a hover → slowly lower collective.**

    src="/images/hell-let-loose-vietnam/hell-let-loose-vietnam-helicopter-landing-hover-collective.webp"

    width={1600}

    className="rounded-xl"
  />
  <figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-slate-400">
    I do not start the final descent while I am still carrying a lot of forward
    speed. I use pitch to settle over the landing area, then lower collective.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

When I approach an LZ, I start slowing earlier than feels necessary.

I use backward pitch to bleed the forward movement.

As the helicopter settles over the area, I keep correcting roll so the aircraft
does not drift sideways into trees or structures.

Only when the horizontal movement is under control do I slowly reduce
collective.

That is the order I use because it gives me separate problems to solve:

**Am I over the landing area?**

Then:

**Am I descending safely?**

Trying to solve both at the last second usually ends with a much harder landing.

<h2 id="landing-too-fast">
  If I overshoot the LZ, I go around instead of forcing it down
</h2>

My landing weakness was carrying too much movement into the final approach.

Once I realize I am going to overshoot, I no longer try to save every landing.

If I still have safe altitude, I increase collective, regain a stable flight
path, circle, and make another approach.

That costs a few seconds.

Forcing a bad approach can cost the helicopter.

I especially go around when:

- I am still moving quickly over the LZ;
- my roll correction is getting larger instead of smaller;
- trees are filling the side of the cockpit;
- the ground is uneven;
- or incoming fire has made the original landing point unsafe.

The important judgment is whether I am still making controlled corrections.

If I am just reacting faster and faster to new problems, I abandon the approach.

<h2 id="landing-near-trees">
  I give trees more room than I think they need
</h2>

Vietnam's foliage makes low helicopter work deceptive.

A clearing can look large enough from altitude and become very tight once I am
actually descending.

I therefore look at the whole rotor area, not just where the cockpit is going.

Branches beside the aircraft matter just as much as the ground underneath it.

On one training approach I had to move away from branches before settling into
the landing.

That is the point where I would rather drift back toward the middle of the
clearing and spend another few seconds stabilizing than keep descending beside
the tree line.

<h2 id="hot-landing-zone">
  Under fire, I decide between a quick drop and leaving the area
</h2>

Not every troop insertion needs a perfect full landing.

If the area is exposed but still usable, there are situations where I only need
to get low and stable enough for the passengers to get out quickly.

I do not sit hovering there afterward.

That is one of the biggest mistakes I avoid in live matches.

A helicopter that stays in the same visible position for too long gives enemy
players time to react, aim, move anti-air weapons, or simply concentrate fire on
the aircraft.

So once the job is done:

**drop troops → gain clearance → move.**

If the LZ is already obviously too hot on approach, I do not force the landing
just because somebody marked that exact location.

I call it out and use a safer position nearby.

Flying everybody directly into the enemy saves no time.

<h2 id="supply-helicopter">
  I treat the Supply Helicopter like a flying logistics route
</h2>

The Supply Helicopter solves a different problem from the Transport Helicopter.

I use it to move supplies to places where the ground route would be slow,
dangerous, or awkward.

The actual supply drop is handled from the **co-pilot seat**.

This is where I stop thinking only like a pilot.

I look at the Tactical Map and ask:

**Where will the team need supplies after the next push or defense?**

Dropping them directly on the loudest firefight is not always the best answer.

A supply drop can be much more useful slightly behind the current line, where a
Squad Leader can actually reach it and create a safe spawn.

For flying, the important part is that I choose a drop path that lets me keep
moving afterward.

I do not want to hover directly over the supply location waiting to see what the
ground team does with it.

My job is to deliver it and get the aircraft ready for the next task.

<h2 id="supply-vs-transport">
  I check whether I am in a Supply or Transport Helicopter before leaving HQ
</h2>

This sounds too basic to be a real problem until the match gets busy.

The two aircraft are not doing the same job.

I have watched infantry run toward a Supply Helicopter expecting a ride only to
discover that it was not the transport they thought it was.

So before I take off, I confirm what aircraft I actually have.

If the team needs troops moved across the map, I want the **Transport
Helicopter**.

If the ground team needs logistics for a new position, I want the **Supply
Helicopter**.

I do not discover the difference after everybody has already run across HQ to
meet me.

<h2 id="helipad-resupply">
  If the Helipad is not repairing or resupplying me, I turn the engine off
</h2>

This was my most concrete helicopter mistake.

I returned to HQ, landed on the Helipad, and waited.

Nothing seemed to happen.

I stayed there for roughly **two minutes** wondering why the system was not
working.

The problem was the engine.

The rule I use now is simple:

**land completely → settle on the Helipad → engine off → wait for repair and
resupply.**

The Helipad can replenish ammunition and supplies and repair damage, but I do
not get those benefits while I leave the engine running.

If I have landed correctly and nothing is happening, this is the first thing I
check.

<h2 id="transport-helicopter">
  Transport Helicopters are for movement first, guns second
</h2>

The Transport Helicopter carries more utility than I expected.

Training gives it:

- troop transport;
- **two machine guns**;
- **recon flares**.

The guns are useful, but I do not treat the aircraft as an attack helicopter.

My first job is still getting players where the team needs them.

My second is information.

The door guns become useful when they support those jobs rather than turning the
entire flight into circles around an enemy objective.

<h2 id="recon-flare">
  I use the co-pilot flare before flying blind into a suspicious area
</h2>

The recon flare is one of the most useful tools on the Transport Helicopter.

The co-pilot can fire it over the ground and then check the Tactical Map to see
the detection radius and revealed enemy positions.

I like using a flare when:

- an LZ looks suspiciously quiet;
- a defensive area is taking pressure from an unclear direction;
- infantry suspects enemies moving through jungle cover;
- or I am already flying over an area where the ground team needs information.

The next step is communication.

If the flare reveals useful movement, I call it out.

The aircraft sees parts of the battle that individual infantry squads often
cannot.

Keeping that information in the helicopter wastes one of its biggest
advantages.

<h2 id="hq-helicopter-spawn">
  A stationary Transport Helicopter at HQ can also be a spawn option
</h2>

One useful detail from training is easy to miss:

**stationary helicopters sitting on HQ Helipads can be used as spawn points.**

That gives me another way to organize a transport pickup.

Instead of spawning somewhere else in HQ and asking a squad to run across the
base, players can use the available helicopter spawn when the aircraft is
stationary on the Helipad.

I still make sure the group is actually ready before taking off.

Leaving with one passenger while five more are still trying to spawn wastes the
main advantage of the transport.

<h2 id="pilot-communication">
  The calls I make are as important as the flight
</h2>

A good flight does not help much if nobody knows what I am doing.

When I am flying, I try to communicate four things:

**Where I am dropping supplies.**

A Squad Leader needs to know the box exists before it becomes useful.

**Whether the landing zone is safe.**

If I see fire or movement that makes the marked LZ unusable, I say so before
everybody commits to it.

**What the flare revealed.**

The ground squads can react to information I picked up from above.

**Whether I can take passengers.**

If I am returning near a squad that needs to reposition, I let them know instead
of flying past with an empty transport.

The biggest waste I have seen is a helicopter crew and infantry team effectively
playing separate matches.

The helicopter should shorten the time between a ground problem and the team's
response to it.

<h2 id="dont-hover-too-long">
  I leave after the job is finished
</h2>

The worst habit I try to avoid is hanging over one location because the flight
currently feels safe.

Safe can change very quickly.

If I have already:

- dropped the supplies;
- dropped the squad;
- fired the useful flare;
- or made the necessary callout,

I move.

A helicopter hovering in roughly the same position gives the enemy repeated
opportunities to react.

That is especially unnecessary when I am not carrying anyone and have no
specific task left at that location.

I would rather be halfway back toward the next pickup than still circling the
last one looking for a few door-gun kills.

<h2 id="helicopter-not-working">
  What I check when the helicopter is not doing what I expect
</h2>

When something feels wrong, I check the simple causes before assuming the
aircraft is bugged.

### I cannot gain altitude

I check collective.

On my PC controls, **Space raises collective** and **Left Ctrl lowers it**.

### I keep overshooting landings

I am probably starting the descent before removing enough forward movement.

I pitch back, establish the hover first, and only then lower collective.

### The Helipad is not resupplying me

I make sure I am fully back on the Helipad and **turn the engine off**.

### The helicopter keeps drifting into trees during landing

I stop the descent and correct the horizontal position before trying to touch
down.

### Infantry cannot use my helicopter as the transport they expected

I check whether I actually brought a **Supply Helicopter** instead of a
Transport Helicopter.

### I am alive but not helping the team

I check my current job.

If I have no passengers, no supply assignment, no useful flare target, and no
pickup request, I reposition instead of hovering over the same area.

The part of helicopter flying that took me longest to learn was not keeping the
aircraft in the sky.

It was knowing **when the flight had already accomplished its job and it was time
to leave**.
